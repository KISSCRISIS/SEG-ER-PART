1. Clinical overview and pathophysiology
Environmental Disorders — high‑yield revision notes (deduplicated HIPPO items)

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Heat illnesses
  - Continuum: heat cramps → heat exhaustion (volume/electrolyte depletion, T often elevated but <≈40–40.5°C) → heat stroke (core hyperthermia with CNS dysfunction, often >40–40.5°C). Exertional heat stroke often in young athletes; non‑exertional in elderly.
  - Pathophysiology: failure of heat dissipation → direct thermal injury + inflammatory cascade → multiorgan dysfunction, rhabdomyolysis. Rapid cooling reduces morbidity/mortality. (Rosen)
- Hypothermia and frostbite
  - Hypothermia produces bradyarrhythmias, Osborn (J) waves on ECG; severity correlates with core temp.
  - Frostbite: ice crystal formation → endothelial injury, microthrombosis, reperfusion injury. Superficial (1st/2nd) → clear blisters; deep (3rd/4th) → hemorrhagic blisters, tissue loss. Thrombolysis (tPA) may be considered for severe deep frostbite in selected patients.
- Drowning / submersion
  - Hypoxic/anoxic injury → lactic acidosis is common. Prognosis worsened by prolonged submersion (>~5 min), delayed CPR, age <3 y, warm water drowning, GCS 3, prolonged resuscitation. (Szpilman / NEJM)
- Envenomations & bites
  - Snake (Crotalids): venom = cytotoxins + hemotoxins → local swelling, tissue destruction, coagulopathy, thrombocytopenia, systemic toxicity. Pulmonary/airway compromise or progressive coagulopathy → antivenom indicated.
  - Spiders: Latrodectus (black widow) → presynaptic latrotoxin causing massive neurotransmitter release → diffuse muscle cramps, autonomic features. Loxosceles (brown recluse) → sphingomyelinase → local necrosis sometimes systemic hemolysis.
  - Scorpion: severity graded I–IV (local → regional → cranial nerve/skeletal muscle dysfunction → severe cardiopulmonary). Most US stings are mild; severe require antivenom/critical care.
  - Reptile (e.g., iguana) bites: often colonized with Salmonella spp.; risk of wound infection.
  - Mammalian (dog/cat) bites: Pasteurella multocida common; polymicrobial including Staph/Strep/Capnocytophaga.
- Marine hazards
  - Jellyfish: venom delivered by nematocysts. Treatment focuses on preventing further nematocyst firing (no fresh water/rubbing), tentacle removal, and hot‑water immersion or species‑specific measures (vinegar for some species).
  - Vibrio vulnificus: gram‑negative marine organism → fulminant cellulitis/sepsis in hours; risk factors: chronic liver disease, hemochromatosis, immunocompromise.
- Altitude & decompression
  - Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS): headache, nausea at >2000 m; acetazolamide speeds acclimatization (causes mild metabolic acidosis → hyperventilation).
  - HACE (high‑altitude cerebral edema): ataxia, AMS, altered mental status — definitive therapy = descent; adjuncts: O2, dexamethasone, hyperbaric bag if descent impossible.
  - HAPE: high‑altitude pulmonary edema due to hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction → pulmonary hypertension → non‑cardiogenic edema; therapy: descent, O2, nifedipine or PDE‑5 inhibitors if needed.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Thermoregulatory extremes
  - Hypothermia: core temperature <35°C. Progressive physiologic depression → bradycardia, dysrhythmias (VF/VT more likely at low temps), coagulopathy, insulin resistance; myocardium irritable (guidewire/defib can provoke arrhythmia). Rewarming reverses. (Ref: NBK545239)
  - Frostbite: freezing injury → intracellular/extracellular ice crystals → endothelial injury, thrombosis, ischemia/necrosis. Damage evolves over days. Rewarming required; avoid dry heat. (Ref: NBK536914)
  - Chilblains (pernio): superficial inflammatory response to nonfreezing cold; burning/pruritic erythematous plaques; usually self-limited. (Ref: NBK549842)
- Hyperthermia / heat-related illness
  - Heat cramps: exercise-associated muscle cramps from electrolyte losses (Na+, K+, Mg2+, Cl–) — treat with oral/IV rehydration and stretching. (Ref: NBK553117)
  - Exertional heat stroke: core temp >40°C with CNS dysfunction; multisystem injury (rhabdomyolysis, hepatic injury, DIC). Rapid cooling is lifesaving. (Ref: NBK553117)
  - Classic (nonexertional) heat stroke: elderly/chronically ill with impaired heat dissipation.
- Diving and barotrauma
  - Arterial gas embolism (AGE): gas bubbles entering systemic circulation during ascent → sudden focal neuro/cardiac deficits, often within minutes of surfacing. Hyperbaric oxygen definitive. (Ref: NBK482321)
  - Decompression sickness: delayed multisystem symptoms from inert gas bubbles.
  - Oxygen toxicity: at depth, high PO2 → CNS toxicity (seizures, twitching). Occurs while at depth. (Ref: NBK430743)
- Radiation and thyroid protection
  - Radioactive iodine uptake by thyroid → increased long-term thyroid cancer risk; potassium iodide (KI) saturates thyroid to block uptake in relevant exposures. (Ref: NBK542320)
- Animal and envenomation syndromes (high-yield patterns)
  - Elapidae (sea snakes, coral snakes, cobras): potent neurotoxins → early tetany then flaccid paralysis and respiratory failure (sea snakes may cause rhabdomyolysis). Sea-snake antivenom (Elapidae) where available. Coral-snake bites may have delayed neurotoxicity → observe/ICU. (Refs: NBK532973; Rosen’s)
  - Crotalinae (rattlesnakes, pit vipers): hemotoxic — local tissue necrosis, coagulopathy; Crotalidae antivenom for systemic/ progressive envenomation.
  - Scorpion (Centruroides): neurotoxin → autonomic storm (tachy, HTN), cranial nerve dysfunction, opsoclonus/hemiballismus; Centruroides antivenom for severe neurotoxicity. (Ref: NBK430928)
  - Stingray: venom is heat-labile → hot water immersion + wound care; leave wound open and irrigate. (Ref: Rosen’s)
  - Brown recluse (Loxosceles): cytotoxic venom → painless initial bite with possible necrotic ulcer later; supportive care, tetanus update; avoid early debridement. (Ref: NBK537045)
  - Honeybee/Africanized bees: toxicity from large numbers of stings (swarming), not inherently more toxic venom per sting; treat anaphylaxis/systemic toxicity supportively. (Ref: Rosen’s)
- Electrical injury

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Heat illness spectrum: heat cramps → heat exhaustion → heat stroke. Heat stroke = life‑threatening failure of thermoregulation with core hyperthermia and central nervous system dysfunction. Two types:
  - Exertional (young, sweaty, hyperdynamic): profuse sweating, rhabdomyolysis, coagulopathy.
  - Classic/classical (elderly, comorbid, impaired cardiovascular response/meds): less tachycardia, higher mortality risk.
- Cooling physiology: evaporative and conductive heat loss are the fastest; peripheral vasoconstriction (e.g., from early vasopressors) impairs cooling.
- Hypothermia: degree ranges from mild to severe; severe cases may have bradycardia, hypotension, arrhythmias. Rewarming strategies range from passive to active internal (lavage, ECMO). ECMO provides fastest core rewarming and oxygenation (can raise core temperature rapidly) (NCBI; Rosen’s).
- Immersion/drowning: primary problem is hypoxemia — ventilation is priority. Submersion time is strongest predictor of outcome (shorter is better).
- Barotrauma and decompression/diving injuries:
  - Arterial gas embolism (AGE): ascent → gas bubble entry/systemic emboli → neuro/cardiac ischemia → treat with hyperbaric oxygen.
  - Pulmonary over‑pressurization (POPS): alveolar rupture → pneumomediastinum/pneumothorax (radiographic continuous diaphragm sign).
- Cold‑injury types:
  - Frostbite staging similar to burns: 1st (superficial), 2nd (clear blisters), 3rd (hemorrhagic blisters/full thickness → hypodermis), 4th (deep tissue involvement → muscle/bone).
  - Trench/immersion foot: prolonged exposure to wet, nonfreezing cold → ischemia; prevention = keep feet dry.
- Envenomations and marine exposures:
  - Stingray: venom heat‑labile → hot water immersion 40–45°C for analgesia.
  - Black widow: neurotoxin → severe abdominal wall muscle spasm can mimic peritonitis.
  - Brown recluse: local loxoscelism → necrotic ulceration; dapsone not supported; surgical review if ulceration.
- Toxic chemical: hydrofluoric acid (HF) penetrates tissue, binds calcium → hypocalcemia, hypomagnesemia, hyperkalemia; treat with calcium (topical gel, IV calcium gluconate).
- Wound infections from marine/animal exposures:
  - Vibrio vulnificus (salt/brackish water, oysters/wound exposure): fulminant wound infection and sepsis; high mortality; risk amplified by liver disease, hemochromatosis, diabetes.
  - Mycobacterium marinum (aquarium/fish tanks): chronic skin lesions (“fish tank granuloma”).
  - Animal bites: Pasteurella multocida common in cat bites; human bites polymicrobial (Eikenella, Strep, Staph).

(References: Rosen’s Emergency Medicine; NCBI Bookshelf: NBK537135, NBK553117, NBK537045, NBK536914, NBK482364, NBK441829, NBK482321, NBK482348, NBK441920.)

1. Clinical overview and pathophysiology
Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Acute pancreatitis: premature activation of pancreatic enzymes → autodigestion; may be interstitial (sterile) or necrotizing. Necrosis can become secondarily infected days later. Prophylactic antibiotics are not recommended; treat infection only when proven/clinically evident (fever, sepsis, gas in collections, positive cultures, or CT‑guided aspiration). (Dellinger et al., Ann Surg 2007; UpToDate/NIH reviews)
- Biliary obstruction/cholangitis: biliary stasis + infection → Charcot’s triad (fever, RUQ pain, jaundice); Reynolds pentad (add hypotension + altered mental status) signals severe disease—biliary decompression (usually ERCP) + antibiotics required.
- Mesenteric ischemia: embolic (AF) or thrombotic occlusion → sudden severe pain often “out of proportion” to exam; lactate may be elevated; can progress to bowel necrosis—diagnose with CTA; urgent revascularization and surgery if necrotic bowel.
- Small vs large bowel obstruction: SBO commonly from adhesions; radiograph/CT: SBO shows dilated small bowel loops with valvulae conniventes across lumen; LBO often from carcinoma, shows haustra that do not cross entire lumen, large-caliber colon dilation.
- Volvulus: sigmoid presents with “coffee‑bean” sign; initial nonoperative reduction via sigmoidoscopic decompression unless peritonitis or perforation.
- Upper GI bleeding: risk stratify with Glasgow‑Blatchford Score (GBS); GBS = 0 low risk → outpatient possible; melena, tachycardia, elevated BUN are higher‑risk features. Witnessed melena correlates strongly with significant UGIB (Srygley et al., JAMA 2012).
- Esophageal/gastric pathology: Mallory‑Weiss tears follow retching/vomiting and often need urgent endoscopy if higher risk; gastric adenocarcinoma risk factors include H. pylori infection.
- Perianal disease: fissure = midline linear posterior (painful defecation, bright blood on paper); perianal abscess = fluctuant, tender collection → require incision & drainage (antibiotics not routine unless systemic/immune compromise).
- Bezoars: trichobezoar (hair) resists chemical dissolution; attempt endoscopic removal first; surgical if endoscopy fails.
- Neonatal jaundice: physiologic unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia peaks in first days of life (usually benign unless very high or Coombs‑positive, hemolysis, conjugated bilirubin elevation).
- Hepatic/autoimmune markers: Primary biliary cholangitis → anti‑mitochondrial antibody (AMA) positive; alcoholic hepatitis commonly shows AST>ALT (often ~2:1) and elevated GGT.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Infections
  - Clostridioides (Clostridium) difficile: antibiotic-associated colitis from dysbiosis; toxin-mediated pseudomembranous colitis; spores resistant to many disinfectants. First-line therapy for initial non-severe disease: oral fidaxomicin or oral vancomycin (IDSA 2021). IV vancomycin does not treat luminal C. difficile (not secreted into gut).   - Entamoeba histolytica: trophozoites can invade bowel and seed liver → amebic liver abscess (travel-associated).

- Obstructive / ischemic processes

  - Ascending cholangitis: biliary obstruction (stones) + infection → Charcot triad (RUQ pain, fever, jaundice); Reynolds pentad adds hypotension + confusion (severe/septic).
  - Hernia incarceration → strangulation: vascular compromise → ischemia, elevated lactate, peritonitis; urgent surgical management.
- Perforation / barotrauma
  - Boerhaave syndrome: full-thickness distal esophageal rupture after forceful vomiting → pneumomediastinum (Hamman’s crunch), rapid deterioration; needs emergent surgical management.
- Pediatric-specific emergencies
  - Button battery in esophagus: alkali current causes liquefactive necrosis and rapid perforation—medical emergency requiring immediate removal.
  - Hypertrophic pyloric stenosis: projectile nonbilious vomiting, palpable “olive” mass, hypochloremic hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis; male predominance; surgery definitive (pyloromyotomy).
  - Meckel diverticulum: true diverticulum from vitelline duct; “rule of 2s”; often asymptomatic; when bleeding, painless lower GI bleed in young child; definitive treatment surgical resection.
- Functional / postoperative
  - Postoperative ileus: bowel dysfunction with gas throughout colon and rectum on plain film; management supportive (NPO/IV fluids, electrolyte correction, early mobilization).

Abdominal & Gastrointestinal Disorders — High‑Yield Emergency Revision Notes

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Structural vs motility vs inflammatory vs infectious vs ischemic causes produce overlapping presentations; use pattern recognition (onset, progression, systemic signs, stool characteristics, prior surgery, immunosuppression).
- Mechanical obstruction: small‑bowel obstruction (SBO) most commonly from postoperative adhesions (developed countries); hernia, neoplasm, volvulus less common. Obstruction → distension, vomiting, pain, absent flatus; risk of strangulation/ischemia.
- Dysphagia: oropharyngeal (transfer/neurologic) vs esophageal (mechanical vs motor). Progressive solids→liquids = mechanical (stricture, malignancy); intermittent solids = motility. Prior radiation/caustic injuries → complex, proximal, often fibrotic strictures.
- Upper GI alarm features requiring urgent endoscopy: new dysphagia/odynophagia, weight loss, GI bleeding, refractory GERD with red flags, age‑related new dyspepsia (guideline thresholds such as age >50 commonly used for referral).
- Infectious diarrheas: secretory vs inflammatory vs malabsorptive (steatorrhea). Foul, greasy, voluminous stools → consider Giardia (camping, water exposure); stool antigen or nucleic acid tests preferred over routine culture or serology for acute diagnosis.
- Liver infections: pyogenic liver abscesses are usually polymicrobial (enteric facultative + anaerobes), often associated with biliary obstruction; Klebsiella important in primary liver abscess in Asia; S. aureus more after instrumentation.
- Pancreatitis: most necrotizing pancreatitis is initially sterile; infected necrosis presents later—surgical debridement reserved for infected necrosis failing antibiotics or hemodynamic instability.
- Cirrhosis complications: ascites → risk of spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP); diagnostic ascitic PMN count ≥250 cells/mm3 indicates SBP and triggers empiric antibiotics.
- Hepatic encephalopathy: ammonia from gut bacteria; lactulose lowers colonic pH and traps NH4+ and increases transit—first‑line therapy.
- Post‑bariatric/Roux‑en‑Y: long‑term malabsorption and micronutrient deficiencies (notably B12) — require lifetime supplementation.
- Pediatric specifics: pyloric stenosis (infant projectile nonbilious vomiting, palpable olive) — ultrasound first; foreign body ingestion → chest/neck x‑rays initially (AP + lateral) for radioopaque items.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Acute pancreatitis: autodigestion of pancreas by activated pancreatic enzymes. Common causes: gallstones (obstruction of biliary/pancreatic ducts) and alcohol. Classic metabolic association: hypocalcemia (calcium saponification with fat necrosis). Complications: pancreatic pseudocyst (late, walled-off fluid collection), pancreatic necrosis, infection, chronic pancreatitis → insulin deficiency/diabetes, pancreatic calcifications. Diagnosis = 2 of 3: characteristic epigastric pain (often radiates to back), serum lipase/amylase ≥3× upper limit OR imaging consistent with pancreatitis. (Ref: NCBI Bookshelf, Rosen’s)
- Biliary disease spectrum:
  - Biliary colic: transient cystic duct obstruction.
  - Acute cholecystitis: persistent cystic duct obstruction → GB wall inflammation, Murphy sign, US shows stones, GB wall thickening, pericholecystic fluid.
  - Choledocholithiasis → obstruction of common bile duct; can cause cholangitis.
  - Ascending cholangitis: Charcot triad = fever + RUQ pain + jaundice; Reynolds pentad adds hypotension/shock and altered mental status (severe).
  - Management distinctions: cholecystectomy definitive for cholecystitis; ERCP for obstructing CBD stone in cholangitis or retained choledocholithiasis. (Ref: NCBI Bookshelf)
- Small-bowel obstruction (SBO): most commonly from adhesions (prior surgery). Presents with crampy abdominal pain, vomiting, distention, high-pitched bowel sounds early.
- Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP): infection of ascitic fluid in cirrhosis; common organisms = enteric gram-negatives (E. coli, Klebsiella). Diagnostic threshold: PMNs in ascitic fluid >250 cells/µL triggers empiric therapy. (Ref: NCBI Bookshelf)
- Infectious diarrheas and extraintestinal complications:
  - Giardia lamblia: foul-smelling, greasy, steatorrhea; stool O&P (multiple samples) or antigen testing.
  - Shigella dysenteriae: bloody/mucoid diarrhea, high fever; can cause seizures/encephalopathy in children.
  - Salmonella Typhi (typhoid): fever, abdominal symptoms, rose spots, relative bradycardia (pulse–temperature dissociation / “Faget sign”); treat empirically with fluoroquinolones where susceptible.
  - Entamoeba histolytica: amebic liver abscess after diarrheal illness → treat with metronidazole.
- Special syndromes:
  - Dumping syndrome after gastric bypass (Roux-en-Y): postprandial cramping, diarrhea, vasomotor sx (flushing, dizziness), syncope; managed with dietary measures.
  - Cystic fibrosis: exocrine pancreatic insufficiency → steatorrhea, recurrent pancreatitis and pulmonary infections; diagnose with sweat chloride test.
  - Necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) in preterm neonates: ischemic necrosis, pneumatosis intestinalis on AXR, can progress to perforation/sepsis.
  - Rectal foreign body: glass/sharp objects require surgical consultation; risk of perforation.
  - Zollinger–Ellison (gastrinoma): refractory PUD, elevated gastrin; consider MEN1 associations (parathyroid, pituitary adenoma, pancreatic NET). (Refs: Rosen’s, Tintinalli, NCBI)

Abdominal and Gastrointestinal Disorders — compact high‑yield emergency revision notes
(Integrated, deduplicated synthesis of common HIPPO QBank concepts)

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Acute abdomen etiologies to prioritize: perforated viscus, mesenteric ischemia, complicated biliary disease (acute cholecystitis, emphysematous cholecystitis, choledocholithiasis, ascending cholangitis), acute appendicitis, bowel obstruction (volvulus, incarcerated/strangulated hernia), ischemic colitis, pancreatitis (acute vs chronic), intussusception (pediatrics), acute radiation proctitis, GI infection ± complications (HUS), hepatobiliary disease (viral hepatitis, hepatic encephalopathy).
- Biliary specifics:
  - Emphysematous cholecystitis = gas‑forming infection of gallbladder wall (Clostridia and gram‑negatives); strong association with diabetes → high risk of perforation and mortality. (See NCBI Bookshelf)
  - Choledocholithiasis → CBD obstruction; ultrasound “double‑barrel sign” (CBD ≈ portal vein) and CBD dilation suggest obstruction. CBD considered enlarged by usual sonographic criteria (commonly >6 mm; note age and post‑cholecystectomy modifiers in radiology references).
  - Porcelain gallbladder = calcified gallbladder wall; associated with increased gallbladder malignancy risk → consider cholecystectomy.
- Vascular:
  - Acute mesenteric ischemia: pain out of proportion to exam, rapidly rising lactate; often arterial embolus/thrombosis in patients with vascular disease. Vasoconstrictors may worsen splanchnic perfusion.
- Pancreas:
  - Chronic pancreatitis may present with calcifications on XR, chronic pain, and normal/low lipase; management is analgesia/supportive, not acute pancreatitis protocols.
- Pediatric:
  - Intussusception commonly follows viral illness (Peyer patch hypertrophy) — classical intermittent colicky pain with vomiting; treat with pneumatic/contrast enema unless signs of perforation/peritonitis.
- Infection/toxic:
  - HUS (STEC): triad microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, acute renal failure after bloody diarrheal illness; antibiotics may worsen outcome in STEC‑HUS — supportive care is primary.
- Liver:
  - Hepatic encephalopathy associated with elevated ammonia; TIPS increases risk of encephalopathy post‑procedure. Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP) commonly precipitates hepatorenal syndrome.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Inflammatory bowel disease
  - Crohn’s disease: transmural, granulomatous inflammation; “skip lesions”; can affect any part mouth→anus; predisposes to fistulae, strictures, malabsorption. (Rosen’s/Tintinalli, NCBI Bookshelf)
  - Ulcerative colitis: mucosal inflammation limited to colon/rectum, continuous, always involves rectum; risk of toxic megacolon, colorectal cancer.
- Perforation syndromes
  - Perforated viscus (e.g., peptic ulcer): full-thickness GI wall defect → free intraperitoneal air → peritonitis.
  - Boerhaave’s syndrome: spontaneous transmural esophageal rupture after violent retching → mediastinitis, pneumomediastinum, pleural effusion (often left-sided).
- Hepatobiliary disease
  - Acute cholecystitis: usually gallstone obstructing cystic duct → inflammation ± secondary bacterial infection (E. coli common; also Klebsiella, Enterococcus, Enterobacter).
  - Ascending cholangitis: biliary obstruction + infection → Reynolds pentad: RUQ pain, fever, jaundice ± hypotension, altered mental status → emergency decompression (ERCP).
  - Gallstone pancreatitis: gallstone obstructing ampulla → elevated lipase; CBD dilation increases suspicion of retained/obstructing stone.
- Liver failure and complications
  - Cirrhosis → portal hypertension → ascites; bacterial translocation → spontaneous bacterial peritonitis (SBP).

Abdominal and Gastrointestinal Disorders — compact high‑yield ED revision notes

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Broad categories: surgical emergencies (perforation, obstruction, ischemia, volvulus), hepatobiliary/pancreatic disease, infectious enteritis, inflammatory colitis, esophageal foreign bodies/impactions, neutropenic enterocolitis.
- Important mechanisms:
  - Acute mesenteric ischemia (AMI): arterial embolus (cardiac emboli, e.g., AF), arterial thrombosis on atherosclerotic plaque, venous thrombosis, non‑occlusive hypoperfusion. Ischemia → mucosal necrosis → transmural infarction/perforation if untreated.

1. Clinical overview and pathophysiology
Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Primary survey and priorities: Airway (with C-spine protection) > Breathing > Circulation > Disability (neuro) > Exposure. Always assume c-spine injury until cleared.
- Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS): eye (4), verbal (5), motor (6). Motor score is the single best predictor of outcome. GCS ≤ 8 generally indicates need for airway protection/intubation.
- Intracranial mass effect/herniation: expanding epidural hematoma (brief LOC → lucid interval → rapid deterioration) and uncal (temporal) herniation produce ipsilateral CN III palsy (dilated, “down-and-out” pupil) and contralateral hemiparesis; Cushing’s reflex = hypertension + bradycardia + irregular respirations → suggests markedly elevated ICP.
- Epidural vs subdural vs subarachnoid vs diffuse axonal CT patterns:
  - Epidural: lentiform (biconvex), extra-axial, does not cross suture lines; classically arterial (middle meningeal) → rapid decline.
  - Subdural: crescent-shaped, can cross sutures, usually venous → slower.
  - Subarachnoid: hyperdensity outlining sulci/ventricles.
  - Diffuse axonal: petechial hemorrhages at gray-white junction, corpus callosum, brainstem.
- Secondary brain injury: raised ICP, hypoxia, hypotension — prevent these to improve outcome.
- Blast injuries: primary (pressure wave — TM rupture, pulmonary contusion, bTBI), secondary (projectiles), tertiary (body displacement, blunt trauma), quaternary (burns, inhalation).
- Compartment syndrome: increased interstitial pressure after crush/fracture → ischemia; early sign = severe pain out of proportion + pain on passive stretch; late signs = paresthesia, pallor, pulselessness, paralysis.
- Penetrating vs blunt abdominal trauma triage: unstable + positive eFAST → OR. Stable + positive eFAST → CT and trauma surgery consult.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Trauma triage/approach: primary survey (Airway with C‑spine control → Breathing → Circulation → Disability → Exposure). Life threats must be recognized and treated immediately. (Rosen’s; ATLS principles)

1) Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Mechanisms matter: axial load → burst (C1 Jefferson) or compression injuries; hyperflexion → bilateral facet dislocation, flexion teardrop (unstable); hyperextension → Hangman’s (C2 pars) fractures; axial + rotation → meniscal tears; FOOSH (fall on outstretched hand) → scaphoid, lunate/perilunate injuries, radial head fracture.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Spinal cord syndromes (mechanism = focal cord compression/hemisection/ischemia)
  - Brown–Séquard (hemisection, classically from penetrating trauma): ipsilateral UMN weakness and loss of vibration/proprioception below lesion; contralateral loss of pain and temperature beginning a few levels below the lesion (spinothalamic decussation).  
  - Anterior cord syndrome: anterior 2/3 of cord → motor and pain/temperature loss with preserved dorsal column (vibration/proprioception).

- Cardiac/pericardial injury in trauma: any pericardial fluid after trauma is abnormal and may represent hemopericardium → tamponade physiology; penetrating thoracic trauma is high risk.
- Commotio cordis: blunt precordial blow at a vulnerable phase of ventricular repolarization → primary ventricular fibrillation in otherwise structurally normal heart (typically young athletes).
- Pediatric distal-radius injuries: torus (buckle) fracture = compression of cortex, stable, requires immobilization only; greenstick and physeal injuries are less stable and need ortho follow-up.
- Mandibular fractures: many (angle, ramus, symphysis) communicate with oral cavity → treated as “open” and receive prophylactic antibiotics. Condylar fractures typically do not require prophylactic antibiotics.

1. Clinical overview and pathophysiology
Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Acute coronary syndromes (ACS): plaque rupture → thrombosis → STEMI/NSTEMI. Reperfusion priority: PCI if timely; fibrinolysis when PCI delayed/unavailable [ACC/AHA STEMI guidelines].
- Arrhythmias:
  - SVT/AF/AFlutter: re-entrant or focal automaticity; hemodynamic stability guides therapy (rate control vs rhythm control vs cardioversion).
  - WPW: accessory pathway → pre-excitation (short PR, delta wave); AV‑nodal blockers can paradoxically accelerate ventricular rate during atrial fibrillation/flutter by promoting conduction via accessory pathway.
  - AV blocks: Mobitz I = progressive PR prolongation then drop; Mobitz II = fixed PR with sudden dropped beats; Mobitz II → high risk progression to complete heart block.
  - Ventricular arrhythmias: torsades de pointes is polymorphic VT associated with prolonged QT (drug-induced, electrolyte disturbance) and can degenerate to VF.
- Structural/cardiac mechanical:
  - Aortic stenosis: fixed outflow obstruction, preload dependent; severe AS → syncope/exertional angina/heart failure — urgent evaluation and valve replacement if symptomatic.
  - Hypertrophic obstructive cardiomyopathy (HOCM): asymmetric septal hypertrophy and systolic anterior motion → outflow obstruction; syncope in young athletes; echo first-line.
  - Myocarditis: viral etiologies (parvovirus B19, enteroviruses, HHV-6); can mimic ACS — troponin may be elevated, CXR cardiomegaly.
  - Pericarditis: pleuritic chest pain, friction rub, diffuse ST elevation with PR depression; colchicine + NSAID/aspirin therapy.
- Vascular emergencies:
  - DVT/PE in pregnancy: physiologic hypercoagulability; LMWH is preferred anticoagulant in pregnancy.
  - Phlegmasia cerulea dolens: massive iliofemoral DVT causing venous occlusion, cyanosis, absent pulses — limb‑threatening → urgent thrombectomy/thrombolysis.
  - Carotid artery dissection: headache, neck pain, ipsilateral Horner syndrome, transient monocular blindness; antithrombotic therapy to prevent stroke.
  - Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA): rupture → hypotension, abdominal/back pain; bedside ultrasound rapid diagnostic in unstable patient; immediate vascular/OR mobilization.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Basic lesion types: macule, papule, pustule, vesicle (<5–10 mm), bulla (>5–10 mm), erosion (epidermis loss), ulcer (dermis/in deeper loss), crust, scale.

  - Type I: urticaria, angioedema (IgE mediated)

CHAPTER 07: TOXICOLOGIC DISORDERS
============================================================================================

1. Clinical overview and pathophysiology
Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Toxicokinetics basics: absorption (rapid for alcohols), distribution (lipid vs water soluble), metabolism to active/toxic metabolites (eg, ethylene glycol → glycolate/oxalate; methanol → formate), elimination (renal, hepatic, dialyzable). Antidotes either (a) bind toxin (idarucizumab → dabigatran), (b) block formation of toxic metabolites (fomepizole → alcohol dehydrogenase inhibitor), (c) replace deficient/coagulation factors (prothrombin concentrate), or (d) reverse receptor effects (naloxone → opioids).
- Dialyzability: many small, low‑protein drugs/poisons are dialyzable. Commonly dialyzable agents (important ED list): salicylates, toxic alcohols (methanol, ethylene glycol), lithium, metformin, isoniazid (INH), theophylline, some beta‑blockers (atenolol, metoprolol), topiramate, acyclovir, phenobarbital, dabigatran (partial). Hemodialysis removes parent compound and some metabolites; used when toxic metabolites or severe clinical deterioration occur.
- Acid–base patterns help identify toxins:
  - Mixed respiratory alkalosis + metabolic acidosis → salicylates.
  - High osmolar gap + anion gap metabolic acidosis → ethylene glycol or methanol (depending on clinical clues).
  - Elevated osmolar gap without anion gap or acidosis → isopropyl alcohol (ketonemia/ketonuria present).
- Toxidromes: cluster signs to narrow differential (opioid, anticholinergic, cholinergic, sympathomimetic, serotonin syndrome, NMS).

Key references: Pollack et al., NEJM 2015 (idarucizumab); 2020 ACC guidance on OAC bleeding; Tintinalli (Rosen’s) chapters; NCBI toxicology reviews.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Toxidrome-first approach: identify pattern (anticholinergic, cholinergic, sympathomimetic, opioid, serotonergic, sedative-hypnotic, dopaminergic, etc.) to guide immediate therapy while diagnostics are pending.
- Mechanisms emphasized in these scenarios:
  - Enzyme inhibition: organophosphates/carbamates inhibit acetylcholinesterase → excess ACh at muscarinic + nicotinic synapses (SLUDGE, bronchorrhea, seizures). Pralidoxime can reactivate AChE if given before “aging.”
  - Ion-channel / receptor blockade: TCAs block fast Na+ channels → wide QRS, arrhythmia; local anesthetics cause Na+ channel blockade and can progress to cardiac collapse (LAST).
  - Mitochondrial inhibition: cyanide blocks cytochrome oxidase → anaerobic metabolism, profound lactic acidosis, cherry-red skin.
  - Oxidation of hemoglobin: oxidized Fe3+ (metHb) cannot carry O2 → pulse ox ~85% that does not correct with supplemental O2; co-oximetry required.
  - Enzyme cofactor depletion: isoniazid → functional pyridoxine (B6) deficiency → refractory seizures.
  - Receptor antagonism vs overstimulation: cocaine/meth/amphetamines cause sympathetic hyperactivity; SSRIs/MAOI interactions → serotonin syndrome (hyperreflexia, clonus) vs neuroleptic malignant syndrome (lead-pipe rigidity, hyporeflexia).
  - Disruption of glucose/insulin: non-dihydropyridine and dihydropyridine CCBs impair insulin release → hyperglycemia in overdose (useful discriminator vs β‑blocker which can cause hypoglycemia in children).
  - Heavy metals/toxic ingestion: lead → GI, neurologic effects and basophilic stippling; chelation thresholds differ by age.

- Toxidromes are patterns of signs/symptoms produced by classes of poisons (anticholinergic, cholinergic, sympathomimetic, opioid, sedative–hypnotic, serotonergic, neuroleptic, etc.). Recognize pattern first, confirm agent second.
- Key mechanisms to recall:
  - Enzyme blockade (cyanide, hydrogen sulfide → cytochrome oxidase inhibition → cellular hypoxia).
  - Receptor antagonism/agonism (opioids → μ‑receptor; anticholinergics → muscarinic blockade; neuroleptics → dopamine D2 blockade).
  - Ion channel blockade (TCA, local anesthetics, some antiarrhythmics → Na+ channel block → QRS widening, arrhythmia).
  - Metabolic conversion to toxic metabolites (ethylene glycol → glyoxylic/oxalic acids; methanol → formic acid; salicylates → uncoupling oxidative phosphorylation → mixed acid–base disturbance).
  - Protein binding and volume of distribution dictate efficacy of dialysis and lipophilic toxin sequestration (lipid emulsion).
- Principles of antidotal therapy: (1) antagonize target receptor or enzyme, (2) chelate/remove toxin, (3) support physiology (airway/circulation/temperature, treat complications), (4) prevent further absorption/decontaminate.
- Sources: Rosen’s Emergency Medicine; Tintinalli’s Emergency Medicine; review chapters cited in QBank explanations.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Procedure risk vs physiologic reserve: choose techniques/drugs that minimize hemodynamic compromise in unstable/septic patients (etomidate, ketamine preferred for hemodynamic stability; avoid agents that cause vasodilation/bradycardia in shock) (NBK535364).
- Local anesthetics: two chemical classes — esters (procaine, benzocaine, chloroprocaine, tetracaine) vs amides (lidocaine, bupivacaine, ropivacaine, mepivacaine). Ester allergy → avoid other esters; amide names usually contain two "i"s (memory aid, not absolute) (Becker & Reed 2012).
- POCUS/FAST principles: FAST detects free intraperitoneal or pericardial fluid (dependent spaces); less sensitive for retroperitoneal injuries. E-FAST adds lung windows to detect pneumothorax/pleural fluid (Roberts & Hedges; Cochrane review).

1. Clinical overview and pathophysiology
Endocrine, metabolic & nutritional disorders — high‑yield emergency revision notes

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Diabetes mellitus
  - Type 1: autoimmune β‑cell destruction → absolute insulin deficiency → prone to ketogenesis → DKA.
  - Type 2: insulin resistance ± relative insulin deficiency → hyperglycemia, hyperosmolar states.
  - Diagnostic thresholds commonly used in ED/clinic: random glucose ≥200 mg/dL with symptoms; fasting glucose ≥126 mg/dL; HbA1c ≥6.5%; 2‑hr OGTT ≥200 mg/dL. (Use clinical context.)
- Diabetic emergencies
  - DKA: insulin deficiency → lipolysis → free fatty acids → ketogenesis → high anion‑gap metabolic acidosis, ketonuria/ketonemia; often younger/type‑1; dehydration variable.
  - HHS: severe hyperglycemia and hyperosmolarity without significant ketosis; often older/type‑2; profound dehydration (typical deficits many liters) and neurologic dysfunction.
- Hypoglycemia
  - Neurogenic (sympathetic symptoms) then neuroglycopenic signs. Sulfonylurea‑induced hypoglycemia may recur; octreotide blocks insulin secretion for sulfonylurea ingestion.
- Electrolyte and acid‑base
  - Hyperkalemia: decreased renal excretion/icellular shifts → ECG changes (peaked T → PR prolongation → loss of P → wide QRS → sine wave) → risk VT/VF.
  - Hypocalcemia: total calcium may be normal but ionized Ca falls with alkalosis (respiratory alkalosis increases protein binding → ↓ionized Ca → tetany, Chvostek/ Trousseau signs).
  - High‑altitude physiology: chronic hypoxia → sustained hyperventilation → primary respiratory alkalosis; renal compensation lowers HCO3− (rule of thumb used clinically: chronic respiratory alkalosis → HCO3 falls ~5 mmol/L per 10 mmHg PaCO2 drop).
- Endocrine emergencies
  - Thyroid storm: life‑threatening thyrotoxicosis with fever, delirium, tachyarrhythmia; needs rapid multimodal therapy.
  - Pheochromocytoma crises: episodic catecholamine surges → paroxysmal hypertension, headaches, diaphoresis, tachycardia; diagnosed by plasma/urine metanephrines.
  - Adrenal insufficiency (Addison): primary adrenal cortex failure — most commonly autoimmune destruction in developed world → glucocorticoid/mineralocorticoid deficiency.
- Hypothalamic‑pituitary lesions
  - Prolactinoma: amenorrhea, galactorrhea, infertility; large lesions → bitemporal hemianopsia. Prolactin >200 ng/mL strongly suggests macroprolactinoma; MRI pituitary with contrast is imaging of choice.
  - Acromegaly: GH‑secreting pituitary adenoma; gradual coarse facial/hand changes and visual field defects; MRI pituitary.
- Nutritional & toxic metabolic states
  - Wernicke encephalopathy: thiamine (B1) deficiency → triad of encephalopathy, ophthalmoplegia (nystagmus/VI palsy), ataxia; treat empirically with IV thiamine before any glucose.
  - Urea cycle disorders/newborn hyperammonemia: present in first days after feeding with vomiting, lethargy, hyperventilation and elevated ammonia; urgent metabolic and supportive care.
- Diabetes insipidus
  - Central DI: ADH deficiency → polyuria (very dilute urine osmolality <300 mOsm/kg) and hypernatremia; treatment = desmopressin (DDAVP).

1) Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Glucose: hypoglycaemia in children — treat promptly; dextrose concentration choice driven by vein size and osmolarity. Pediatric dextrose bolus target = 0.5 g/kg IV. "Rule of 50s": concentration (%) × volume (mL/kg) ≈ 50. Practical regimens: neonates/infants D10 5 mL/kg (0.5 g/kg); toddlers/children D25 2 mL/kg; adolescents D50 1 mL/kg. (Rosen)
- Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA): volume depletion + insulin deficiency → hyperglycaemia, ketosis, metabolic acidosis. First treat volume; correct potassium before insulin if low. Insulin infusion standard: 0.1 U/kg/hr (no routine bolus); hold insulin if K+ < 3.3 mmol/L. Corrected Na for hyperglycaemia: add 1.6 mEq/L for every 100 mg/dL glucose >100 mg/dL. Cerebral oedema is a rare, life‑threatening complication in children (more common in young/newly diagnosed). (ADA, Rosen)
- Potassium disorders: hyperkalaemia causes progressive ECG changes — peaked T waves → P‑wave flattening/PR prolongation → wide QRS → sine wave/VT/VF. Immediate membrane stabilisation (IV calcium) if ECG changes or QRS widening. Temporizing intracellular shift therapies: insulin + dextrose, nebulized beta2 agonist (albuterol). Removal: diuretics, cation exchangers, dialysis. Hypokalaemia causes U waves, flattened T waves, arrhythmia risk; symptoms often when K+ < ~3.0 mmol/L. (Rosen, Pfennig/Slovis)
- Calcium disorders: hypercalcaemia (malignancy, primary hyperparathyroidism, vitamin D excess) → polyuria, neuropsychiatric symptoms, constipation, nephrolithiasis. Initial ED therapy = isotonic saline hydration; follow with calcitonin and bisphosphonates; dialysis in severe renal/heart failure. Hypocalcaemia post‑neck surgery (parathyroid/thyroidectomy) causes perioral/finger paresthesias, carpopedal spasm, tetany; treat symptomatic hypocalcaemia with IV calcium (calcium gluconate). (Rosen)
- Adrenal insufficiency: primary (Addison) — low cortisol and aldosterone → hypotension, hyperpigmentation, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia. Secondary (exogenous steroid withdrawal or pituitary disease) — low ACTH → low cortisol but usually preserved aldosterone → less hyperkalaemia; hyponatraemia possible; hyperpigmentation absent. Random cortisol low suggests insufficiency; cosyntropin (ACTH) testing used later. (NCBI reviews, Rosen)
- Thyroid storm/ severe thyrotoxicosis: life‑threatening hypermetabolic state. Sequence of ED therapy: beta‑blockade (propranolol or esmolol if unstable) → antithyroid drug (PTU preferred acutely because it reduces peripheral T4→T3 conversion and blocks synthesis; methimazole used for long term; PTU higher hepatotoxicity) → iodine (only after antithyroid drug given) → glucocorticoids (reduce T4→T3 conversion, treat possible adrenal insufficiency). Avoid NSAIDs for fever (they displace T4 from binding globulin); acetaminophen preferred.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Wernicke encephalopathy: thiamine (B1) deficiency in malnourished/heavy drinkers → classic triad ophthalmoplegia/nystagmus, ataxia, confusion. Hypomagnesemia impairs response to thiamine. (Rosen’s EM)

  - Thyrotoxicosis / thyroid storm: excess circulating T3/T4; high‑output cardiac state that can progress to systolic (low‑output) heart failure if untreated.
  - Pregnancy: propylthiouracil (PTU) preferred in first trimester (and in thyroid storm) because it inhibits peripheral T4→T3 conversion; iodine given only after antithyroid drug started.
- Adrenal disorders:
  - Adrenal crisis (primary or secondary after steroid withdrawal) → hypotension, hyponatraemia, hyperkalaemia, hypoglycaemia; urgent IV glucocorticoids required.
  - Etomidate can cause transient adrenocortical suppression (important when considering repeated/continuous use).
  - Pituitary apoplexy → acute loss of ACTH → secondary adrenal insufficiency; presents with severe headache, visual/CN deficits, nausea/vomiting, hypotension.
- Catecholamine‑secreting tumours (pheochromocytoma): episodic headaches, diaphoresis, tachycardia, hypertension; biochemical diagnosis with plasma/urine metanephrines; pre‑op alpha blockade required.
- Calcium–parathyroid axis:
  - Symptomatic hypercalcaemia: causes include primary hyperparathyroidism (↑PTH) and malignancy (↓/low PTH). Symptoms: “stones, bones, groans, thrones, psychic overtones.” ECG can show shortened QT.
  - Hypoparathyroidism most commonly follows thyroidectomy (causes hypocalcaemia, positive Chvostek sign).
- Electrolyte disturbances:
  - Hypomagnesaemia (common with alcoholism) → arrhythmias (prolonged PR, flattened T waves, widened QRS, predisposition to torsades). Mg required for some thiamine responses.
  - Hypokalaemic periodic paralysis (familial or thyrotoxic): intracellular K shift → profound weakness; treat carefully with K repletion (oral if no respiratory/ECG compromise).
- Diabetes emergencies:
  - DKA: insulin deficiency → hyperglycaemia, ketosis, metabolic anion gap acidosis. First priority = isotonic fluid resuscitation; check serum potassium BEFORE insulin (insulin drives K intracellularly). Bicarbonate rarely indicated.
  - Metformin‑associated lactic acidosis (MALA): severe lactic acidosis, elevated lactate; no specific antidote — supportive care; consider dialysis.
- Lithium complications: nephrogenic diabetes insipidus (polyuria/polydipsia) — diagnosis by water deprivation + vasopressin challenge (no urine osmolality rise in nephrogenic DI); lithium can also increase serum calcium/PTH.

Core concepts & pathophysiology
- Electrolyte shifts are often life‑threatening because of brain/cardiac excitability (Na, K, Ca, Mg) and require immediate recognition of symptom severity (seizures, arrhythmia, coma).  
- Hyponatremia: cerebral edema risk when acute/rapid fall → seizures/coma. Chronic hyponatremia: brain adapts; risk of osmotic demyelination (central pontine myelinolysis, ODS) if corrected too rapidly.

- Metabolic hyperglycemic crises: DKA = insulin deficiency + ketogenesis + anion gap metabolic acidosis; HHS = extreme hyperglycemia + hyperosmolarity with minimal ketones; both need aggressive fluids ± insulin and electrolyte management.  
- Nutritional deficiencies: B1 (thiamine) → Wernicke/Korsakoff; B3 (niacin) → pellagra (dermatitis, diarrhea, dementia); B12 → megaloblastic anemia + posterior column dysfunction.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Hyponatremia: brain adapts to chronic low Na by losing osmolytes → risk of osmotic demyelination (central pontine myelinolysis) if corrected too rapidly. Safe correction commonly cited: limit 8–12 mEq/L per 24 h (use lower limit, e.g., ≤8 mEq/L/day, for high‑risk or chronic cases). Acute symptomatic hyponatremia (seizure, coma) requires prompt controlled rise using hypertonic saline. (Rosen’s EM)
- Diabetes insipidus (DI): failure to concentrate urine due to ADH axis problems.
  - Central DI = deficient ADH secretion (posterior pituitary).
  - Nephrogenic DI = renal resistance to ADH (lithium, hypercalcemia, genetic).
  - Both → polyuria, polydipsia, hypotonic urine (low urine osmolality), possible hypernatremia if free water cannot be replaced.
- Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), classically 21‑hydroxylase deficiency: impaired cortisol (± aldosterone) synthesis → if salt‑wasting form: hyponatremia, hyperkalemia, hypotension/dehydration, metabolic acidosis; females show virilization (ambiguous genitalia), males may present later with salt‑wasting shock. Hyperkalemia causes peaked T waves/arrhythmia risk. (NCBI review)

Recognition and differential (ED red flags & discriminators)
- Hyponatremia severity:
  - Red flags: seizures, obtundation, respiratory depression, acute severe headache, persistent vomiting — treat as severe/symptomatic.
  - Chronic/insidious: nausea, headache, gait disturbance, mild confusion.
- Distinguish causes using volume status, urine Na/osmolality:
  - Hypovolemic hyponatremia: low ECF (vomiting, diarrhoea, diuretics) → urine Na low if extrarenal losses, high if renal losses.
  - Euvolemic hyponatremia: SIADH, hypothyroid, glucocorticoid deficiency; urine osmolality >100 mOsm/kg, urine Na >30–40.
  - Hypervolemic hyponatremia: CHF, cirrhosis, nephrosis.
- Polyuria/polydipsia differential:
  - Diabetes insipidus (urine osmolality low, serum osmolality high/normal).
  - Primary polydipsia (psychogenic): very dilute urine but concentrates with water deprivation.
  - Osmotic diuresis (hyperglycaemia): high serum glucose, glycosuria.
- Neonatal shock with electrolyte abnormalities:
  - CAH (21‑hydroxylase) — salt wasting, hyperkalemia, hyponatremia; infants (especially males) may present with lethargy, vomiting, hypotension, arrhythmia.

1. Clinical overview and pathophysiology
Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Upper airway: larynx level obstruction produces inspiratory stridor; trachea/bronchi produce expiratory stridor/wheeze. Important pediatric causes: croup (viral laryngotracheobronchitis, subglottic narrowing), epiglottitis (supraglottic infection → rapid obstruction), bacterial tracheitis (post-viral bacterial infection of the subglottic/tracheal mucosa). (Rosen’s; NCBI Bookshelf)
- Lower airway: bronchiolitis (infants, usually RSV → small-airway inflammation, mucus, air‑trapping), acute asthma (bronchospasm, reversible airflow obstruction, risk of status asthmaticus "silent chest"), COPD/emphysema (alveolar destruction, hyperinflation, blebs → risk of spontaneous pneumothorax).
- Pleuropulmonary vascular: pulmonary embolism (thromboembolus → V/Q mismatch, RV strain; severity: massive = hemodynamic instability → reperfusion therapy; submassive = RV strain without shock), ARDS (noncardiogenic pulmonary edema, diffuse infiltrates, ↓compliance; hypoxemia with reduced PaO2/FiO2).
- Airway devices/iatrogenic: recent tracheostomy can erode into innominate artery → sentinel bleed → tracheo‑innominate fistula (life‑threatening).
References: NCBI Bookshelf chapters on croup, bronchiolitis, PE, ARDS; Rosen’s Emergency Medicine.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Pneumothorax: air in pleural space → lung collapse; tension physiology causes mediastinal shift and cardiovascular compromise. Primary spontaneous in tall thin smokers/young; secondary on background lung disease (eg COPD). (See NCBI Bookshelf: pleural disease.)
- Pulmonary embolism (PE): thromboembolus → acute RV afterload increase → RV strain, hypoxemia, possible hemodynamic collapse. Spectrum: small-subsegmental → submassive (RV strain without hypotension) → massive (hemodynamic instability/shock).
- Diffuse alveolar hemorrhage (DAH): capillary/alveolar bleeding from vasculitis, autoimmune disease, drugs → acute respiratory failure and bilateral alveolar infiltrates; hemoptysis variable.
- Fat embolism syndrome: long‑bone fracture/orthopedic surgery → fat droplets → pulmonary capillary obstruction + inflammatory response; classic triad = respiratory distress, neurologic signs, petechial rash.

- Aspiration pneumonitis vs aspiration pneumonia vs lung abscess: chemical pneumonitis occurs immediately after gastric content aspiration (often early, may not require antibiotics); bacterial aspiration pneumonia arises later (>48 h) with anaerobic/upper‑airway flora and can progress to lung abscess (cavitation, air‑fluid level).

- Septic pulmonary emboli: right‑sided endocarditis/IVDU → multiple cavitary bilateral lesions; systemic sepsis and murmurs often present.
- Occupational pneumoconioses (eg silicosis, coal worker’s): chronic inhalational exposure → progressive interstitial fibrosis; CXR shows innumerable small rounded opacities; clubbing and progressive dyspnea.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Typical vs atypical pneumonia
  - Typical (eg Streptococcus pneumoniae): abrupt onset, high fever, productive cough, lobar consolidation on CXR; common cause of post‑influenza secondary bacterial pneumonia.

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Degenerative (mechanical)
  - Osteoarthritis (OA): cartilage wear → joint-space narrowing, osteophytes, crepitus; pain worse with activity, morning stiffness <30–60 min. First-line analgesic: acetaminophen (NSAIDs risk if GI bleed). (Rosen’s EM Ch.102)

  - Adhesive capsulitis (“frozen shoulder”): prolonged immobilization → capsular adhesions → loss of both active and passive ROM.
- Inflammatory / autoimmune
  - Rheumatoid arthritis / Juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA): symmetric small-joint synovitis; JIA common oligoarticular form in young children; ANA often positive, RF often negative in oligoarticular JIA.
  - Spondyloarthropathies (ankylosing spondylitis): inflammatory back pain improving with exercise, sacroiliitis, “bamboo spine”; common extra‑articular associations include inflammatory bowel disease.
- Crystal arthropathies
  - Gout: monosodium urate (MSU) crystals = needle-shaped, negatively birefringent under polarized light; classic first MTP (podagra); precipitated by diuretics (e.g., HCTZ), alcohol.
  - Pseudogout (CPPD): calcium pyrophosphate = rhomboid-shaped, positively birefringent; often knee or wrist; x-ray may show chondrocalcinosis.
- Infectious bone & joint disease
  - Septic arthritis: hematogenous or direct spread → purulent joint; urgent diagnosis to prevent joint destruction.
  - Osteomyelitis (hematogenous or contiguous): diabetics, IVDU, prosthetic devices; MRI most sensitive for early disease.
  - Necrotizing soft-tissue infection (including Fournier gangrene): rapidly progressive, severe pain out of proportion, crepitus, skin necrosis; often polymicrobial (type I) in diabetics; strep/staph/clostridial or polymicrobial mixes.
  - Disseminated gonococcal infection (DGI): triad of tenosynovitis, dermatitis (pustular lesions), polyarthralgias — treat with ceftriaxone.
- Neurologic compression syndromes
  - Lumbar spinal stenosis: neurogenic claudication — bilateral leg pain worse with walking/extension, improves with sitting/flexion; MRI diagnostic.
  - Cauda equina syndrome: compression of sacral nerve roots → bilateral sciatica, saddle anesthesia, bowel/bladder dysfunction, decreased sphincter tone — surgical emergency; most commonly due to large herniated lumbar disc.

Hematologic disorders — consolidated high‑yield emergency revision notes

Core concepts and pathophysiology
- Sickle cell disease complications
  - Splenic sequestration: acute pooling of large fraction of blood in an enlarged spleen → hypovolemia, anemia, high reticulocyte count (pre‑autosplenectomy). Can be life‑threatening.
  - Aplastic (pure red‑cell) crisis: marrow arrest of erythropoiesis caused most commonly by parvovirus B19 → severe anemia with low reticulocyte count.

- Tumor lysis syndrome (TLS)
  - Rapid tumor cell lysis → hyperuricemia, hyperphosphatemia, hyperkalemia, secondary hypocalcemia, AKI from uric acid and calcium‑phosphate precipitation.
- Transfusion reactions
  - Febrile non‑hemolytic (most common), allergic/anaphylactic, acute hemolytic (ABO incompatibility; life‑threatening), TRALI (non‑cardiogenic pulmonary edema within 6 hrs), TACO (volume overload).
- Coagulopathies / microangiopathies
  - DIC: consumptive coagulopathy in sepsis/trauma/malignancy → bleeding + thrombosis; labs: ↑PT/PTT, ↑D‑dimer, ↓fibrinogen, thrombocytopenia, schistocytes.
  - TTP: ADAMTS13 deficiency → large vWF multimers, platelet microthrombi → thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, neurologic/renal signs, fever sometimes.
  - ITP: immune platelet destruction → isolated thrombocytopenia, mucocutaneous bleeding; otherwise normal CBC and smear.
- Hematologic malignancies & paraproteinemias
  - ALL: most common childhood leukemia; marrow blasts >20% → pancytopenia, systemic symptoms.
  - AML: can present with gingival hyperplasia, cytopenias, blasts; Auer rods may be seen.
  - Multiple myeloma: monoclonal M‑protein, lytic bone lesions, anemia, renal failure, hypercalcemia; hyperviscosity syndromes possible.
- Thrombophilia
  - Factor V Leiden: common inherited cause of recurrent VTE; management of acute VTE is standard anticoagulation; consider long‑term anticoagulation if unprovoked/recurrent.
