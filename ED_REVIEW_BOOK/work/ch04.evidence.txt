A: Spurious elevation due to hemolysis during blood sampling
EXPLANATION: Hyperkalemia is generally defined as a serum potassium level > 5.0 mEq/L, although symptoms often do not become apparent until at least > 5.5 mEq/L. Potassium is a predominantly intracellular ion, with < 2% of potassium circulating in the blood. Serum potassium levels are tightly regulated by the kidney, with normal values ranging between 3.5 and 5.0 mEq/L. The most common cause of hyperkalemia is sampling error from hemolysis during or after the blood drawing process, leading to a spurious elevation. The most common cause of true hyperkalemia is acute or chronic kidney failure. This is because the vast majority of potassium excretion occurs through the kidney.

Q: A 68-year-old man with a history of thyroid surgery who has not been taking any medications presents to the ED with new-onset tremor, rigidity, and bradykinesia worsening over several months. He also reports new intermittent muscle cramps and paresthesias. Physical exam reveals cogwheel rigidity and resting tremor of the hands.
A: Calcification of the basal ganglia
EXPLANATION: Chronic hypoparathyroidism, most commonly postsurgical after thyroidectomy, results in persistent hypocalcemia and hyperphosphatemia. Despite the serum calcium level being low, the relative excess of phosphate causes the calcium-phosphate product to rise above the solubility threshold, leading to precipitation of calcium-phosphate complexes in soft tissues. The basal ganglia are particularly susceptible because of their high metabolic activity and unique vascular supply, predisposing them to mineral deposition over time. This process explains why calcium deposits can occur even in the context of hypocalcemia.

Q: An ill-appearing 35-year-old woman with a history of Addison disease presents to the emergency department with 2 days of nausea and vomiting and is unable to tolerate anything by mouth. A bolus of intravenous normal saline with 5% dextrose is initiated.
A: Hydrocortisone
EXPLANATION: Hydrocortisone should be given as the patient is presenting in adrenal crisis. This disorder is characterized by hypotension responsive to parenteral glucocorticoids within 1 hour of their administration. Cortisol, a glucocorticoid, is produced in the adrenal cortex. Its primary functions are to maintain normoglycemia via modulation of gluconeogenesis and blood pressure by sensitizing blood vessels to vasoconstrictors. Aldosterone, a mineralocorticoid also secreted by the adrenal cortex, is controlled by the renin-angiotensin system and responds to elevated potassium levels.

Q: Which of the following is a risk factor for developing hypercalcemia?
A: Sarcoidosis
EXPLANATION: Hypercalcemia is a common clinical condition typically defined by a serum calcium level > 10.5 mg/dL. Hypercalcemia is considered mild at levels 10.5–12 mg/dL, and levels > 14 mg/dL can be life threatening. Broadly speaking, there are five common categories of causes of hypercalcemia: malignancy, endocrine disease, granulomatous disease, pharmacologic agents, and miscellaneous cases. Within these categories, hyperparathyroidism and malignancy make up most cases. Once these have been ruled out, further diagnoses should be considered, including granulomatous diseases like sarcoidosis and histoplasmosis.

Q: A 2-year-old boy who is actively seizing presents to the emergency department early one morning. Oxygen is supplied, and the patient is placed on monitors. An intravenous line is placed, and the patient is given two doses of intravenous lorazepam but continues to seize. The parent reports he has never had a seizure before and has not been sick.
A: Fingerstick glucose
EXPLANATION: Hypoglycemia is an extremely uncommon condition in children, as it can be increased through the release of multiple hormones, including glucagon, cortisol, epinephrine, thyroxine, growth hormone, and adrenocorticotropic hormone. Most children undergoing a resuscitation will have a normal or high serum glucose unless they have low glycogen stores (e.g., in a neonate), a defect in glycogen metabolism (e.g., glycogen storage disease), or adrenal insufficiency. This, and glucose being commonly measured by emergency medical services, makes hypoglycemia in children incredibly easy to overlook. Management of seizures starts with supplemental oxygen and airway positioning.

Q: A previously healthy 21-year-old man is found unresponsive by his roommates. On arrival at the emergency department, his pupils are pinpoint, and his oxygen saturation is 88% on room air. A blood gas reveals a pH of 7.25, PCO2 of 60 mm Hg, PO2 of 65 mm Hg, and bicarbonate of 26 mEq/L. Which of the following mechanisms is the most likely cause of his laboratory abnormalities?
A: Decreased respiratory drive
EXPLANATION: Hypercapnia is defined as a PCO2 > 45 mm Hg. Hypercapnia is caused by alveolar hypoventilation. If ventilation is inadequate, carbon dioxide is not exhaled at an appropriate rate, and levels build up in the blood. This patient’s elevated PCO2 and bicarbonate at the upper limit of normal indicate that he has an acute respiratory acidosis from hypoventilation. Hypoventilation results from a decreased respiratory rate, decreased tidal volume, or increased dead space ventilation.

Q: A 56-year-old man is brought to the ED by EMS after being found at home with a decreased level of consciousness. On arrival, his vital signs are a T of 39.2°C (102.6°F), BP of 86/52 mm Hg, HR of 138 bpm, RR of 28/min, and SpO2 of 91% on room air. He is intubated for airway protection. Broad laboratory studies are obtained, including thyroid function tests.
A: Nonthyroidal illness syndrome
EXPLANATION: This patient presents with sepsis and multiorgan dysfunction, and his thyroid studies are classic for nonthyroidal illness syndrome (NTI), also called euthyroid sick syndrome. It occurs in patients with severe systemic illnesses such as sepsis, trauma, myocardial infarction, or major surgery. The course of the disease reflects a progressive disturbance in thyroid hormone metabolism and regulation. In the early phase, there is reduced peripheral conversion of T4 to T3 due to decreased activity of type I 5’-deiodinase, leading to low serum T3 and within normal limits or slightly increased reverse T3 levels, while T4 and TSH remain within normal limits.

### [ROSH | rosh-em-initial-2026-endocrine-metabolic-and-nutritional-disorders-q-322]
Q: A 5-day-old infant without prenatal care presents to the emergency department after a seizure. His history and exam findings suggest DiGeorge syndrome as he has difficulty feeding, micrognathia, low-set ears, a harsh systolic murmur, no thymus on X-ray, and long QT on ECG. Measurements of his electrolytes show an ionized calcium of 0.6 mmol/L (normal 1.05–1.3 mmol/L) verified by repeat analysis.
A: High phosphorus measurement
EXPLANATION: There are four parathyroid glands located near the thyroid that are primarily responsible for the creation of parathyroid hormone. Parathyroid hormone regulates serum calcium levels by stimulating bone resorption and intestinal calcium absorption while decreasing kidney calcium excretion and facilitating calcitriol (1,25-hydroxy vitamin D) synthesis. Hypoparathyroidism can be caused by genetic deletions such as DiGeorge syndrome or X-linked recessive hypoparathyroidism, surgical removal of the parathyroid glands, or autoimmune disorders. Patients with DiGeorge syndrome typically present with midline abnormalities such as palatal or facial defects and cardiac abnormalities.
