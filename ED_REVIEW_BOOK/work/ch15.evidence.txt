He has a history of hypertension, diabetes, and kidney failure and is on peritoneal dialysis. His temperature is 101.3°F (38.5°C), blood pressure is 120/80 mm Hg, heart rate is 110 bpm, respiratory rate is 18/min, and oxygen saturation is 96% on room air.
A: Peritoneal fluid WBC count of 150 cells/µL with 60% neutrophils
EXPLANATION: Peritonitis is a common complication of patients undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD) and is responsible for > 15% of deaths in this patient population. This diagnosis can lead to decreased filtration rates in PD, necessitating conversion to hemodialysis. The most common symptoms of peritonitis in these patients are abdominal pain, fever, and cloudy dialysis effluent. Any patient with cloudy dialysis effluent should be suspected to have peritonitis, and effluent should be sent to the laboratory for cell count, differential, Gram stain, and culture.

Q: A 4-year-old boy presents to the ED with extreme scrotal pain and discoloration. He is difficult to console. He has an absent cremasteric reflex on the right with an erythematous right hemiscrotum. The right testis appears to be in a horizontal lie. Which of the following is the most appropriate next step to take?
A: Administer parenteral analgesia
EXPLANATION: This patient’s clinical presentation is concerning for testicular torsion. This is a urologic emergency in which delaying diagnosis and treatment can lead to testicular infarction, loss of the testis, or infertility. When the testis is abnormally fixed to the tunica vaginalis within the scrotum, the testis or spermatic cord are at risk for torsion. Other causes of acute scrotal pain include epididymitis, incarcerated hernia, testicular trauma, and torsion of testicular appendages. Minor trauma, periods of growth (e.g., puberty), and sleep (during which unilateral cremasteric contraction occurs) may lead to testicular twisting.

Q: A 32-year-old woman presents with 3 days of dysuria and lower abdominal discomfort. She is sexually active with one male partner and uses condoms inconsistently. She reports no vaginal bleeding or discharge. Her medical history is unremarkable. Vital signs show a BP of 118/65 mm Hg, HR of 67 bpm, T of 98.3°F, RR of 16 breaths/min, and SpO2 of 99%.
A: Escherichia coli
EXPLANATION: Acute cystitis is an isolated bladder infection. Female individuals are more likely than male individuals to have lower UTIs due to shorter urethral length. UTIs typically result from ascending bacteria from the urethra. Symptomatic patients with a urine culture that grows > 102 CFU/mL are considered infected. The concentration of bacteria in the bladder can increase exponentially in female patients after sexual intercourse. Escherichia coli is the most common causative organism and is responsible for > 80% of UTIs. E. coli converts nitrates to nitrite, which is a very useful test to confirm the diagnosis of a UTI. The urine nitrate reaction has > 90% specificity.

Q: An 11-year-old boy presents to the ED with a gradual onset of right-sided testicular pain. He reports no penile discharge or pain with urination. He is fully vaccinated and has had no recent travel or animal exposure. On exam, his vital signs are within normal limits for age, he is prepubertal, and he has a normal-appearing penis and testis with bilateral cremasteric reflexes.
A: Supportive care
EXPLANATION: Epididymitis is inflammation of the epididymis, most commonly from ascending infection. It often but not always presents with a gradual onset of scrotal pain compared to testicular torsion, which is more frequently acute. Reports of painful urination, fevers, and penile discharge may be present, suggesting a bacterial cause of epididymitis. Antecedent trauma suggests a traumatic cause. Patients may have some scrotal edema and erythema but have normal cremasteric reflexes on exam.

### [ROSH | rosh-em-initial-2026-renal-and-urogenital-disorders-q-159]
Q: A 12-year-old boy presents to the ED with swelling and pain in his penis. He reports that, over the past 6 hours, he has been unable to reduce his foreskin over the glans, and the area has become progressively more swollen. He does not report dysuria, discharge, testicular pain, or any other symptoms prior to his foreskin being retracted earlier today.
A: Paraphimosis
EXPLANATION: Paraphimosis is the inability to reduce the foreskin back to its anatomic position over the glans of the penis. It has several causes, including infection, trauma, hair tourniquets, and sexual activity. Distal venous congestion develops and can lead to arterial compromise, necrosis, and gangrene. It is a urologic emergency. This patient’s absence of penile discharge and testicular symptoms, along with the sudden development of symptoms after not being able to reduce his foreskin, strongly point to paraphimosis as the diagnosis.
