## Diagnosis

### Anaphylaxis and angioedema

- Urticaria with vomiting and throat tightness means three organ systems are involved; hives with wheeze during outdoor exercise points to exercise as the trigger; return of stridor and swelling after initial improvement marks severe anaphylaxis. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

| Feature | Mast cell–mediated angioedema | Bradykinin-mediated angioedema |
|---|---|---|
| Examples | Allergic reaction, anaphylaxis | ACE inhibitor, angiotensin receptor blocker, aliskiren, thrombolytics, estrogens; hereditary C1 inhibitor deficiency |
| Urticaria, itch, flushing, bronchospasm | Present | Absent |
| Response to epinephrine | Yes | No |
| Diagnosis and treatment | Clinical | Serum C4 for hereditary angioedema; stop the drug, then C1 inhibitor, fresh frozen plasma, tranexamic acid or icatibant |

- Gradual onset over 24 hours with asymmetric, nonpitting, non-gravity-dependent edema suggests C1 esterase inhibitor deficiency; swelling progressing over an hour with dyspnea on lisinopril threatens the airway. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Systemic lupus erythematosus

- Antinuclear antibody is present in 95% of patients, so a negative result makes lupus very unlikely; anti-Smith antibody is the most specific test. Diagnosis requires at least 4 of 11 criteria, which include malar or discoid rash, photosensitivity, oral ulcers, symmetric polyarthritis, serositis, and kidney, neurologic or hematologic disorders; about 20% of patients present with discoid rashes. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Pericarditis shows diffuse concave-up ST elevation with PR depression or a small pericardial effusion; chest pain in a patient with known SLE warrants cardiac enzymes because of accelerated atherosclerosis and a hypercoagulable state. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Rheumatoid arthritis versus osteoarthritis

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Morning stiffness and pain lasting more than 1 hour, improving with activity | Rheumatoid arthritis rather than osteoarthritis [PEER-PH][ROSH] |
| Constitutional symptoms such as fatigue, fever, malaise or weight loss | Rheumatoid arthritis; absent in osteoarthritis [ROSH] |
| Symmetric metacarpophalangeal, proximal interphalangeal, wrist and metatarsophalangeal involvement, distal interphalangeal sparing | Rheumatoid arthritis [ROSH] |
| Neck pain at the base of the skull, occipital headache, arm paresthesias, vertigo on looking down | Atlantoaxial subluxation; flexion and extension views are contraindicated [ROSH] |
| Rheumatoid arthritis with neutropenia and splenomegaly | Felty syndrome [PEER-PH] |

### Systemic sclerosis and sarcoidosis

- Sclerodactyly with Raynaud phenomenon defines systemic sclerosis; CREST syndrome adds calcinosis, esophageal dysmotility and telangiectasia, while digital ulceration or finger contractures with crepitus along the triceps tendon also indicate systemic sclerosis. New renal failure with severe hypertension and encephalopathy and a normal urinalysis indicates scleroderma renal crisis. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Sarcoidosis: the chest radiograph is abnormal in 90% of patients, and 5–20% present initially with bilateral uveitis. Elevated serum angiotensin-converting enzyme, hypercalcemia with a shortened QT interval, or flank pain with dark urine and a low parathyroid hormone level reflect granuloma production of calcitriol; restrictive cardiomyopathy, atrioventricular block or arrhythmia indicates cardiac sarcoidosis, in approximately 5% of patients. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Streptococcal sequelae

| Category | Findings |
|---|---|
| Major Jones criteria | Carditis, polyarthritis, chorea, erythema marginatum, subcutaneous nodules [PEER-PH][ROSH] |
| Minor Jones criteria | Fever, arthralgia, elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate or C-reactive protein, prolonged PR interval [PEER-PH][ROSH] |
| Requirement | Preceding streptococcal infection plus two major criteria, or one major plus two minor [PEER-PH][ROSH] |

- Acute rheumatic fever occurs 2–4 weeks after group A *Streptococcus* pharyngitis, most commonly in children aged 5–15 years, and subclinical carditis on echocardiography is a major criterion. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis: dark, tea-colored urine with periorbital edema, hypertension, hematuria, red blood cell casts and oliguria is the nephritic syndrome. It follows pharyngitis at 1–3 weeks or impetigo at 3–6 weeks, a positive antistreptolysin O titer confirms the antecedent infection, and it is the most common cause of acute nephritis in children worldwide. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Kawasaki disease

| Criterion | Detail |
|---|---|
| Required | Fever for 5 days or more [PEER-PH][ROSH] |
| Plus four of five | Bilateral nonexudative conjunctivitis; oral mucous membrane changes such as fissured lips, injected pharynx or strawberry tongue; extremity erythema, edema or desquamation; polymorphous nonvesicular rash; cervical lymphadenopathy, typically unilateral and greater than 1.5 cm [PEER-PH][ROSH] |
| Abbreviated presentation | Fewer than four signs suffices when echocardiography shows coronary artery disease or laboratory tests support it; a patient meeting all other criteria can be diagnosed with only 4 days of fever [PEER-PH][ROSH] |

- It most commonly affects children under 5 years with a male predominance, and the acute phase lasts 1–2 weeks. Supplemental laboratory criteria are sterile pyuria of 10 or more white blood cells per high-power field, elevated inflammatory markers, low albumin, high platelet count and elevated alanine aminotransferase, with platelets rising after day 7. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Reactive arthritis and transplant complications

- Reactive arthritis: asymmetric oligoarthritis of the lower extremities 2–6 weeks after urethritis or dysentery, with conjunctival injection, low back pain and a synovial white blood cell count of 40,000 cells/mm³, associated with *Chlamydia trachomatis*, *Ureaplasma urealyticum*, *Salmonella*, *Shigella*, *Yersinia*, *Campylobacter*, *Escherichia coli* and *Clostridioides difficile*. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

| Timing after transplant | Most likely process |
|---|---|
| Minutes to hours | Hyperacute rejection from preformed antibodies [ROSH] |
| First month | Nosocomial or surgical complications [PEER-PH] |
| 1–6 months | Opportunistic infection — cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus, *Aspergillus* — and acute rejection [PEER-PH][ROSH] |
| Beyond 6 months | Community-acquired pneumonia, urinary tract infection, late viral infection such as human JC polyomavirus [PEER-PH] |

- Acute rejection at 2 weeks to 6 months presents with fever, decreased urine output, hypertension, graft tenderness and creatinine rising from 0.8 to 1.9 mg/dL; most patients are asymptomatic and the diagnosis is made by surveillance, confirmed by biopsy showing lymphocytic infiltration. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Cytomegalovirus at 2 months often manifests as pneumonia; *Aspergillus fumigatus* causes dyspnea, cough and hemoptysis around 5 months; human JC polyomavirus beyond 6 months causes progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy with visual change and dementia. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Dyspnea with nonproductive cough and wheeze years after lung transplant indicates bronchiolitis obliterans; dyspnea on exertion 10 months after heart transplant suggests acute cardiac allograft rejection; hypertension with a rising protein to creatinine ratio years after transplant suggests chronic allograft nephropathy. Painless blood blisters turning necrotic within hours indicate ecthyma gangrenosum from *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*, and a rash on the neck and posterior shoulders weeks after allogeneic stem cell transplant indicates acute graft-versus-host disease. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Drug hypersensitivity eruptions

- Fever with a diffuse erythematous rash and lymphadenopathy 2–3 weeks after starting carbamazepine, with skin tenderness, blistering, erythroderma, mucositis or facial edema, indicates severe drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS); a recurrent hyperpigmented lesion blistering at the same site after a drug is a fixed drug eruption, and pruritic papules or vesicles with linear streaking after contact is contact dermatitis. [ROSH]

## Management

### Anaphylaxis — sequence

1. Remove the trigger and call for help. [ROSH]
2. Give intramuscular epinephrine immediately: 1:1,000 (1 mg/mL) 0.3 mg in an adult, or 0.01 mL/kg of 1 mg/mL in a child. The intramuscular route is preferred initially because it is efficacious, easily administered and has fewer adverse effects than the intravenous route. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
3. Establish intravenous access, give oxygen, and begin aggressive crystalloid resuscitation early for third spacing and loss of vasomotor tone. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
4. Add adjuncts — antihistamines, histamine-2 blockers, corticosteroids, inhaled beta-agonists for bronchoconstriction — but never in place of epinephrine. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
5. If collapse persists after two intramuscular doses, switch to an epinephrine intravenous infusion and avoid bolus dosing; give glucagon 1 mg IV every 5 minutes if the patient takes a beta-blocker. Admit to intensive care for the infusion and observe for biphasic return of stridor and swelling. [PEER-PH]

### Angioedema — sequence

1. Assess the airway first. Intubation is the most definitive step when the airway is threatened; angioedema makes intubation very challenging, so it must not be delayed. For mast cell–mediated angioedema with anaphylaxis, epinephrine is the cornerstone after the airway is secured. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
2. For ACE-inhibitor angioedema, discontinue the drug and give supportive care; tranexamic acid, icatibant and C1 esterase inhibitor may be useful, though data are limited, and typical anaphylaxis medications are ineffective. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
3. For hereditary angioedema, C1 inhibitor is most efficacious because it stops the bradykinin cascade; fresh frozen plasma is an alternative, and epinephrine, steroids and antihistamines do not work. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Localized reactions and connective tissue disease

- A reaction confined to the point of allergen contact — one side of the tongue and cheek with itching but no dysphagia or dyspnea — is treated with intravenous diphenhydramine plus methylprednisolone plus famotidine; inhaled corticosteroids should be given with a spacer. [ROSH]
- Acute lupus flares are treated with glucocorticoids including prednisone; screen women with SLE for antiphospholipid syndrome when pregnancy is planned. Rheumatoid arthritis is managed symptomatically with NSAIDs and steroids, with disease-modifying agents such as methotrexate for long-term control, and flexion and extension views of the cervical spine are avoided because they may precipitate acute cervical myelopathy. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Methotrexate toxicity — stomatitis, throat pain, severe diarrhea — is treated with folinic acid, the activated form of folic acid; cumulative doses greater than 100–150 mg over a few days increase risk, and other measures include multi-dose activated charcoal, urinary alkalinization, hemodialysis and glucarpidase. [PEER-PH]
- Raynaud phenomenon persisting after gentle rewarming is treated with nifedipine or nitrates; scleroderma renal crisis is treated with captopril first-line, adding nitroprusside for severe hypertension with central nervous system effects, and high-dose steroids are avoided. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Sarcoidosis and vasculitis

- Suspected giant cell arteritis: start steroids immediately to prevent irreversible vision loss; temporal artery biopsy can be performed after steroids are started. [PEER-PH]
- Erythema nodosum in sarcoidosis: NSAIDs, rest and elevation, with corticosteroids only for refractory or severe cases. Most newly diagnosed sarcoidosis can be managed as an outpatient with corticosteroids as the mainstay for ocular disease, and bilateral uveitis with bilateral hilar adenopathy does not require emergency antibiotics or surgery. [PEER-PH]

### Streptococcal sequelae

- Poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis has no specific therapy and management is supportive; intravenous furosemide is used for the hypertension and volume overload of the nephritic presentation. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Acute rheumatic fever: eradicate the streptococcal infection — penicillin is the antibiotic of choice, with long-acting penicillin G benzathine preferred, and clindamycin for severe penicillin allergy, with azithromycin and erythromycin as alternatives despite higher resistance rates. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Kawasaki disease

1. Admit and obtain echocardiography to assess the coronary arteries and left ventricular function. [ROSH]
2. Give intravenous immune globulin 2 g/kg over 8–12 hours; this reduces the risk of coronary artery aneurysms from 20–25% to 3–4%. [PEER-PH]
3. Give aspirin, continuing it until the patient has been afebrile for 48 hours and then reducing the dose. [PEER-PH]
4. Plasma exchange is not indicated. [PEER-PH]

### Reactive arthritis, rejection and drug reactions

- Reactive arthritis: first-line treatment is an NSAID, commonly indomethacin; antibiotics may benefit postchlamydial reactive arthritis, but NSAIDs remain first-line for pain. [PEER-PH]
- When renal graft rejection is suspected, obtain renal ultrasound with Doppler first to assess for thrombosis, vascular stenosis, hematoma, fistula, urine leakage, abscess and other infections; discuss contrast studies with the transplant team because of nephrotoxicity, and confirm rejection with biopsy. In a transplant patient on a calcineurin inhibitor with decreased renal function, the three most common causes are infection, rejection and medication-induced nephropathy. [PEER-PH]
- Acute cardiac allograft rejection is treated with intravenous methylprednisolone and cardiology consultation for endomyocardial biopsy; ecthyma gangrenosum requires immediate broad-spectrum antibiotics with double antipseudomonal coverage. [ROSH]
- Drug eruptions: stop the offending medication, obtain a complete blood cell count and peripheral smear when DRESS is suspected, and treat exanthematous, fixed drug and contact eruptions symptomatically — oral antihistamines and topical steroids for fixed drug eruption, and removal of the antigen with antihistamines for contact dermatitis. [ROSH]
