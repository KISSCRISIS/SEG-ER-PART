## Diagnosis

### Hernias of the abdominal wall

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Groin bulge worse with straining or Valsalva | Inguinal hernia |
| Bulge lateral to the epigastric vessels through the internal inguinal ring | Indirect inguinal hernia |
| Defect below the inguinal ligament, medial to the femoral vein | Femoral hernia |
| Small defect beside the semilunar line at the arcuate line | Spigelian hernia |
| Tender protrusion with purple or erythematous skin in a toxic patient | Strangulated hernia |

- Inguinal hernias are 75% of all abdominal wall hernias and the most common hernia in both sexes; two-thirds are indirect and indirect hernias are twice as common as direct. [PEER-PH]
- Femoral hernias are 4% of groin hernias but up to 40% present incarcerated or strangulated, the highest strangulation risk of the groin hernias, and are more common in women; Spigelian hernias show about 20% incarceration and up to 15% strangulation. [PEER-CS][ROSH]
- Preterm birth raises inguinal hernia incidence from 3–5% in full-term infants to as high as 30% in premature infants, and 90% of pediatric inguinal hernias occur in boys; umbilical hernias occur in 10–30% of births and close spontaneously within 2 years. [PEER-PH]
- CT with intravenous contrast is preferred when incarceration or strangulation is suspected. [PEER-PH]

### Esophageal and swallowing disorders

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Burning retrosternal pain after meals, worse lying flat, relieved by antacids | GERD |
| Odynophagia and chest pain after a new medication | Pill esophagitis |
| Odynophagia with white oral plaques in advanced HIV | Esophageal candidiasis |
| Dysphagia to solids and liquids, regurgitation, no heartburn | Achalasia |
| Sudden unrelenting chest pain radiating to neck and back after forceful vomiting | Boerhaave syndrome |
| Copious secretions and choking with feeds in a neonate | Tracheoesophageal fistula |

- Endoscopy rather than empiric therapy is required for age over 60, weight loss, anorexia, persistent vomiting, iron-deficiency anemia, GI bleeding, dysphagia, odynophagia, or GI cancer in a first-degree relative. [PEER-PH]
- Pill esophagitis from doxycycline and other tetracyclines, bisphosphonates, NSAIDs, or potassium chloride causes acute chest pain and odynophagia, especially with a large pill, little fluid, and dosing at bedtime; achalasia reflects myenteric aganglionosis. [ROSH]
- Esophageal perforation is most often iatrogenic from endoscopy then dilation; Boerhaave rupture is spontaneous and distal, a water-soluble contrast esophagram is definitive, and the esophagus carries the highest mortality of any perforated GI structure. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Upper gastrointestinal bleeding

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Coffee-ground emesis with burning epigastric pain, NSAID or ulcer history | Peptic ulcer disease |
| Hematemesis after repeated forceful vomiting, often binge drinking | Mallory-Weiss syndrome |
| Massive hematemesis after a herald bleed in a patient with an aortic graft | Aortoenteric fistula |

- Peptic ulcer disease is the most common cause of upper GI bleeding at any age and the most common below 50; H. pylori causes 70–80% of duodenal and 60–70% of gastric ulcers, and bleeding complicates 15–20% of ulcers. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Mallory-Weiss is a longitudinal partial-thickness tear at the gastroesophageal junction after forceful vomiting, accounts for 8–15% of upper GI bleeds, and usually stops spontaneously; varices are 10–20% of bleeds and lie superficially in the distal esophagus. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Aortoenteric fistula causes a herald bleed then catastrophic hemorrhage after aortic graft or aneurysm, and CT angiography is the preferred study. [ROSH]

### Biliary tract, liver, and pancreas

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Postprandial or nocturnal dull RUQ pain for 30 minutes to 6 hours, no fever | Biliary colic |
| Constant RUQ pain, fever, sonographic Murphy sign, wall over 3 mm | Acute cholecystitis |
| Severe RUQ pain radiating to the back, diabetes, gas in the gallbladder wall | Emphysematous cholecystitis |
| RUQ pain, fever, jaundice with a dilated common bile duct | Ascending cholangitis |
| Epigastric pain radiating to the back with raised lipase | Acute pancreatitis |

- Gallstone risk includes advanced age, female sex (2–3 times men), parity, obesity, rapid weight loss, family history, and oral contraceptives; cholelithiasis affects about 20% of adults in the developed world and 20–40% develop complications. [PEER-PH][PEER-CS][ROSH]
- Ultrasound is the initial study of choice for cholecystitis (sensitivity 94%, specificity 78%) and shows stones, wall thickening over 3 mm, pericholecystic fluid, and a sonographic Murphy sign with a positive predictive value above 90% when stones are present. [PEER-PH][ROSH][PEER-CS]
- The common bile duct is under 6 mm below age 60 and increases about 0.1 cm per decade after 60. Cholangitis is caused by gallstones in about 65% of cases; Charcot triad appears in 50–75% and Reynolds pentad adds hypotension and altered mental status in under 10%. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Alcohol-related liver disease gives an AST:ALT ratio above 2; acute hepatitis B shows HBsAg, anti-HBc IgM, and HBeAg positive; chronic hepatitis C accounts for nearly one third of hepatocellular carcinoma, which arises almost exclusively in cirrhosis with a 1–4% annual risk. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Acute pancreatitis is most often caused by gallstones worldwide, then alcohol; triglycerides above 500 mg/dL are causative and above 1,000 mg/dL cause more severe inflammation. Ranson criteria include glucose above 200 mg/dL, AST above 250 U/L, and age over 55. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Bowel obstruction, volvulus, and ischemia

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Dilated bowel with prominent plicae circulares and bilious nasogastric aspirate | Small bowel obstruction |
| Haustral markings, peripheral loops, faeces in the lumen | Large bowel obstruction |
| Air in the colon and rectum on plain film | Paralytic ileus rather than obstruction |
| Coffee-bean loop with an air-fluid level | Volvulus |
| Pain out of proportion to examination, raised lactate, soft abdomen | Acute mesenteric ischemia |
| Distension with pneumatosis intestinalis in a premature infant | Necrotizing enterocolitis |

- Adhesions cause over 60% of small bowel obstructions, neoplasms about 20%, hernias about 10%, and gallstone ileus 1–4%; without surgical history malignancy is most likely, colorectal cancer is the most common cause of large bowel obstruction, and a cecal width of 13 cm signals impending ischemia. [PEER-CS][PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Acute mesenteric ischemia usually follows embolic occlusion of the superior mesenteric artery with atrial fibrillation as a major risk factor, and CT angiography is the test of choice; ischemic colitis is the most common cause of bowel ischemia and causes left-sided pain with bloody stools. [ROSH][PEER-CS]
- Hirschsprung disease is an aganglionic distal colon involving the rectosigmoid in 75% and presents with meconium beyond 48 hours and explosive stool on rectal examination; malrotation leads to volvulus in about 75% of cases with a corkscrew at the ligament of Treitz on upper GI series. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Pyloric stenosis presents at 2–8 weeks with nonbilious projectile vomiting and an olive-shaped epigastric mass; necrotizing enterocolitis affects premature infants within the first 3 weeks with pathognomonic pneumatosis intestinalis and 15–30% mortality. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Anorectal disease

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Painful purple perianal mass | Thrombosed external hemorrhoid |
| Pain during and after defecation with a posterior midline tear | Anal fissure |
| Fluctuant tender perianal mass | Perianal or perirectal abscess |

### Abdominal pain from outside the GI tract

- Examine the genitals in any male with abdominal pain; absence of the cremasteric reflex has 96% sensitivity and 88% specificity for testicular torsion and Doppler ultrasound has 93% sensitivity and 100% specificity. [PEER-CS]

## Management

### Upper gastrointestinal hemorrhage

1. Secure the airway in any patient with hematemesis or coffee-ground emesis who cannot protect it, and obtain large-bore access for crystalloid and blood. [PEER-PH]
2. Start an intravenous proton pump inhibitor (pantoprazole or esomeprazole) for suspected ulcer bleeding. [PEER-PH][PEER-CS]
3. Give prophylactic antibiotics to every cirrhotic patient with GI bleeding before endoscopy; ceftriaxone reduces mortality, rebleeding, and infection, and untreated cirrhotic patients have a 30–40% infection risk. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
4. Give octreotide for suspected variceal bleeding, bolus 50–100 mcg then infusion at 50 mcg/hr, then arrange upper endoscopy, which is both diagnostic and definitive through band ligation or sclerotherapy; if endoscopy fails to localize the source in active hematochezia, the next test is CT angiography. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
5. For hemorrhage persisting despite octreotide and endoscopy, use balloon tamponade as a temporizing measure or proceed to transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS), which raises survival in severe variceal bleeding from 10% to 60% with hemostasis in over 90%. [PEER-CS][PEER-PH]

| Tamponade step | Precaution |
|---|---|
| Before full gastric balloon inflation | Confirm on radiograph that the balloon is below the diaphragm |
| Gastric balloon | Inflate to 30–40 mm Hg |
| Esophageal balloon | Reduce by 5 mm Hg every 3 hours to 25 mm Hg |
| Most lethal complication | Esophageal perforation with mediastinitis |

### Cholangitis and cholecystitis

1. Cholangitis: resuscitate with intravenous fluids and start broad-spectrum antibiotics immediately; ceftriaxone with metronidazole is appropriate for the febrile jaundiced patient with right upper quadrant tenderness. [ROSH]
2. Cholangitis: proceed to endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography for decompression. Mild to moderate disease responds to antibiotics alone in 70–80%, but unstable vital signs or septic shock require emergent decompression, and all patients are admitted. [PEER-PH]
3. Cholecystitis: give broad-spectrum antibiotics and obtain surgical consultation; perforated calculous cholecystitis requires piperacillin-tazobactam, since E. coli is the most common organism. Emphysematous cholecystitis needs antibiotics covering gas-producing organisms plus decompression or cholecystectomy. [PEER-PH]
4. Suspected duct stones: intermediate risk (age over 55, abnormal liver function tests, or dilated duct; 10–50% probability) is evaluated with magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography or endoscopic ultrasound rather than endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography. [PEER-PH]

### Acute pancreatitis

1. Resuscitate with crystalloid, 30 mL/kg when vital signs are unstable; Lactated Ringer is appropriate in most cases, but use normal saline in hypercalcemia because Lactated Ringer contains 3 mEq/L calcium. [PEER-CS][ROSH]
2. Give analgesia and antiemetics, maintain bowel rest, support the diagnosis with lipase, and obtain CT of the abdomen when necrotizing pancreatitis is suspected. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
3. Refer symptomatic or obstructing pseudocysts to interventional gastroenterology for endoscopic ultrasound-guided drainage, and document direct disclosure of indeterminate incidental findings. [PEER-PH]

### Bowel obstruction and volvulus

1. Keep the patient nothing by mouth, place a nasogastric tube, admit, and give intravenous fluids, analgesia, and antiemetics; vomiting causes significant dehydration. [PEER-PH]
2. Obtain CT with intravenous contrast, the modality of choice for complete small bowel obstruction, and follow with serial abdominal examinations; most patients improve with nonoperative management over 48–72 hours. [PEER-PH]
3. Operate emergently for ischemic bowel, perforation, or peritonitis, and admit Crohn disease with obstruction for intravenous steroids. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
4. Large bowel obstruction: hydrate intravenously, give piperacillin-tazobactam when ill, and involve surgery. Sigmoid volvulus without peritoneal signs is decompressed with flexible sigmoidoscopy; peritonitis, ischemia, or failed decompression requires resection. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Mesenteric ischemia and ischemic colitis

1. Suspect it when pain is disproportionate to a soft abdomen; obtain lactate and CT angiography. [ROSH]
2. Give intravenous crystalloid, keep the patient nothing by mouth, admit for bowel rest, and start a heparin infusion when thrombosis is the mechanism; consult surgery, since the window before transmural necrosis may be as short as 6 hours. Ischemic colitis is managed the same way, with operation reserved for infarction or perforation. [ROSH][PEER-CS]

### Cirrhosis: ascites, peritonitis, encephalopathy, hepatorenal syndrome

1. Perform bedside diagnostic paracentesis for ascites with fever, abdominal pain, or encephalopathy; paracentesis is safe in the second trimester of pregnancy. Diagnose spontaneous bacterial peritonitis with an ascitic polymorphonuclear leukocyte count above 250 cells/mm3 (an absolute neutrophil count of 300 cells/mm3 also qualifies) and treat with cefotaxime 2 g intravenously, using intravenous ciprofloxacin for severe cephalosporin allergy. [PEER-CS][PEER-PH][ROSH]
2. Give albumin for hepatorenal syndrome, 1 g/kg up to 100 g for 2 days, because it improves mortality; treat the precipitant (sepsis, GI bleeding, large-volume paracentesis) and refer for liver transplantation, the definitive therapy. [PEER-CS][ROSH]
3. Treat hepatic encephalopathy with lactulose, which acts as an osmotic laxative, lowers stool pH to trap ammonia as ammonium, and stimulates colonic bacteria to break down ammonia; check glucose in altered mental status and anticipate worsening encephalopathy after TIPS because the shunt raises circulating ammonia. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Hernia reduction

1. Reduce an incarcerated hernia only when strangulation is absent: supine Trendelenburg position with an ice pack over the swelling, plus intravenous analgesia or procedural sedation to relax the abdominal wall. [PEER-PH][PEER-CS]
2. Palpate the fascial defect with the non-dominant hand and apply gentle constant inward pressure with the dominant hand. Do not attempt reduction with overlying erythema, exquisite tenderness, systemic illness, or peritoneal signs, because ischemic bowel pushed into the abdomen can perforate and cause peritonitis. [PEER-PH][PEER-CS]
3. Strangulated hernia: nothing by mouth, immediate surgical consultation, intravenous fluids, analgesia, antibiotics, and preoperative laboratory studies. Discharge a reducible hernia for outpatient surgical follow-up and manage an infant umbilical hernia with reassurance. [PEER-PH][PEER-CS]

### Anorectal procedures

| Condition | Management |
|---|---|
| Thrombosed external hemorrhoid within 72 hours | Anesthetize, excise with an elliptical incision, remove the clot; recurrence 5–19% |
| Thrombosed external hemorrhoid beyond 72 hours, improving | Conservative care |
| Symptomatic hemorrhoids | Hydrocortisone with lidocaine cream and sitz baths |
| Acute anal fissure | Sitz baths and a high-fiber diet |
| Chronic anal fissure | Topical nifedipine with lidocaine gel; nitroglycerin ointment as an alternative |
| Simple perianal abscess | Incision and drainage with a radial incision close to the anal verge |
| Deep or indurated perirectal abscess | CT scan and surgical consultation |
| Irreducible rectal prolapse | Topical granulated sugar to reduce edema, then gentle continuous pressure |

### Caustic and foreign body ingestion

1. Intentional large-volume strong caustic ingestion: admit for monitoring and arrange endoscopy to grade injury. Acid causes coagulation necrosis with an eschar that limits depth but carries higher mortality from gastric damage; alkali causes liquefaction necrosis with deeper penetration. [PEER-PH]
2. Assess the airway early for stridor, hoarseness, or drooling, and remove the patient's clothes for decontamination; a small accidental ingestion of low-concentration household alkali in an asymptomatic patient (bleach is 3–6% sodium hypochlorite at pH 11) may be discharged. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
3. Button battery in the esophagus: emergent endoscopic removal, because lodgment can cause mucosal injury and perforation within 6 hours; battery versus coin is distinguished by the double ring sign on the anteroposterior view and a stack of two coins on the lateral view. [PEER-PH]
4. Urgent endoscopy is indicated for sharp or elongated objects, multiple objects, and multiple magnets, using CT to locate a radiolucent foreign body. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Pediatric surgical emergencies

1. Malrotation with midgut volvulus: nothing by mouth, nasogastric decompression, intravenous fluids, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and emergent laparotomy, because vascular compromise can cause ischemic necrosis in as little as 1 hour. [ROSH][PEER-CS]
2. Intussusception: ultrasound first, then air contrast enema, which is both diagnostic and therapeutic. Necrotizing enterocolitis is treated by stopping feedings, nasogastric decompression, and intravenous antibiotics, with resection of necrotic bowel if this fails. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
3. Hirschsprung enterocolitis: stop feedings and treat as sepsis with intravenous antibiotics, hydration, and possible surgery; rectal biopsy confirms the diagnosis. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
4. Pyloric stenosis: correct the hypochloremic hypokalemic metabolic alkalosis with intravenous fluids and electrolytes before pyloromyotomy. [ROSH]
5. Tracheoesophageal fistula: echocardiography before surgery to detect associated congenital heart disease, since a right-sided aortic arch changes the approach, plus laryngoscopy and bronchoscopy; a neonate who becomes shocked as the ductus closes needs prostaglandins to maintain patency. [PEER-PH]

### Abdominal compartment syndrome

1. Suspect it in the patient with large-volume resuscitation, burns, pancreatitis, or trauma who develops a tight abdomen, hypotension, tachycardia, oliguria, or hypoxia. Normal intra-abdominal pressure is 5–7 mm Hg and sustained pressure above 12 mm Hg defines intra-abdominal hypertension. [PEER-PH]
2. Measure bladder pressure with a Foley catheter and involve the surgical or trauma service; decompress with a nasogastric tube, sedation and analgesia, neuromuscular blockade such as rocuronium, vasopressors for perfusion, emergent bedside paracentesis for tense ascites, and surgical decompression. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Bariatric and ostomy complications

| Presentation | Interpretation and action |
|---|---|
| Flushing, palpitations, and colicky pain soon after eating months after Roux-en-Y bypass | Dumping syndrome; smaller more frequent meals |
| Ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, and confusion with weeks of vomiting after Roux-en-Y bypass | Thiamine deficiency; give thiamine |
| Symptoms unchanged after gastric band deflation | Band slippage rather than overinflation |
| Dislodged gastrostomy tube, no same-size replacement available | Place a Foley catheter of the same French size to maintain patency |

### Diarrhea and colitis

- Acute diarrhea of 14 days or less in an immunocompetent host is usually infectious and self-limited; norovirus is the most common cause overall at 26% of emergency department diarrhea cases. Test stool when there are at least three unformed stools in 24 hours plus risk factors for Clostridioides difficile: healthcare exposure, age 65 or older, and antibiotics within the preceding 10 weeks. Treat with oral vancomycin. [PEER-CS][ROSH]
- Salmonella in an immunocompetent patient needs only reassurance and oral hydration; Yersinia enterocolitica ileocolitis mimics appendicitis with right lower quadrant tenderness and a normal appendix on CT; staphylococcal food poisoning from a preformed heat-stable enterotoxin in potato salad, egg salad, mayonnaise dishes, or meats presents rapidly after ingestion. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Giardiasis follows contaminated water exposure and causes frequent small-volume stools with gassy bloating, treated with tinidazole; amebiasis after travel causes bloody diarrhea with right upper quadrant pain and hepatic abscess, treated with metronidazole plus paromomycin. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Diverticulitis without peritoneal signs or abscess: amoxicillin-clavulanate with a clear liquid diet advanced as tolerated and primary care follow-up. Fulminant colitis with toxic megacolon (transverse colon over 6 cm, loss of haustrations, thumbprinting) requires an abdominal radiograph first, then urgent surgical evaluation, and peritonitis mandates exploratory surgery. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Gonococcal proctitis is treated with ceftriaxone 500 mg intramuscularly plus doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 7 days, herpetic proctitis with acyclovir, and radiation proctitis with sucralfate enemas. [ROSH]
- Fournier gangrene requires piperacillin-tazobactam, vancomycin, and clindamycin for polymicrobial coverage including Pseudomonas, MRSA, and anaerobes, with clindamycin added to suppress exotoxin production; antibiotics must not delay surgical consultation, because early aggressive debridement is the only definitive treatment. [PEER-CS]
