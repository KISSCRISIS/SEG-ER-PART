## High-Yield Exam Review

- Platelets below 50,000/mm3 in an actively bleeding patient require platelet transfusion; the risk of further haemorrhage rises below this level. [PEER-PH]
- First step for any suspected acute transfusion reaction: stop the transfusion, keep the intravenous line open with normal saline, then reassess. [PEER-PH]
- Acute haemolytic reaction: fever, rigors, low back pain, hypotension and tachycardia roughly 30 minutes into the transfusion; usually ABO incompatibility from a human error in specimen collection, testing or patient identification. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Direct antiglobulin (Coombs) testing turns positive and haptoglobin falls in haemolytic transfusion reactions. [ROSH]
- Febrile nonhaemolytic reaction is the most common transfusion reaction: recipient antibodies against donor leukocytes plus cytokines released during storage; acetaminophen is the treatment. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Allergic (urticarial) reaction: pruritus and hives without hypotension or wheeze; diphenhydramine, and the transfusion may continue without further workup. [PEER-PH]
- Anaphylactic transfusion reaction with angioedema, hives and respiratory distress points to IgA deficiency. [ROSH]
- Transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI): acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure with bilateral infiltrates and a normal cardiac silhouette within 6 hours of transfusion, accompanied by fever and hypotension; management is supportive, often with positive pressure ventilation. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO): hypoxia, hypertension and pulmonary oedema from volume overload; risk factors are advanced age, heart failure, female sex, low body weight and rapid or large-volume transfusion. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- High fever favours TRALI over TACO. [ROSH]
- Transfusion-associated hypocalcaemia from citrate: circumoral numbness, muscle cramps, Chvostek sign, carpopedal spasm and QTc prolongation; give intravenous calcium and monitor ionised calcium. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Massive transfusion is ≥10 units of packed red blood cells within 24 hours; a 1:1:1 ratio of platelets, red cells and fresh frozen plasma reduces coagulopathy, and warmed blood corrects hypothermia-driven coagulopathy. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- One unit of packed red blood cells raises haemoglobin by about 1 g/dL and haematocrit by 3% in adults. [ROSH]
- Transfusion-transmitted HIV, hepatitis B or hepatitis C risk is approximately 1 in 1,000,000 with modern nucleic acid screening. [PEER-PH]
- Haemophilia A is factor VIII deficiency and haemophilia B (Christmas disease) is factor IX deficiency; both are X-linked and give a normal PT with a prolonged aPTT. [ROSH]
- Factor VIII dose = weight (kg) × 0.5 × desired percentage rise; an 80 kg patient needs 2,000 U to reach 50% activity. [PEER-PH]
- Life-threatening or CNS bleeding in haemophilia A: 50 units/kg (IU/kg) of factor VIII to reach approximately 100% activity, given before head CT is obtained. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Haemophilia B with head trauma: 100 IU/kg of factor IX. [ROSH]
- Haemarthrosis is treated to a factor activity of 40–50%. [PEER-PH]
- Avoid intramuscular injections in haemophilia; uncontrolled muscle bleeding can cause compartment syndrome. [PEER-PH]
- von Willebrand disease is the most common congenital bleeding disorder; type 1 is the mildest and most common, type 3 is an absolute lack of von Willebrand factor. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Desmopressin treats minor bleeding in von Willebrand disease types 1 and 2; type 3 requires factor VIII and von Willebrand factor concentrate. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Aspirin is avoided in von Willebrand disease. [PEER-PH]
- Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC): low platelets, low fibrinogen, prolonged PT and aPTT, high D-dimer; the only intervention that stops progression is treatment of the underlying illness. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Cryoprecipitate or fibrinogen concentrate is given in DIC with active bleeding and hypofibrinogenaemia. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Infection is the most common cause of acute DIC. [PEER-PH]
- Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP): microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia with schistocytes plus thrombocytopenia, with normal PT, aPTT and fibrinogen; treat with plasma exchange. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Sickle cell acute chest syndrome: new infiltrate plus fever, cough, chest pain or hypoxia; oxygen, hydration, antibiotics and transfusion to a haemoglobin above 10 g/dL. [ROSH]
- Parvovirus B19 causes the aplastic crisis of sickle cell disease — a precipitous haemoglobin fall with a low reticulocyte count; exchange transfusion is used for acute ischaemic stroke. [ROSH]
- Febrile neutropenia: single temperature ≥38.3°C or ≥38°C sustained for 1 hour with an absolute neutrophil count (ANC) below 1,000–1,500/µL; give empiric cefepime covering *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

## Core Concepts

### Transfusion reactions — the organising framework

- Classify by mechanism: immune-haemolytic (ABO incompatibility), nonhaemolytic febrile, allergic/anaphylactic, pulmonary (TRALI, TACO) and metabolic (citrate hypocalcaemia, hyperkalaemia, hypothermia).
- Any fever during a transfusion is treated as a possible acute haemolytic reaction until excluded; the transfusion stops first and the blood product and patient identification are rechecked. [PEER-PH]
- Nonhaemolytic febrile reactions are benign and recur in patients exposed to multiple antigens through frequent transfusion. [ROSH]
- Allergic reactions span a spectrum: mild urticaria and pruritus are type I IgE-mediated reactions to donor plasma proteins and occur in up to 1–3% of transfusions, particularly with plasma-containing products such as platelets and fresh frozen plasma; anaphylaxis is a multisystem diagnosis. [ROSH]
- TRALI and TACO are the two pulmonary reactions and are distinguished clinically: TRALI is noncardiogenic permeability oedema with fever and hypotension, TACO is hydrostatic oedema with hypertension. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- TRALI is a leading cause of transfusion-related mortality and complicates roughly 1 in 5,000 transfused components. [ROSH]

### Bleeding disorders

- Congenital: haemophilia A, haemophilia B and von Willebrand disease. Acquired: DIC, liver disease, anticoagulants, immune thrombocytopenia.
- Haemophilia severity tracks the residual factor level: severe disease with activity below 1% presents within the first year of life, while mild disease with 5–40% activity may not bleed until provoked by trauma or surgery. [ROSH]
- von Willebrand factor is made and released by vascular endothelium; it supports platelet adhesion and carries factor VIII, protecting it from degradation. Loss of the carrier function is why von Willebrand disease prolongs the PTT. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Mucosal and skin bleeding dominate von Willebrand disease; deep haemarthroses and muscle bleeds dominate haemophilia. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- In haemophilia, every head injury is treated as a bleeding episode regardless of symptoms or imaging. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Thrombocytopenia — a mechanism-based approach

- Work through four buckets: reduced production (marrow failure, chemotherapy, aplastic anaemia, leukaemia), increased consumption (DIC, TTP, HUS), increased destruction (immune thrombocytopenia, heparin-induced thrombocytopenia) and sequestration (hypersplenism, splenic sequestration crisis).
- Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is an acquired immune-mediated thrombocytopenia in which autoantibodies against platelet membrane glycoproteins form antibody-platelet complexes that are cleared by the spleen; it is primary when no underlying condition is found. [ROSH]
- ITP in children typically follows a viral illness, presents in a previously well child with sudden petechiae and purpura, and usually resolves spontaneously within 6 months; adult ITP is more often chronic. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Spontaneous bleeding risk climbs below a platelet count of 20,000/µL and especially below 10,000/µL; acute altered mental status at these counts demands urgent noncontrast head CT to exclude intracranial haemorrhage. [PEER-PH]
- Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia is a consumptive, prothrombotic state: a platelet fall of 50% or more, characteristically 5–14 days after heparin exposure, occurs in up to 7% of treated patients, with thrombotic events in 30–50%. [PEER-PH]
- Thrombocytopenia in pregnancy has a broad differential and requires full laboratory evaluation rather than an assumption of a pregnancy-specific cause. [PEER-PH]

### Anticoagulation reversal — the emergency framework

- Warfarin blocks synthesis of factors II, VII, IX and X; the INR gauges its effect, with a usual therapeutic goal of 2.0–3.0 for atrial fibrillation and venous thromboembolism. [PEER-PH]
- Life-threatening or intracranial bleeding on warfarin is reversed with four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate (factors II, VII, IX and X) plus vitamin K; vitamin K alone acts only after 4–6 hours and is insufficient for emergency reversal, and the goal is an INR below 1.4. [ROSH]
- Dabigatran, a direct thrombin inhibitor, is reversed by idarucizumab, a monoclonal antibody fragment that binds it with high affinity. [ROSH]
- Apixaban, rivaroxaban and edoxaban are factor Xa inhibitors, non-inferior to warfarin for long-term anticoagulation; reversal in life-threatening bleeding is complex and includes four-factor prothrombin complex concentrate. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Over-anticoagulation without bleeding is managed by holding the next dose and treating the bleeding source rather than reflexive reversal. [ROSH]

### Microangiopathic haemolytic anaemias

- DIC, TTP and haemolytic-uraemic syndrome (HUS) all injure red cells through microthrombi, producing schistocytes, anaemia, raised indirect bilirubin and reticulocytosis. [PEER-PH]
- TTP is driven by acquired or congenital deficiency of the von Willebrand factor-cleaving protease ADAMTS13, causing accumulation of ultra-large von Willebrand factor multimers and platelet-rich microthrombi. [ROSH]
- HUS typically follows *Escherichia coli* bloody diarrhoea in children 5–10 days later, with Shiga toxin activating platelets and renovascular cells. [PEER-PH]
- The five features of the TTP pentad — thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, fever, renal impairment and neurological change — are present together in only a minority; diagnosis rests on microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia plus thrombocytopenia without another explanation. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Sickle cell disease

- Disease complications cluster by age: dactylitis in infants 6 months to 2 years, aplastic crisis, splenic sequestration and ischaemic stroke in children, and haemorrhagic stroke and subarachnoid haemorrhage in adults, usually in their twenties. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Acute chest syndrome is the leading cause of death; infection is the most common precipitant, with fat emboli and lung infarction also implicated. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Functional asplenia develops by about age 5 through repeated microinfarction, leaving patients vulnerable to encapsulated organisms. [PEER-PH]
- Aplastic crisis and splenic sequestration both drop the haemoglobin abruptly; the reticulocyte count is suppressed in aplastic crisis and elevated in sequestration. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Anaemia by red cell size

- Iron deficiency is the most common cause of anaemia overall, most often from chronic blood loss, and new iron deficiency in an adult prompts evaluation for occult malignancy or gastrointestinal bleeding. [PEER-PH]
- Thalassaemia is the classic microcytic anaemia with normal ferritin, normal-to-high iron and a normal reticulocyte count; iron deficiency shows the opposite iron studies. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Megaloblastic anaemia from vitamin B12 or folate deficiency raises the mean corpuscular volume above 100 fL; the most common cause is vitamin B12 deficiency. [ROSH]
- Aplastic anaemia is pancytopenia with a reduced reticulocyte count, most commonly iatrogenic from chemotherapy or radiation. [PEER-PH]

### Oncological emergencies

- The four time-critical syndromes are febrile neutropenia, hyperleukocytosis with leukostasis, tumour lysis syndrome and hypercalcaemia of malignancy, plus cord and vena cava compression.
- Leukaemia is the most common childhood cancer, accounting for 29% of paediatric cancers; acute myeloid leukaemia carries the highest complication rate and the highest risk of hyperleukocytosis. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Hyperleukocytosis is a white cell count above 100,000/µL; leukostasis is the symptomatic end-organ consequence and carries a mortality rate up to 40%. [ROSH]
- Tumour lysis syndrome follows rapid breakdown of bulky, chemosensitive tumours — acute leukaemias, high-grade non-Hodgkin lymphomas and Burkitt lymphoma — and is biochemically defined by hyperkalaemia, hyperphosphataemia, hypocalcaemia and hyperuricaemia. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
