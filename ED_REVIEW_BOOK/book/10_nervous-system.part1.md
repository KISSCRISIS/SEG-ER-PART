## High-Yield Exam Review

- Ischemic stroke is the most common stroke type in adults; anterior circulation ischemia predominates and the middle cerebral artery (MCA) is the vessel most commonly affected [ROSH].
- Noncontrast CT of the head is the first-line study in suspected acute stroke and is the most sensitive and specific test for hemorrhagic stroke [ROSH].
- Noncontrast CT detects subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) with 98–100% sensitivity within 6 hours of onset; after that window a negative CT is followed by CT angiography (CTA) of the head and neck [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Fingerstick blood glucose is the first step in any suspected stroke — hypoglycemia reproduces focal deficits and is rapidly reversible [ROSH][PEER-CS].
- Intravenous thrombolysis is used to 4.5 hours in selected patients. Contraindications include ischemic stroke within 3 months, prior intracranial hemorrhage, gastrointestinal hemorrhage within 21 days, and multilobar infarction or hypodensity of more than one-third of a hemisphere [ROSH].
- Mechanical thrombectomy improves outcomes in large-vessel occlusion presenting 6–24 hours after onset, and 6–16 hours in selected patients [PEER-CS].
- Elevated troponin is associated with increased mortality in acute ischemic stroke [PEER-PH].
- The NIH Stroke Scale underestimates posterior circulation strokes [PEER-PH].
- Hypertensive vasculopathy is the leading cause of spontaneous intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH), and the putamen is the most common site [ROSH].
- In aneurysmal SAH, nimodipine improves mortality; systolic blood pressure is kept below 160 mm Hg and mean arterial pressure (MAP) below 110 mm Hg [ROSH][PEER-CS].
- Cluster headache: 100% oxygen by nonrebreather mask at 12–15 L/min for 15 minutes is first-line; subcutaneous sumatriptan 6 mg is second-line; verapamil prevents recurrence [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Tension-type headache is the most common primary headache: dull, bilateral, band-like, without photophobia, phonophobia, or focal deficit [PEER-PH].
- Migraine aura is the most specific feature of migraine; migraine without aura is the most common type [ROSH].
- Jaw claudication is the feature most highly associated with a positive temporal artery biopsy in giant cell arteritis; erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) and C-reactive protein are the initial tests, and intravenous methylprednisolone is given when vision is threatened [ROSH].
- Idiopathic intracranial hypertension (IIH) affects obese women aged 20–44 years, causes papilledema with pulsatile tinnitus and transient visual loss, and is treated first-line with acetazolamide 250 mg twice daily [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Status epilepticus is seizure activity beyond 5 minutes, or recurrent seizures without return to baseline; first-line therapy is lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg IV to a maximum of 4 mg [PEER-CS][ROSH].
- Midazolam 10 mg IM is as safe and effective as intravenous lorazepam for terminating out-of-hospital seizures when IV access is unavailable [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- After benzodiazepines, fosphenytoin or levetiracetam is the appropriate second agent; phenytoin is a sodium channel antagonist and is ineffective for seizures caused by tricyclic antidepressant overdose [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Isoniazid ingestion causes seizures refractory to standard therapy; pyridoxine is given together with benzodiazepines [ROSH].
- Simple febrile seizure: generalized, under 15 minutes, a single episode in 24 hours, in a child 6 months to 5 years old. Complex features are duration over 15 minutes, focal onset, more than one episode in 24 hours, and age under 6 months [ROSH].
- Bacterial meningitis: ceftriaxone plus vancomycin empirically; ampicillin is added for patients over 50 years, under 3 months, or immunocompromised; dexamethasone is given with or before the first antibiotic dose [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Antibiotics are never delayed for CT or lumbar puncture [PEER-PH][PEER-CS].
- Head CT before lumbar puncture is indicated for focal neurologic deficit, altered mental state, head trauma, new-onset seizures, papilledema, or immunocompromise [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Chemoprophylaxis for close contacts of meningococcal meningitis is rifampin, ciprofloxacin, or ceftriaxone [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS): progressive weakness with reduced or absent reflexes, albuminocytologic dissociation on cerebrospinal fluid (CSF), treatment with immune globulin or plasmapheresis; succinylcholine is contraindicated [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Myasthenia gravis is caused by autoantibodies against nicotinic acetylcholine receptors; a negative inspiratory force (NIF) of −30 cm H2O warrants critical care admission, and a NIF of −20 cm H2O with CO2 retention warrants intubation [PEER-PH].
- Back pain is the most common symptom of spinal epidural abscess; C-reactive protein is the most sensitive laboratory test, and MRI of the entire spine is the diagnostic study of choice [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Cauda equina syndrome is a surgical emergency: urinary retention with post-void residual greater than 200 mL, saddle anesthesia, and bowel incontinence [PEER-CS].
- Acute dystonic reactions are treated with benztropine 2 mg IM or diphenhydramine 25–50 mg; laryngeal dystonia is potentially life-threatening [PEER-PH].
- The normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH) triad is gait disturbance, urinary incontinence, and cognitive decline; gait disturbance is the earliest and most treatment-responsive feature [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Delirium is distinguished from dementia by its fluctuating course; the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) and the Delirium Triage Screen are the bedside tools [PEER-PH][ROSH].

## Core Concepts

### Headache Syndromes

- Four primary headaches dominate emergency practice: tension-type, migraine, cluster, and the secondary headaches that mimic them.
- Secondary headaches that must be actively excluded are SAH, giant cell arteritis, IIH, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis (CVST), intracranial mass lesion, carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning, and shunt failure.
- Red flags that move a headache out of the primary category: thunderclap onset, onset during exertion or sexual activity, posture-dependent pain, fever or meningismus, focal deficit, papilledema, and multiple household members affected.
- Cluster headache is defined by timing and autonomic features, not by pain alone: attacks of 15 minutes to 3 hours, clustered over days to weeks, often at night and at the same time of day, with ipsilateral lacrimation, rhinorrhea, conjunctival injection, miosis, ptosis, and facial sweating.
- Migraine is differentiated by pulsatile quality, nausea, photophobia, and phonophobia; cluster headache patients are restless and pace, while migraine patients prefer a dark, quiet room.

### Cerebrovascular Syndromes

- Vascular territory predicts the examination: anterior cerebral artery (ACA) infarction causes contralateral leg weakness and numbness; MCA infarction causes contralateral hemiparesis, hemisensory loss, aphasia, and gaze deviation toward the lesion; posterior circulation events cause vertigo, ataxia, diplopia, dysarthria, and dysphagia.
- Roughly 90% of strokes are ischemic and 10% are hemorrhagic; noncontrast CT separates them and directs every subsequent decision [PEER-CS].
- Transient ischemic attack (TIA) is transient focal neurologic dysfunction without infarction; symptoms usually last under 30 minutes and are negative phenomena — loss of function rather than positive phenomena such as flashes or jerking. This distinction separates TIA from migraine, seizure, and syncope [ROSH].
- Stroke risk after TIA is highest in the first 2 days [PEER-PH].
- ICH is typically deep and hypertensive; SAH is typically aneurysmal, arising from saccular aneurysms at the circle of Willis, most often between ages 40 and 60, with smoking, hypertension, alcohol, and sympathomimetic drugs as risk factors [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Cerebral venous thrombosis is a separate mechanism: venous outflow obstruction causes intracranial hypertension, venous infarction, and sometimes hemorrhage.

### Seizures and Status Epilepticus

- Seizures are primary when no cause is found and secondary when a structural or metabolic cause exists — mass lesion, hemorrhage, infection, hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, or drug effect.
- The clinical features that separate a seizure from a nonepileptic attack are abrupt onset and termination, lack of recall, purposeless movement, a postictal period of confusion and lethargy, urinary incontinence, and lateral tongue biting.
- A serum prolactin level drawn within 15–20 minutes of the event rises to twice baseline or more after a generalized tonic-clonic or complex partial seizure, and normalizes within about an hour; it is a supportive test, not a rule-out [ROSH].
- Status epilepticus is a time-dependent emergency: the longer the seizure continues, the more refractory it becomes, and benzodiazepines are frequently underdosed [PEER-CS].
- Nonconvulsive status epilepticus is suspected when a patient has an unexpectedly prolonged postictal period or persistent altered mentation despite adequate observation time, and it is confirmed with electroencephalography (EEG) [PEER-CS].

### CNS Infection

- Meningitis and encephalitis are separated clinically by neurologic dysfunction: encephalitis adds focal weakness, seizures, personality change, psychiatric symptoms, or altered mental status, and altered mental status is the most specific presentation of viral encephalitis in children [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Bacterial meningitis is an emergency in which outcome is tied to time to antibiotics; cultures are desirable but never a reason to wait [PEER-CS].
- The empiric regimen is chosen by age and host, because the responsible organisms change across the lifespan: neonates, older adults, and the immunocompromised each require additional coverage.
- Adjunctive dexamethasone improves unfavorable outcomes when given with or just before the first antibiotic dose, with the greatest effect in pneumococcal disease [PEER-PH][PEER-CS].
- Fungal meningitis is a consideration in HIV/AIDS, hematologic malignancy, transplant, and other immunosuppressed states; Cryptococcus neoformans is the most common agent [ROSH].

### The Spinal Cord as a Compartment

- The cord and cauda equina are enclosed structures: expanding lesions cause pain first, then sensory change, then weakness, then sphincter loss.
- The compressive emergencies are spinal epidural abscess, spinal epidural hematoma, and metastatic epidural cord compression; all require urgent MRI and, for abscess and hematoma, urgent surgical decompression.
- Transverse myelitis is the inflammatory mimic: rapid progression of motor weakness and sensory loss with paresthesias, progressing to urinary or fecal incontinence, associated with autoimmune disease, infection, paraneoplastic syndromes, and rarely vaccination [PEER-PH].

### Neuromuscular Transmission and Peripheral Nerve

- Weakness that worsens with use and improves with rest is a neuromuscular junction pattern: myasthenia gravis is postsynaptic (antibodies against nicotinic acetylcholine receptors), while Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome is presynaptic and improves with continued stimulation, and is associated with a perihilar mass [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Ascending, symmetric, areflexic weakness is the GBS pattern; reflexes are typically reduced or absent and respiratory muscles may be involved [ROSH].
- Ascending flaccid paralysis with ataxia after outdoor exposure is tick paralysis, in which the diagnostic and therapeutic step is a complete skin and hair examination to find and remove the tick [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Acute flaccid myelitis is an acquired disorder of motor neurons in brainstem and spinal cord following viral infection, occurring in late summer and early autumn and most commonly affecting children aged 7–11 years [ROSH].

### Disorders of Consciousness

- Delirium is acute, fluctuating, and attentional; dementia is gradual and progressive; NPH is the treatable triad of gait disturbance, incontinence, and cognitive decline [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Transient global amnesia is isolated anterograde amnesia with repetitive questioning, no focal deficit, and no disorientation to self; it resolves within 24 hours, on average within 6 hours, and physical exertion is the most common precipitant [PEER-CS].
- Withdrawal states, toxic ingestions, and metabolic derangements are reversible causes of altered mental status that must be actively sought before a primary neurologic diagnosis is accepted.
