## Pathophysiology

### Ultraviolet injury and cutaneous carcinogenesis

- Ultraviolet (UV) light is the dominant carcinogenic exposure in skin cancer. It is thought to contribute to 90% of melanoma cases in the United States; UV emitted by tanning beds can be fifteen times stronger than midday sun. [PEER-PH]
- Melanoma is a malignancy of melanocytes, arising de novo in about 70% of cases and from dysplastic nevi in the remaining 30%. Because outcome depends on vertical growth into the dermis, Breslow depth of invasion is the strongest prognostic variable for localized disease. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Kaposi sarcoma is a vascular tumour driven by human herpesvirus 8 (HHV-8) in advanced HIV-related immunosuppression; it is an AIDS-defining illness associated with CD4 counts below 200 and tracks skin tension lines as it spreads through dermal planes. [PEER-PH]

### Bacterial invasion of skin and soft tissue

- Skin and soft tissue infection begins when bacteria penetrate the superficial skin barrier. Cellulitis is infection of the deep dermis and subcutaneous adipose tissue; erysipelas is infection of the upper dermis and superficial lymphatics. [ROSH]
- Abscesses are collections of pus in the dermis and deeper layers, most often caused by *Staphylococcus aureus*. Furuncles and carbuncles are abscess forms arising from infected hair follicles in areas of friction and perspiration — axillae, buttocks, back of the neck, and face. [ROSH]
- Necrotizing soft tissue infection results from microbial invasion of skin, fascia, and muscle that spreads rapidly across poorly vascularized fascial planes; this is why it progresses over hours and why antibiotics alone cannot clear it. The underlying muscle is often spared because its robust vascular supply resists the advancing infection. [ROSH]
- Type 1 necrotizing infection accounts for approximately 55–75% of cases and is polymicrobial, combining gram-positive cocci, gram-negative rods, and anaerobes — the pattern typical of diabetes and immunosuppression. Type 2 accounts for 20–30% and is monomicrobial, most often group A *Streptococcus* or methicillin-resistant *S. aureus* (MRSA). *Clostridioides* species have become uncommon with improved sanitation. [ROSH]
- Fournier gangrene is a necrotizing fasciitis of the perineum that includes female labial and perineal disease. Diabetes and excessive alcohol use are the most common predisposing factors, with extremes of age, malignancy, chronic steroid use, cytotoxic drugs, malnutrition, drug use, HIV, and other immunocompromise also contributing. [ROSH]
- Omphalitis develops because the necrotic umbilical stump becomes a nidus colonized from the maternal genital tract or the environment; immature neonatal immunity permits rapid spread into surrounding tissue. [ROSH]

### Exotoxin-mediated epidermal injury

- Staphylococcal scalded skin syndrome (SSSS) is caused by exfoliative toxins A and B produced by *S. aureus* at a distant focus — pharyngitis, cellulitis, or otitis media — that reach the skin by hematogenous spread. [PEER-PH]
- The exfoliative toxins cleave desmoglein 1 in the upper epidermis, producing a split high within the superficial epithelium with superficial peeling, blistering, crusting, and a positive Nikolsky sign. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Bullous impetigo is caused by the same exfoliative toxins, but infection is local rather than hematogenous; progression from bullous impetigo to SSSS is recognized. [PEER-PH]
- Risk of SSSS rises when circulating exotoxin levels are high. Kidney disease reduces exotoxin excretion, while immunodeficiency or an overwhelming staphylococcal infection increases production — which explains cases in older children and adults. [PEER-PH]
- In necrotizing soft tissue infection, exotoxin production is one of the key components driving extensive tissue damage, which is why an antitoxin agent is added to the antimicrobial regimen. [PEER-PH]

### Autoimmune blistering disease

- Pemphigus vulgaris is an intraepidermal blistering disease. IgG autoantibodies target desmoglein-3 and sometimes desmoglein-1, cadherin-type transmembrane glycoproteins that maintain keratinocyte adhesion; the resulting loss of cell-cell adhesion (acantholysis) produces flaccid, fragile bullae and a positive Nikolsky sign. [ROSH]
- Mucosal involvement reflects desmoglein-3 expression in stratified squamous mucosa and skin. It occurs in almost all patients and often precedes cutaneous disease, with oral lesions preceding skin lesions in up to 60% of patients. Infections, medications including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors, and emotional stress are triggers; genetic susceptibility is the most common precipitating factor. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Bullous pemphigoid is a subepidermal blistering disease caused by IgG autoantibody and complement deposition at the epithelial basement membrane zone, targeting the hemidesmosomal proteins bullous pemphigoid antigen 180 and antigen 230. Because the cleavage plane is deeper, blisters are tense and the Nikolsky sign is negative. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Bullous pemphigoid may be provoked by repeated skin trauma or coexisting inflammatory skin disease such as psoriasis, but it is often idiopathic. It is largely a disease of older adults, most presenting between ages 60 and 80. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Severe cutaneous adverse reactions

- Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS) and toxic epidermal necrolysis (TEN) are a spectrum of one disease: an immune complex–mediated hypersensitivity reaction with autoimmune detachment of the epidermis and mucous membranes. SJS is defined by full-thickness skin necrosis over less than 10% of body surface area (BSA), SJS/TEN overlap by 10–30%, and TEN by more than 30%. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Most SJS and TEN is medication-related. Implicated drugs include sulfa antibiotics such as trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, penicillin antibiotics, allopurinol, lamotrigine and other anticonvulsants, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), and oxicam anti-inflammatories. Malignancy and HIV infection are also causes. [ROSH]
- Infectious causes of SJS/TEN are more likely in children and younger adults; the most common are *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* and herpes simplex virus (HSV). [ROSH]
- Ruptured bullae and desquamation denude the skin and risk secondary infection, the leading cause of death. SJS can also affect the kidneys, liver, and lungs. [ROSH]
- Drug-induced hypersensitivity syndrome (DIHS), also called drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), is a T cell–mediated type IV hypersensitivity reaction associated with specific human leukocyte antigen polymorphisms; reactivation of human herpesvirus 6, Epstein-Barr virus, or cytomegalovirus is believed to contribute. Mortality exceeds 10%. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Hypersensitivity mechanisms in skin disease

- Allergic contact dermatitis is a delayed type IV hypersensitivity reaction: CD4+ T lymphocytes recognize antigen 48 to 72 hours after exposure and trigger a cell-mediated response, which is why the eruption follows exposure by hours to days. [PEER-PH]
- Urticaria is an IgE-mediated type 1 hypersensitivity reaction: antigen cross-linking degranulates cutaneous mast cells, releasing histamine and other vasodilatory substances that cause localized swelling and pruritus. [ROSH]
- Erythema multiforme is an immune-mediated cutaneous disorder in which macules and papules evolve over 24–48 hours into target lesions with a dusky centre and a halo of erythema. Reactivated HSV and *Mycoplasma pneumoniae* are the most common known causes. [ROSH]
- Erythema nodosum is a delayed-type hypersensitivity reaction of the dermis and adjacent subcutaneous fat, probably involving immune complex deposition and recruitment of inflammatory mediators; it is often a marker of an underlying systemic disease. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Fixed drug eruption is a cutaneous delayed hypersensitivity reaction defined by lesions that recur at the same anatomical site each time the drug is re-exposed. Hypersensitivity vasculitis (serum sickness and serum sickness–like reaction) is small-vessel inflammation triggered by an antigen, typically a beta-lactam antibiotic, with symptoms developing 7–10 days after exposure. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Barrier dysfunction, keratinization, and infection

- Atopic dermatitis arises primarily from epidermal barrier dysfunction in the stratum corneum. An altered stratum corneum increases permeability and water loss, producing dry skin and intense pruritus; abnormal humoral and cell-mediated immunity contribute, but the exact mechanism is unknown. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Diaper dermatitis is caused and exacerbated by overhydration of the skin from prolonged contact with urine and feces. When moisture is chronic, *Candida albicans* proliferates, producing a beefy erythematous rash concentrated in the skin folds with satellite lesions. [ROSH]
- Psoriasis is an autoimmune condition involving CD4 and CD8 T cells, Th17 cells, and many chemical mediators, and is associated with human leukocyte antigen B27. It is relapsing and remitting, with physiologic stressors such as infection or trauma acting as triggers. Removal of the silvery scale exposes punctate bleeding (Auspitz sign). [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Tinea is a dermatophyte infection of the keratinous layers of skin. Tinea capitis differs from other tinea infections because it involves both skin and hair shafts, which is why topical therapy fails and oral therapy is required; a kerion is the severe inflammatory, suppurative response. Tinea versicolor is instead a superficial infection by *Malassezia furfur*. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Molluscum contagiosum is caused by a large double-stranded DNA poxvirus producing localized epidermal infection; it spreads by direct contact or autoinoculation, favouring intertriginous areas. [ROSH]
- Scabies is infestation by the female *Sarcoptes scabiei* mite, which burrows into the skin and extends the burrow a few millimeters every 24 hours while laying eggs. The intense pruritus is a delayed hypersensitivity reaction to the mite, its feces, and its eggs, which is why pruritus takes 3–6 weeks to develop on first infestation. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Vascular, vasculitic, and coagulopathic purpura

- Immunoglobulin A vasculitis (IgAV, formerly Henoch-Schönlein purpura) is an immune-mediated small-vessel vasculitis with IgA-dominant immune complex deposition producing leukocytoclastic vasculitis. It is often preceded by an upper respiratory infection, and many patients have group A beta-hemolytic streptococci cultured from the oropharynx. Vancomycin, cefuroxime, and angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors are recognized triggers. [ROSH]
- The rash of IgAV begins erythematous and macular and progresses to ecchymoses, petechiae, and palpable purpura. Because it reflects immune complex deposition in dependent vessels, it appears in crops in gravity- and pressure-dependent areas — buttocks, lower extremities, waistband, and the tops of socks. [ROSH]
- In disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), widespread activation of coagulation consumes platelets and clotting factors: fibrinogen falls, D-dimer rises, prothrombin time and activated partial thromboplastin time prolong, thrombocytopenia develops, and factors V and VIII are consumed. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause, most often sepsis. [PEER-PH]
- Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura causes microangiopathic haemolysis, so schistocytes appear on the peripheral blood smear; immune thrombocytopenia does not cause haemolysis. [PEER-PH]

### Venous, arterial, neuropathic, and pressure ulceration

- Chronic venous stasis disease is caused by venous hypertension in the lower extremities from venous valvular incompetence with reflux, venous obstruction, or venous thrombosis. Blood backs up, oedema and stasis dermatitis develop, the skin becomes dry, tight, and hairless with orange-brown hyperpigmentation, and chronic stasis leads to ulceration. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Arterial insufficiency ulcers arise from peripheral arterial disease, a known complication of poorly controlled diabetes. A skin injury fails to heal because perfusion is inadequate, progresses to ulceration, and then becomes infected; infection causes progressive tissue destruction and frequently involves bone, producing osteomyelitis. [PEER-PH]
- Neuropathic ulcers arise because diabetes-related neuropathy prevents perception of tissue damage; they are painless and occur over pressure points, typically punched out within a thick callus on the plantar surface under the first and fifth metatarsal heads, the great toe, and the heel. [ROSH]
- Pressure injury results from external mechanical forces combined with host-specific factors: immobility, malnutrition, decreased skin perfusion, and neurological disorders causing sensory loss. Injury is staged by the depth of tissue involved, and adjacent bone may become involved by contiguous spread. [ROSH]
