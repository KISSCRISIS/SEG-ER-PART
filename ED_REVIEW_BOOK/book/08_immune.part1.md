## High-Yield Exam Review

- Epinephrine is first-line for anaphylaxis and the only medication proven effective; antihistamines and steroids are adjuncts and must never delay it. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Anaphylaxis in an adult: 1:1,000 (1 mg/mL) epinephrine 0.3 mg intramuscularly. A child receives 0.01 mL/kg of 1 mg/mL (1:1,000) intramuscularly. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- The 1:10,000 epinephrine concentration is intended for intravenous use, typically in cardiac arrest. [PEER-PH]
- After exposure to a likely allergen, anaphylaxis is diagnosed when two of the following are present: skin or mucosal involvement, respiratory compromise, persistent gastrointestinal upset, or reduced blood pressure with end-organ malperfusion. [PEER-PH]
- Wheezing indicates respiratory compromise and is what distinguishes anaphylaxis from a non–life-threatening allergic reaction. [PEER-PH]
- Skin is the organ system most often involved in anaphylaxis, affected in nearly 90% of patients. [ROSH]
- Cardiovascular collapse despite two intramuscular epinephrine doses is treated with an intravenous epinephrine infusion; bolus intravenous dosing is avoided because of higher cardiovascular complication risk. [PEER-PH]
- Refractory anaphylaxis in a patient taking a beta-blocker such as atenolol: glucagon 1 mg IV every 5 minutes as needed. [PEER-PH]
- Persistent hypotension in anaphylaxis despite epinephrine is explained by increased vascular permeability with third spacing; aggressive crystalloid resuscitation is instituted early. [PEER-PH]
- A patient receiving a continuous epinephrine infusion requires intensive care unit admission for monitoring. [PEER-PH]
- Angioedema that does not respond to epinephrine and antihistamines is bradykinin-mediated — either ACE-inhibitor induced or hereditary. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- ACE-inhibitor angioedema is managed by stopping the drug and giving supportive care; intubation is the definitive step when the airway is threatened. [ROSH]
- Hereditary angioedema is an autosomal dominant C1 inhibitor deficiency; C1 inhibitor is the specific therapy and fresh frozen plasma is an alternative. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Suspected giant cell arteritis is treated with steroids immediately to prevent irreversible vision loss; biopsy can be performed after steroids are started. [PEER-PH]
- Scleroderma renal crisis is treated with an ACE inhibitor such as captopril; nitroprusside may be added for severe hypertension with CNS effects. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Raynaud phenomenon that persists after rewarming is treated with nifedipine. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Migratory polyarthritis is the most common clinical manifestation of acute rheumatic fever, seen in up to 80% of cases; carditis is the second most common. [PEER-PH]
- Jones criteria: evidence of a preceding streptococcal infection plus either two major criteria, or one major and two minor criteria. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Major Jones criteria are carditis, polyarthritis, chorea, erythema marginatum and subcutaneous nodules. Minor criteria are fever, arthralgia, elevated ESR or CRP, and a prolonged PR interval on ECG. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Every patient with acute rheumatic fever receives antibiotics — penicillin, with penicillin G benzathine preferred; azithromycin, erythromycin or clindamycin for penicillin allergy. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Kawasaki disease: fever for 5 days or more plus four of five findings — bilateral nonexudative conjunctivitis, oral mucous membrane changes, extremity changes, polymorphous nonvesicular rash, and cervical lymphadenopathy. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Kawasaki disease is treated with intravenous immune globulin 2 g/kg over 8–12 hours plus aspirin; IVIG reduces the risk of coronary artery aneurysms from 20–25% to 3–4%. [PEER-PH]
- Coronary artery aneurysms are the most serious complication of Kawasaki disease. [ROSH]
- Poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis follows pharyngitis at 1–3 weeks or a streptococcal skin infection at 3–6 weeks; antistreptolysin O titer is the test of choice to confirm the antecedent infection. [ROSH]
- Reactive arthritis is an asymmetric oligoarthritis appearing 2–6 weeks after urethritis or dysentery; first-line treatment is an NSAID such as indomethacin. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Acute transplant rejection occurs within 6 months of transplantation, is recipient T-cell mediated, and presents with fever, graft site tenderness, decreased urine output, hypertension and a rising creatinine. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- When renal graft rejection is suspected, the first imaging study is renal ultrasound with Doppler. [PEER-PH]
- Cyclosporine and tacrolimus both cause dose-related nephrotoxicity; it is the most common serious side effect of tacrolimus, occurring in 20–40% of solid organ transplant patients. [PEER-PH]
- Graft-versus-host disease occurs most commonly after allogeneic stem cell transplantation and most often affects skin, gastrointestinal tract and liver. [PEER-PH]
- Sarcoidosis hypercalcemia results from macrophage production of calcitriol; the ECG shows a shortened QT interval. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Tuberculosis must be ruled out by pretreatment screening before a tumor necrosis factor-alpha inhibitor is started. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

## Core Concepts

### Classification of hypersensitivity reactions

| Type | Mechanism | Examples in this chapter |
|---|---|---|
| I | IgE-mediated; antigen binds IgE on mast cells and basophils, causing degranulation | Anaphylaxis, urticaria, food and insect-sting allergy [ROSH] |
| II | Antibody directed against host tissue after molecular cross-reactivity | Acute rheumatic fever [ROSH] |
| III | IgG or IgM antibody binds antigen to form soluble immune complexes that deposit and fix complement | Serum sickness, systemic lupus erythematosus, poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis [ROSH][PEER-PH] |
| IV | Delayed, cell-mediated, T-cell driven rather than humoral | Contact dermatitis, acute transplant rejection, fixed drug eruption [ROSH][PEER-PH] |

- Anaphylaxis is usually IgE mediated but can be immune complex or complement mediated; reactions to drugs tend to be immune complex or complement mediated. [ROSH]
- Non-immunologic anaphylaxis results from direct mast cell or basophil degranulation in the absence of immunoglobulins and produces the same rapidly fatal disease process. [ROSH]

### Anaphylaxis and allergic reactions

- Anaphylaxis is a serious allergic reaction that can progress rapidly, involves multiple organ systems (skin or mucosa, respiratory, cardiovascular, gastrointestinal), and may cause death. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Symptoms typically occur within minutes of exposure; urticaria and bronchospasm do not both need to be present for the diagnosis. [ROSH]
- Common triggers are foods, medications, insect stings, latex, exercise and allergen immunotherapy. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Diagnostic criteria: hypotension after allergen exposure; or skin or mucosal involvement with respiratory compromise or hypotension; or two of skin or mucosal involvement, respiratory compromise, gastrointestinal symptoms or hypotension after a suspected allergen. [ROSH]
- Skin and mucosal findings are the most frequent presentation, including urticaria, pruritus, lip and oropharyngeal swelling, periorbital edema and conjunctival injection. [ROSH]
- A localized reaction confined to the site of allergen contact — for example, one side of the tongue and cheek after eating a spice — is treated with antihistamine, steroid and histamine-2 blocker rather than epinephrine. [ROSH]
- Beta-2 agonist action of epinephrine stabilizes mast cells; epinephrine also decreases airway edema and induces bronchodilation and vasoconstriction. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Angioedema

- Angioedema is local extravasation of fluid into interstitial tissues; it affects the face, extremities and genitals, and on the face it involves the lips, mouth, uvula, larynx and pharynx. [ROSH]
- It is characteristically rapid in onset, asymmetric, and not confined to gravity-dependent areas. [ROSH]
- Mast cell–mediated angioedema is part of the allergic spectrum and is accompanied by bronchospasm, itching, flushed skin, hypotension and urticaria; onset is within hours and resolution within 1–2 days. [ROSH]
- Bradykinin-mediated angioedema is caused most commonly by ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers; other causes are aliskiren, thrombolytics and some estrogens such as oral contraceptives. [ROSH]
- Hereditary angioedema is an autosomal dominant disorder manifesting before age 30, with recurrent acute attacks of angioedema without urticaria or pruritus; minor trauma may trigger an attack but often no trigger is identifiable. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Serum C4 level is used to diagnose hereditary angioedema. [ROSH]

### Connective tissue and autoimmune disease at a glance

| Disease | Hallmark | Signature features |
|---|---|---|
| Systemic lupus erythematosus | Multisystem autoimmune disease, considered a type III hypersensitivity reaction | Malar or discoid rash, photosensitivity, oral ulcers, symmetric polyarthritis, serositis, nephritis; ANA highly sensitive, anti-Smith most specific [ROSH] |
| Rheumatoid arthritis | Chronic symmetric inflammatory peripheral polyarthritis | MCP and PIP joint involvement with DIP sparing, morning stiffness, constitutional symptoms, atlantoaxial subluxation, Felty syndrome [ROSH] |
| Systemic sclerosis (scleroderma) | Uncontrolled accumulation of collagen with widespread vascular involvement | Sclerodactyly with Raynaud phenomenon, CREST, tendon friction rubs, gastrointestinal dysmotility, scleroderma renal crisis [ROSH][PEER-PH] |
| Sarcoidosis | Systemic granulomatous disease with noncaseating granulomas | Bilateral hilar lymphadenopathy, lupus pernio, erythema nodosum, uveitis, hypercalcemia [ROSH] |

### Sarcoidosis

- Sarcoidosis is a systemic granulomatous disease of unclear etiology that most often affects the lungs but can involve skin, eyes and the lymphatic system; it is characterised histologically by noncaseating granulomas and most commonly affects people aged 20–40. [ROSH]
- The granulomas are composed of helper T cells and other inflammatory cells such as macrophages. [ROSH]
- Löfgren syndrome is the triad of erythema nodosum, bilateral hilar adenopathy and arthralgia. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Lupus pernio is the most specific cutaneous manifestation, presenting as chronic violaceous indurated plaques and papules on the nose, cheeks, lips and ears. [ROSH]
- Erythema nodosum presents as red nodules on the fronts of the lower legs and is treated supportively with NSAIDs, rest and elevation; corticosteroids are reserved for refractory or severe cases. [PEER-PH]

### Vasculitis by vessel size

| Vessel size | Entity | Discriminating features |
|---|---|---|
| Large | Takayasu arteritis | Aorta and its major branches including subclavian and carotid arteries; young women; diminished upper extremity pulses, unequal arm blood pressures, bruits, hypertension, retinopathy, limb claudication [ROSH][PEER-PH] |
| Large | Giant cell (temporal) arteritis | Superficial branches of the carotid artery, especially the superficial temporal artery; over 50 years; headache, temporal tenderness, jaw claudication [PEER-PH] |
| Medium | Polyarteritis nodosa | Necrotizing vasculitis of medium-sized arteries; ages 40–50; hepatitis B virus and intravenous drug use; kidneys, skin, gastrointestinal tract and peripheral nerves involved, lungs classically spared [ROSH] |
| Medium | Kawasaki disease | Children under 5 years; predilection for the coronary arteries [PEER-PH][ROSH] |
| Small | IgA vasculitis (Henoch-Schönlein purpura) | Children 2–11 years; skin, gut and kidney; IgA immune deposits in target organs [PEER-PH][ROSH] |

### Post-infectious and post-streptococcal syndromes

- Acute rheumatic fever and poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis are both nonsuppurative sequelae of group A *Streptococcus* infection but differ in latency, target organ and mechanism. [ROSH]
- Acute rheumatic fever results from molecular cross-reactivity between streptococcal and host cell proteins with antibody formation; poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis results from immune complex deposition in the glomerulus. [ROSH]
- Reactive arthritis is a sterile inflammatory arthritis triggered by a distant genitourinary or enteric infection, not by joint infection itself. [ROSH]
- Multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children (MIS-C) is a postinfectious overactive inflammatory response occurring 2–6 weeks after SARS-CoV-2 infection; it shares features with Kawasaki disease but is thought to be a distinct illness. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Transplant immunology

- Rejection occurs when the graft is identified as foreign; injury is mediated through cellular, humoral and inflammatory pathways. [ROSH]
- Graft-versus-host disease is a multisystem condition in which transplanted cells recognise recipient tissue as foreign and incite a T-cell immune response against it. [ROSH]
- Immunosuppression carries a time-dependent infection risk: nosocomial and surgical complications in the first month, opportunistic infection at 1–6 months, and community-acquired infection beyond 6 months. [PEER-PH]

### Drug hypersensitivity eruptions

| Entity | Latency | Key features |
|---|---|---|
| Exanthematous drug eruption | 1–2 weeks after starting a medication | Most common drug eruption; treatment is symptomatic other than stopping the drug [ROSH] |
| Drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS) | Approximately 2–6 weeks | Fever, malaise, lymphadenopathy, skin eruption; facial edema, skin tenderness, blistering, erythroderma or mucositis suggest severe hypersensitivity [ROSH][PEER-PH] |
| Fixed drug eruption | After re-exposure to the offending agent | Fixed lesion recurring in the same area; oval or circular hyperpigmented lesion that may blister or necrose centrally [ROSH] |
| Contact dermatitis | After direct contact | Well-demarcated erythematous pruritic papules, plaques or vesicles, often with linear streaking [ROSH] |
