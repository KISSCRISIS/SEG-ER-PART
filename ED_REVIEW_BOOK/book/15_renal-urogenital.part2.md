## Pathophysiology

### Mechanisms of Acute Kidney Injury

- Prerenal injury reflects decreased renal blood flow. Causes are a hypovolemic state (haemorrhage, diarrhoea, insensible losses) and reduced effective circulating volume (reduced ejection fraction, portal hypertension). Restoring circulating blood volume usually restores kidney function [ROSH].
- Tubular injury is the most common subtype of intrinsic injury: acute tubular necrosis causes necrosis and sloughing of tubular epithelial cells with occlusion of kidney tubules, and is the most common cause of in-hospital AKI.
- Acute interstitial nephritis affects the tubules and interstitium but spares the glomeruli. It results from a hypersensitivity reaction rather than direct drug toxicity, is not dose dependent, and can recur when the same medication is reintroduced [ROSH].
- Postobstructive kidney failure raises pressure within the kidney tubules, impairing glomerular filtration, and is followed by vasoconstriction to the kidney. Relief of the obstruction promptly decreases tubular pressure and the resultant vasoconstriction.
- Exposure to radiocontrast agents is a common cause of hospital-acquired AKI. Kidney perfusion can be decreased for up to 20 hours after exposure [ROSH].
- Pre-existing kidney insufficiency is the greatest risk factor for contrast-associated AKI; patients with a serum creatinine > 1.5 mg/dL are at greatest risk [ROSH].

### Mechanisms of Glomerular Injury

- Poststreptococcal glomerulonephritis is a type III hypersensitivity: IgM-containing immune complexes deposit in membranes, fix complement, and cause tissue damage, lowering the serum C3 level [ROSH].
- PSGN is most often caused by prior group A beta-haemolytic streptococcal pharyngeal or skin infection, including impetigo, which presents as a honey-crusted rash [ROSH].
- Nephrotic syndrome results from a pathologic increase in permeability of the glomerular capillary wall, allowing urinary loss of protein. The resulting hypoproteinemic state decreases intravascular oncotic pressure, so fluid shifts into the extravascular space and causes diffuse swelling [ROSH].
- The same protein loss creates a hypercoagulable state with increased risk of thrombosis in the renal veins, deep veins, and pulmonary vasculature; severe infection is a further risk [PEER-PH].
- Minimal change disease may be preceded by a viral upper respiratory illness. Primary nephrotic syndrome accounts for the vast majority of cases and mainly affects young children; other primary forms are focal segmental glomerulosclerosis and membranous nephropathy [PEER-PH].
- Alport syndrome damages the kidney filtration system through an X-linked gene responsible for collagen and basement membrane formation, producing glomerulonephritis with sensorineural hearing loss [ROSH].
- Goodpasture syndrome is autoimmune: IgG is produced against type IV collagen in the basement membrane of both kidney and lung, which explains the combination of pulmonary and kidney disease [ROSH].

### Haemolytic Uremic Syndrome

- HUS is a multisystem disorder producing microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, consumptive thrombocytopenia, and acute kidney injury [ROSH].
- Shiga toxin damages endothelial cells, especially those in the colon and kidneys. Microthrombi form, producing glomerular thrombotic microangiopathy and cortical necrosis [ROSH].
- Red cells are sheared by microthrombi, generating schistocytes. These clots do not consume clotting factors, so the PT and PTT remain normal despite a low platelet count - the feature that distinguishes HUS from disseminated intravascular coagulation [ROSH].
- Microthrombi in the renal vasculature cause kidney impairment and uremia [ROSH].
- The classic triad occurs in only about 10% of patients. About 90% of cases are typical (diarrhoeal), from Shigella, Shiga toxin-producing E. coli including O157:H7, or Streptococcus pneumoniae; the remaining 10% are atypical, from hereditary complement-mediated disease, malignancies, medications, or pregnancy [ROSH].
- Shiga toxin-producing E. coli is the most frequent cause in children. Neuraminidase-producing S. pneumoniae causes HUS usually alongside pneumonia with or without empyema; it accounts for 5% of HUS but 40% of non-diarrhoeal HUS [ROSH].
- HUS generally occurs in children under 5 years and is one of the most common causes of acute kidney injury in childhood [ROSH].
- Antibiotics may increase release of the toxin and worsen the disease process, which is primarily toxin-mediated [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura has a similar presentation but often occurs in adults with neurologic dysfunction and fever; plasmapheresis is its treatment of choice, although it can also be used in HUS [ROSH].

### Mechanisms of Stone Formation and Obstruction

- Urologic stone formation results from supersaturation of dissolved salts in the urine, which then condense into a solid; calcium oxalate and calcium phosphate calculi predominate [ROSH].
- Struvite stones form in the presence of urea-splitting bacteria such as Proteus, Klebsiella, Providencia, Pseudomonas, and Staphylococcus, and are the most common cause of staghorn calculi. Antibiotic penetration of the stone is poor and the risk of urosepsis persists as long as the stone remains [ROSH].
- Indinavir crystallizes in urine and causes ureteral stones, illustrating a drug-induced mechanism; ceftriaxone, ciprofloxacin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and triamterene behave similarly [PEER-PH].
- As stones move along the genitourinary tract, impaction often occurs. This affects renal blood flow and glomerular filtration rate on the affected side, and renal excretion shifts to the unobstructed side [PEER-PH].
- When a stone obstructs the ureter it causes swelling of the kidney's Gerota fascia, which is the source of flank pain [PEER-PH].

### Mechanisms of Scrotal and Testicular Disease

- Testicular torsion results from an extravaginal or intravaginal defect that allows the testis to twist on the spermatic cord; abnormal anatomy, including a horizontal lie and a long spermatic cord, is the largest risk factor.
- Twisting initially decreases venous return and eventually causes arterial obstruction, producing ischaemia and a necrotic testicle within 4-6 hours. Shortening of the spermatic cord with twisting produces a high-riding testis in a transverse lie [ROSH].
- Inadequate fixation of the lower pole of the testis to the tunica vaginalis is the underlying anatomical defect; the testis or spermatic cord is then at risk of torsion [ROSH].
- The appendix testis, a remnant of the paramesonephric duct on the superior aspect of the testicle, is prone to torsion because of its pedunculated shape, causing ischaemia and eventual infarct [ROSH].
- Epididymitis results from retrograde infection of the vas deferens, causing oedema from passive congestion and inflammation; untreated, it may progress to involve the testis, producing epididymo-orchitis [ROSH].
- Hydrocele is fluid accumulation in the tunica vaginalis. Communicating hydroceles occur when the upper processus vaginalis fails to be obliterated, leaving an open tract between peritoneum and scrotum; the tract is closed in non-communicating hydroceles [ROSH].
- Varicoceles result from incomplete drainage due to high downstream venous pressures with retrograde flow from incompetent venous valve leaflets. They much more commonly occur on the left because of the length of the left spermatic vein and the perpendicular angle at which it enters the renal vein [ROSH].
- Germ cell tumours make up the overwhelming majority of testicular cancers. Because most occur in young men, the largest predisposition is believed to result from in utero changes; cryptorchidism is the most commonly identified risk after inborn genetic error [ROSH].
- Testicular malignancies are usually asymptomatic; pain, when present, is most often caused by intratumoral haemorrhage [ROSH].
- Fournier gangrene begins as a benign infection or simple abscess that becomes virulent, producing microthrombosis of small subcutaneous vessels and gangrene of the overlying skin [ROSH].

### Mechanisms of Priapism and Foreskin Disorders

- Ischemic (low-flow) priapism is the most common form. Impaired relaxation and paralysis of cavernosal smooth muscle cause failure of detumescence; mixed venous blood becomes trapped from a derangement of vasoregulatory mechanisms and endothelial adhesion, creating a compartment syndrome with increasing hypoxia and acidosis [ROSH].
- Non-ischemic (high-flow) priapism is caused by unregulated arterial inflow, usually from a fistula between the cavernosal artery and the corpus cavernosum after penile trauma or instrumentation. There is no ischaemia, and most cases resolve spontaneously within a few days [ROSH].
- In sickle cell disease, low-flow priapism accounts for more than 90% of cases and is a urologic emergency because of the risk of permanent damage to penile tissue [ROSH].
- Paraphimosis occurs when the retracted foreskin cannot be replaced past the penile corona, and venous congestion can progress to arterial compression, urinary obstruction, penile necrosis, and gangrene [PEER-PH].
- Phimosis is due to adhesions between the prepuce and glans that lyse over time, which is why the foreskin of young uncircumcised boys is normally non-retractile.

### Mechanisms of Urinary Infection, Dialysis Complications and Herniation

- Urinary tract infection typically results from ascending bacteria from the urethra. Female patients are more susceptible because of shorter urethral length, and E. coli converts nitrate to nitrite, which underlies the nitrite reaction on dipstick testing [ROSH].
- Acute pyelonephritis is more common in pregnancy because of physiologic urinary tract changes: smooth muscle relaxation, urethral dilation, and pressure on the urinary tract from an enlarging uterus [ROSH].
- Peritoneal dialysis peritonitis most often occurs through bacterial inoculation via the catheter or an exit-site infection, or as secondary bacterial peritonitis from an intra-abdominal infection. PD-associated peritonitis is usually caused by gram-positive skin flora, unlike spontaneous bacterial peritonitis, which is mostly enteric gram-negative organisms [ROSH].
- Hypotension during hemodialysis results from a reduction in circulating intravascular volume combined with the patient's inability to compensate by shifting fluid from the interstitial and intracellular compartments into the vascular space [ROSH].
- Dialysis disequilibrium syndrome is attributed to the rapid removal of urea during hemodialysis, leading to transient cerebral edema; it occurs during or shortly after a session [ROSH].
- Autonomic dysfunction is a prominent feature of the polyneuropathies of advanced chronic kidney disease and is thought to relate to chronic uremia [ROSH].
- Applying local pressure to a bleeding arteriovenous fistula puncture site can occlude the fistula and cause thrombosis, which is why the thrill must be checked and documented afterwards [ROSH].
- Inguinal hernias may be direct or indirect. Direct hernias extrude through a weakened transversalis fascia; indirect hernias pass from the internal to the external inguinal ring through a patent processus vaginalis. The medial border of the indirect tract is the inferior epigastric vessels, which also border direct hernias laterally [ROSH].
- A hernia may be reducible, incarcerated, or strangulated. Strangulation follows incarceration and involves impairment of blood flow - arterial, venous, or both - to the viscus [ROSH].

### Mechanisms of Inherited and Neoplastic Kidney Disease

- Polycystic kidney disease is a genetic disorder in which multiple kidney cysts develop from nonfunctioning renal tubules; cyst formation is driven by excessive angiogenesis and is not limited to the kidneys, also affecting the liver, pancreas, and spleen [PEER-PH].
- The autosomal dominant form requires a mutation in the normal allele before cyst formation occurs. Most cases relate to the PKD1 gene, with PKD2 accounting for a smaller proportion; cyst formation and progression to end-stage kidney disease develop earlier in PKD1-related disease (54.3 years versus 74 years) [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- ADPKD complications include hypertension (typically diastolic), portal hypertension, kidney insufficiency, kidney cyst infection, heart valve infection from abnormal valves, ventral hernia, and cerebral aneurysms [PEER-PH].
- Wilms tumour arises from embryonic kidney tissue and is often associated with mutations in genes regulating kidney development, such as WT1 and WT2 on chromosome 11 [ROSH].
- Renal cell carcinoma symptoms reflect the extent of disease; 25% of patients have metastatic or late-stage local disease once they become symptomatic [ROSH].
- Bladder cancer is driven by environmental carcinogens; cigarette smoking is the most important, with arsenic, chlorine, opium, and industrial chemicals also implicated [ROSH].
