## Pathophysiology

### Atherosclerosis and Plaque Rupture

- Atherosclerosis is the substrate for most acute coronary syndromes and for thrombotic limb ischaemia: obstruction follows plaque rupture or endothelial erosion complicated by thrombotic occlusion, and rupture of a plaque in a previously diseased artery accounts for the large majority of thrombotic limb ischaemia. [PEER-PH]
- Ischaemia may also follow arterial aneurysm or dissection, native-vessel thrombosis in thrombophilia, or traumatic arterial injury and compression. [PEER-PH]
- In NSTEMI, subendocardial ischaemia produces myocardial damage with a troponin rise; complete occlusion produces transmural ischaemia and ST elevation. [PEER-PH]

### Myocardial Ischaemia and Localisation

- ST elevation myocardial infarction represents acute coronary artery occlusion, and hyperacute T waves are the earliest ECG change. [ROSH]
- The right coronary artery supplies the inferior wall and, through a branch, the atrioventricular (AV) node in 70–80% of people, so its occlusion often produces an inferior infarct with transient complete heart block and a narrow QRS. [ROSH]
- Posterior infarction follows occlusion of the posterior descending artery, usually a right coronary branch and less often the left circumflex, and presents with ST depression in V1–V3 with tall R waves and upright T waves rather than ST elevation; it is 15–20% of acute myocardial infarctions. [ROSH]
- Anterior infarction follows left anterior descending (LAD) occlusion with ST elevation in V2–V4; left circumflex occlusion with inferior ST elevation is suggested by ST elevation in V5–V6. [ROSH]
- de Winter pattern — upsloping precordial ST depression with tall symmetric T waves and ST elevation in aVR — predicts acute proximal LAD occlusion; Wellens syndrome is deep inverted or biphasic T waves in V2–V3 indicating critical LAD stenosis and anterior STEMI within 30–90 days. [ROSH]
- Cocaine causes coronary vasospasm and increased myocardial oxygen demand, whereas type 2 myocardial infarction is a supply–demand mismatch from another illness such as pneumonia with hypoxia and tachycardia. [ROSH]

### Post-Infarction Remodelling and Complications

- After infarction the weakened wall bulges during systole and the infarcted muscle becomes fibrotic, producing dyskinesis; true left ventricular aneurysm is full-thickness outpouching during contraction, most often after anterior infarction from total LAD occlusion without collateral circulation. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Well-formed Q or QS waves persist, and ST elevation persists in up to 60% of anterior and 5% of inferior STEMIs from incomplete reperfusion and transmural scar. Consequences are heart failure, myocardial rupture, dysrhythmia and mural thrombus. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Mechanisms of Tachyarrhythmia

- Atrial flutter is an atrial re-entry loop depolarising at 250–350 bpm, and the AV node refractory period limits conduction, classically producing 2:1 block and a ventricular rate of 150 bpm. In atrial fibrillation no single impulse depolarises the atria completely, so the ventricular response is irregularly irregular, with untreated rates averaging about 170 bpm. [PEER-PH]
- Ventricular tachycardia originates at or below the bundle of His; monomorphic VT most often reflects scar-related re-entry after infarction, whereas polymorphic VT during acute ischaemia more likely reflects abnormal automaticity. [PEER-PH]
- Torsades de pointes results from delayed repolarisation that allows after-depolarisations to trigger autonomous ventricular depolarisation in a polymorphic, undulating pattern. Causes include congenital long QT syndrome, drugs (tricyclics, fluconazole, macrolides, fluoroquinolones, ondansetron, methadone, antipsychotics, quinidine), infarction, cardiomyopathy, hypomagnesaemia, hypokalaemia and hypocalcaemia. Because it is polymorphic and undulating, a defibrillator may fail to lock onto the QRS complexes for synchronised cardioversion. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Preexcitation and Accessory Pathways

- Wolff–Parkinson–White results from an accessory pathway with a short refractory period that bypasses the AV node and can conduct atrial impulses far faster than the AV node, producing rates of 150–300 bpm during pre-excited atrial fibrillation. The triad is a PR interval under 120 ms, a delta wave and a widened QRS. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Orthodromic AV reentrant tachycardia — anterograde down the AV node, retrograde through the accessory pathway — is 90–95% of AVRT and produces a narrow-complex tachycardia, whereas antidromic conduction produces a wide-complex tachycardia. All drugs that slow AV conduction are contraindicated in pre-excited atrial fibrillation because they may precipitate ventricular fibrillation. [PEER-PH]

### Bradyarrhythmias and Conduction System Disease

- AV block reflects delayed or interrupted conduction from atrium to ventricle; a PR interval of 200 ms or more defines first-degree block. Mobitz type I shows progressive PR prolongation before a non-conducted beat and is typically transient, whereas Mobitz type II shows a constant PR interval with intermittent non-conducted P waves and is infranodal, so it is more likely to progress. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Complete heart block produces AV dissociation with an escape pacemaker driving the ventricles more slowly than the atria: a junctional escape gives 40–60 bpm with a narrow QRS, an infranodal escape under 40 bpm and usually a wide QRS. Escape rhythms are generally inadequate to maintain cardiac output, and infranodal blocks do not respond to atropine. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Bundle-branch block reflects delayed depolarisation down one bundle, so the contralateral bundle depolarises first and the QRS widens beyond 120 ms; in left bundle-branch block, R waves in I, aVL and V5–V6 become broad and notched and V1 shows a late deep S wave. Delay down the left anterior fascicle causes left axis deviation with a normal QRS duration. [PEER-PH]
- Both the left anterior fascicle and the right bundle branch are supplied by the proximal LAD, so a new bifascicular block suggests LAD occlusion and predicts progression to complete heart block. [PEER-PH]

### Acute Aortic Syndromes

- Aortic dissection occurs when blood enters through an intimal tear into the media, forming a false lumen between the intimal and adventitial layers that extends proximally, distally or both. The Stanford classification separates type A, involving the ascending aorta, from type B. [ROSH]
- Pain occurs in 95% of patients, chest pain in 72.7% and back pain in 53.2%; type A more often presents with chest pain and type B with back pain. Extension of the tear can disrupt the aortic valve, producing an early diastolic blowing murmur of aortic regurgitation, and bleeding into the pericardial space causes tamponade with electrical alternans. [PEER-PH]
- Branch-vessel involvement produces pulse deficits, asymmetric blood pressures and focal neurologic deficits — left common carotid involvement causes right-sided facial droop and arm weakness. Pregnancy is an independent risk factor, usually in the third trimester or postpartum. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Aortic Aneurysm and Rupture

- An abdominal aortic aneurysm is an aortic diameter above 3 cm; risk rises with age over 60, male sex and smoking, which is the most important risk factor and accelerates growth and rupture. Rupture risk rises steeply with diameter, most ruptured aneurysms exceed 5 cm, and a diameter above 5.5 cm is the strongest risk factor. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Rupture is usually retroperitoneal, and blood may dissect into the perineum and groin producing scrotal or vulvar haematoma, periumbilical ecchymosis (Cullen sign) or flank ecchymosis (Grey Turner sign); without intervention it is universally fatal within 1 week. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Carotid artery dissection occurs with minor neck trauma or manipulation; the ascending sympathetic fibres lie close to the carotid, so it produces a partial Horner syndrome of miosis and ptosis without anhidrosis, and it is the most frequent cause of stroke under age 45. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Acute Limb Ischaemia

- Thrombotic occlusion from plaque rupture or endothelial erosion accounts for about 80% of lower limb and 50% of upper limb acute arterial ischaemia, whereas emboli originate in the heart in the great majority of cases and lodge at vessel bifurcations. A known embolic source, a normal contralateral limb and sudden onset favour embolism over thrombosis. [PEER-PH]

### Pericardial Syndromes

- Pericarditis is inflammation of the pericardium, most often idiopathic; other causes are infection (coxsackievirus most commonly), malignancy, drugs, connective tissue disease and post-infarction, and up to 90% of cases in resource-rich countries are idiopathic. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- The inflamed pericardium causes pleuritic pain worse lying back and relieved by sitting forward; the ECG shows diffuse ST elevation with PR depression and no reciprocal change. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Pericardial effusion limits diastolic filling. Right atrial collapse during diastole is the earliest sonographic sign and right ventricular diastolic collapse the most specific for tamponade. Tamponade depends on the rate of accumulation as much as the volume, so trauma is the cause most likely to produce haemodynamic compromise from a small effusion; about 250 mL is needed for cardiomegaly on chest radiograph. [ROSH]
- Constrictive pericarditis results from a rigid, non-compliant pericardium, often after tuberculosis, cardiac surgery or radiation: the right ventricle cannot accommodate increased venous return, so jugular venous pressure paradoxically rises on inspiration (Kussmaul sign), with a pericardial knock and a prominent y descent. [ROSH]

### Valvular Pathophysiology

- Aortic stenosis obstructs left ventricular outflow and arises from a bicuspid valve, rheumatic disease or degenerative calcification, the commonest aetiology in the United States and predominant in adults over 65. Symptoms progress from dyspnoea to angina to exertional syncope and finally congestive heart failure, which carries the poorest prognosis. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Aortic regurgitation results from failure of leaflet closure with retrograde diastolic flow; compensatory left ventricular hypertrophy and dilatation eventually compromise systolic function, and it occurs with Marfan syndrome and ascending aortic dissection. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Mitral regurgitation leaks blood from the left ventricle into the left atrium during systole; mitral valve prolapse is the commonest cause, and acute regurgitation follows chordal or papillary muscle rupture. Tricuspid regurgitation flows from the right ventricle into the right atrium during systole, raising right atrial pressure, and the commonest cause is dilation of the right atrium and ventricle. [ROSH]

### Heart Failure

- Low-output heart failure results from systolic or diastolic dysfunction with reduced cardiac output and increased systemic vascular resistance; dilated cardiomyopathy produces a nonspecific phenotype in which ischaemia, inflammatory infiltration and toxins drive neurohumoral changes causing persistent myocyte damage and remodelling. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- In high-output heart failure cardiac output is elevated beyond physiological demand with low systemic vascular resistance and a low arterial–venous oxygen difference. Causes include hyperthyroidism, leukaemia and lymphoma, mineralocorticoid excess, arteriovenous fistula, cirrhosis, pregnancy, severe anaemia and nutritional deficiency, and the extremities are warm and well perfused with a widened pulse pressure and S3 gallop. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- BNP is released by cardiac myocytes in response to ventricular stretch and high end-diastolic pressure; it promotes vasodilation, diuresis and sodium elimination and suppresses the renin–angiotensin system. ProBNP is cleaved into active BNP (half-life 20 minutes) and inactive NT-proBNP, whose half-life is up to six times longer, and obesity lowers measured levels. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Cor pulmonale is right ventricular hypertrophy or dysfunction from pulmonary hypertension or chronic lung disease, most commonly chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, with pulmonary hypertension defined as a mean pulmonary arterial pressure above 20 mm Hg. The right ventricle becomes thin-walled, dilated and highly afterload-sensitive, so decompensation triggers a cycle of falling coronary perfusion and worsening failure. [PEER-PH]

### Cardiomyopathies and Myocarditis

- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is an autosomal dominant disease of cardiac sarcomere proteins producing compensatory left ventricular hypertrophy without hypertension or aortic stenosis, most commonly asymmetric hypertrophy of the anterior interventricular septum, with disorganised cellular architecture and mitral valve leaflet malformation. These features predispose to outflow obstruction and fatal tachydysrhythmia, and the murmur increases with manoeuvres that reduce preload. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy is a genetic disease with fibrofatty infiltration of the right ventricle causing right-sided heart failure and ventricular arrhythmia with a risk of sudden cardiac death; the ECG shows a prolonged S-wave upstroke, an epsilon wave and T-wave inversion in V1–V3. [ROSH]
- Takotsubo cardiomyopathy follows acute catecholamine release causing endothelial and myocyte dysfunction, with transient apical ballooning extending beyond a single coronary territory; significant emotional stress precipitates about 90% of cases in postmenopausal women, and abnormalities resolve in 2–3 weeks. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Myocarditis is inflammation of the heart muscle with focal infiltration by lymphocytes, plasma cells and histiocytes, most often viral (Coxsackie B) and typically following a recent viral illness with fever, myalgia and disproportionate tachycardia; endocardial biopsy is the gold standard but is rarely performed. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Congenital Heart Disease Physiology

- Congenital lesions produce cyanosis or shock in the first weeks of life as the ductus arteriosus closes. Cyanosis that does not improve with 100% oxygen suggests a fixed intracardiac right-to-left shunt, whereas pulmonary disease responds — the basis of the hyperoxia test. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Ductal-dependent lesions rely on ductal patency for pulmonary or systemic blood flow: critical coarctation, hypoplastic left heart syndrome, pulmonary atresia with intact interventricular septum, transposition of the great arteries, tetralogy of Fallot, interrupted aortic arch, and critical aortic or pulmonary stenosis. [PEER-PH]
- Ventricular septal defect is the commonest congenital heart defect, over 30% of cases, and shunts left to right from the high-pressure left ventricle to the low-pressure right ventricle during systole; large defects cause congestive heart failure, and unrepaired defects may cause pulmonary hypertension from right ventricular volume overload. [PEER-PH]
- Tetralogy of Fallot is the commonest cyanotic congenital heart disease and the commonest structural lesion presenting outside the neonatal period, about 1 in 3,000 births. Tet spells arise from worsening right ventricular outflow obstruction with reduced pulmonary blood flow, and isoproterenol can worsen the haemodynamic state. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Pulmonary Embolism and Right Heart Strain

- Massive pulmonary embolism causes acute right ventricular dilatation and obstructive shock, and bedside echocardiography showing new severe right ventricular dilatation in a patient with a hypercoagulable state and sudden dyspnoea and chest pain supports thrombolysis; thrombolytics catalyse conversion of plasminogen to plasmin. [PEER-PH]
- Narrow-complex pulseless electrical activity typically results from mechanical obstruction, coronary ischaemia or hypovolaemia; mechanical causes include tension pneumothorax (associated with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and trauma), pulmonary embolism and cardiac tamponade. [PEER-PH]

### Pathophysiology of Cardiac Arrest

- EtCO2 reflects pulmonary blood flow and therefore perfusion during cardiopulmonary resuscitation: near-normal values indicate effective compressions, and a gradual fall suggests compressor fatigue rather than a change in rhythm. [PEER-PH]
- Pulseless electrical activity is a disorder of electromechanical coupling in myocytes, usually associated with myocardial energy depletion and acidosis — organised electrical activity without myocardial contraction. In pseudo-pulseless electrical activity myocardial activity is present but inadequate to generate a pulse, and compressions asynchronous with intrinsic contractions can reduce preload and cardiac output. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Ventricular fibrillation produces chaotic, disorganised electrical activity of varying amplitude and width; asystole is complete cessation of myocardial electrical activity and is usually a secondary event after prolonged ventricular fibrillation, pulseless electrical activity, hypoxia or acidosis. [ROSH]
- Hyperkalaemia in renal failure slows conduction, producing peaked T waves that flatten and widen, small P waves, QRS widening and ultimately the sine-wave pattern that can degenerate into pulseless electrical activity arrest. [PEER-PH]
- Over-ventilation during resuscitation raises intrathoracic pressure and reduces venous return, decreasing cerebral and coronary blood flow. Epinephrine increases the probability of ROSC, admission and discharge but has not been shown to improve neurologically favourable survival, and is believed to act by increasing coronary perfusion pressure. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Refractory ventricular fibrillation and VT storm may arise from coronary disease, acute coronary syndrome, long QT syndrome or electrolyte abnormality. Dopamine is a poor choice because of its proarrhythmic effect: as a beta-1 and alpha-1 agonist it increases sinoatrial conduction and can induce narrow-complex tachyarrhythmia and ventricular ectopy. [PEER-PH]

### Infective Endocarditis and Cardiac Tumours

- Infective endocarditis is bacterial or fungal infection of the cardiac valves; the left side is affected more often than the right, with the mitral valve commonest overall, while in intravenous drug use the tricuspid valve predominates. Vegetation on a valve is the echocardiographic hallmark and a major Duke criterion, and embolisation of vegetations produces mycotic aneurysm while valve destruction produces regurgitation and heart failure. [ROSH]
- Myxomas are the commonest primary benign cardiac tumours: about 75–85% arise in the left atrium at the fossa ovalis. They are typically pedunculated and gelatinous, and the tumour plop is produced by impact against the endocardial wall or abrupt halt of tumour excursion. Left atrial myxomas cause systemic emboli and right atrial myxomas pulmonary emboli. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
