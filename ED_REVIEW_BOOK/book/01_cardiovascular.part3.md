## Diagnosis

### Chest Pain — Pattern Recognition

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Pain radiating to both arms | ACS — the highest positive likelihood ratio [PEER-PH] |
| Sharp pleuritic pain, worse lying back, relieved sitting forward | Pericarditis [ROSH][PEER-PH] |
| Abrupt tearing pain radiating to the back | Aortic dissection [PEER-PH] |
| Tearing interscapular pain with facial droop and arm weakness | Dissection of the left common carotid artery [ROSH] |
| Epigastric fullness, nausea, lightheadedness | Inferior STEMI [ROSH] |

### ECG — Ischemia and Infarction

- STEMI: ≥ 1 mm of ST elevation in two or more contiguous leads, with or without a biomarker rise [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Inferior STEMI: II, III and aVF with reciprocal ST depression in aVL [ROSH]. High lateral MI [PEER-PH]; ST elevation in V5 and V6 [ROSH].
- Posterior infarction: ST depression with tall R waves and upright T waves in V1 to V3 from posterior descending artery occlusion; 15–20% of acute MIs [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- de Winter T waves: upsloping precordial ST depression over 1 mm with tall symmetric T waves plus ST elevation in aVR — proximal LAD occlusion [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Wellens syndrome: deep or biphasic anterior T waves while pain free with normal or mildly elevated markers; critical LAD stenosis with anterior STEMI within 30 to 90 days [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Isolated 1-mm ST elevation in aVR with reciprocal depression in V5 to V6 is not a STEMI equivalent; it reflects subendocardial ischemia from left main, LAD or multivessel disease [PEER-PH].

### Left Bundle-Branch Block — Sgarbossa Criteria

| Criterion | Detail |
|---|---|
| Concordant ST elevation | ≥ 1 mm in any lead with a positive QRS complex [ROSH][PEER-PH] |
| Concordant ST depression | ≥ 1 mm in leads V1 to V3 [ROSH][PEER-PH] |
| Discordant ST elevation, original | ≥ 5 mm in leads with a negative QRS complex [ROSH] |
| Modified Sgarbossa | Discordant ST elevation ≥ 1 mm equal to 30% of the preceding S wave [PEER-PH] |

- LBBB: QRS 130 ms with notched R waves in I, aVL, V5 to V6 and a deep late S wave in V1 [PEER-PH]. RBBB: an RSR' in lead V1 is the classic finding [PEER-PH][ROSH].

### Biomarkers and Risk Stratification

- Troponin is the most specific biomarker for acute MI [PEER-PH]; a normal troponin distinguishes unstable angina from NSTEMI [ROSH].
- High-sensitivity troponin 900 ng/L with 1 mm inferior ST depression and no ST elevation indicates NSTEMI [PEER-PH].
- HEART score ≤ 3 with two negative troponins supports discharge; a score of 2 carries a 0.9% to 1.7% 30-day risk of major adverse cardiovascular events [PEER-PH].
- Adverse-event predictors in acute heart failure: BUN 45 mg/dL, renal worsening in up to 25%, and a 7% mortality rise per 10 mL/min fall in glomerular filtration rate [PEER-PH].

### Dysrhythmia Recognition

| Rhythm | Defining features |
|---|---|
| Atrial fibrillation | Irregularly irregular with no distinct P waves; the most common cardiac dysrhythmia [ROSH] |
| Atrial flutter | Sawtooth waves, atrial rate 250 to 350 bpm; 2:1 conduction gives 150 bpm, 3:1 gives 100 bpm [PEER-PH] |
| AVNRT | Regular narrow complex, 120 to 200 bpm; P waves hidden in the preceding T wave [PEER-PH] |
| Ventricular tachycardia | QRS over 120 ms, rate 100 to 200 bpm, AV dissociation and fusion beats [PEER-PH][ROSH] |
| Torsade de pointes | Polymorphic VT with prolonged QT and cyclic alteration of the QRS axis [ROSH] |
| Wolff-Parkinson-White | PR under 120 ms, delta wave, widened QRS; features may be transient [ROSH] |

- With a prior MI, a wide-complex tachycardia is almost always VT [ROSH].

### AV Block and Bradyarrhythmia

- Mobitz I (Wenckebach): progressive PR prolongation before a dropped QRS; typically transient, seen with inferior MI, digoxin toxicity, myocarditis or cardiac surgery [PEER-PH].
- Mobitz II: constant or prolonged PR interval with spontaneous loss of the QRS, often in a 4:1, 3:1 or 2:1 pattern [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Third-degree block: no association between P waves and QRS. Junctional escape gives a narrow QRS at 40 to 60 bpm; infranodal escape is usually wide and below 40 bpm [PEER-PH][ROSH].

### Aortic and Peripheral Vascular Emergencies

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Tearing pain, pulse deficit, 40 mm Hg inter-arm difference, early diastolic murmur | Aortic dissection [PEER-PH] |
| Widened mediastinum on chest x-ray | Aortic dissection [PEER-PH][ROSH] |
| Electrical alternans with tachycardia and hypotension | Dissection with pericardial bleeding and tamponade; effusion occurs in up to 30% of dissections [PEER-PH][ROSH] |
| Flank pain, hypotension, pulsatile mass | Ruptured AAA — the classic triad is uncommon; about 10% present with syncope [ROSH] |
| GI bleeding after aortic surgery | Secondary aortoenteric fistula, most commonly into the third or fourth part of the duodenum [PEER-PH] |
| Sudden severe foot pain with an irregular pulse and no trauma | Arterial embolism, most often from atrial fibrillation [ROSH][PEER-PH] |

- AAA is an abdominal aortic diameter over 3 cm; size determines rupture, most occur above 5 cm, and repair is considered above 5–5.5 cm or with growth beyond 0.5 cm per year. Smoking is the most important risk factor [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Carotid artery dissection is the most frequent cause of stroke below age 45: abrupt unilateral neck pain with partial ipsilateral Horner syndrome — miosis and ptosis without facial anhidrosis [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Acute limb ischemia is most often thrombosis of a native artery or stent; atrial fibrillation is the most common cause of arterial embolism. D-dimer is 82% to 98% sensitive but not specific for aortic dissection [PEER-PH][ROSH].

### Imaging Selection

| Study | Role |
|---|---|
| CTA of the chest | First-line for aortic dissection; sensitivity 83% to 100%, specificity 87% to 100% [PEER-PH] |
| Transesophageal echocardiography | 98% sensitivity and 96% specificity for type A dissection, done at the bedside in unstable patients [PEER-PH] |
| CT abdomen and pelvis with contrast | Most accurate for AAA and able to show rupture; non-contrast CT may show the aneurysm but not the rupture [ROSH][PEER-PH] |
| Echocardiography | Test of choice for hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and of greatest value in suspected peripartum cardiomyopathy [PEER-PH][ROSH] |

### Heart Failure, Cardiomyopathy and Pericardial Disease

- Acute decompensated heart failure: dyspnea, orthopnea, jugular venous distension, pulmonary congestion and edema; an S3 gallop and widespread B lines on lung ultrasound support the diagnosis [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- E-point septal separation on M-mode estimates left ventricular function: under 7 mm is normal, over 10 mm suggests heart failure [PEER-PH].
- High-output heart failure is elevated cardiac output with low systemic vascular resistance; causes include arteriovenous fistula, cirrhosis, pregnancy, severe anemia and thyrotoxicosis [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy requires left ventricular systolic dysfunction with an ejection fraction below 45% [PEER-PH].
- Takotsubo syndrome: apical ballooning extending beyond a single coronary distribution after an emotional or physical stressor, resolving within 2 to 3 weeks; 2% to 6% of suspected ACS cases are stress cardiomyopathy [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: autosomal dominant sarcomere protein mutation causing asymmetric septal hypertrophy with systolic anterior motion of the mitral valve; the murmur increases with Valsalva and decreases with squatting [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Cardiac tamponade: right ventricular diastolic collapse is the most specific feature; right atrial diastolic collapse, low-voltage QRS complexes and a large effusion with hypotension, tachycardia and jugular venous distension also indicate tamponade [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Myocarditis: disproportionate tachycardia with fever, myalgias and chest pain, most often between ages 20 and 50 after a recent viral illness; biomarker elevation distinguishes myopericarditis from pericarditis [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- The most common complication of infective endocarditis is congestive heart failure [ROSH]; the modified Duke criteria are met by two major and one minor criterion [ROSH]. The mitral valve is involved most commonly and the tricuspid valve in intravenous drug use, with Staphylococcus aureus the most common organism [ROSH][PEER-PH].

### Valvular and Congenital Findings

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Systolic crescendo-decrescendo murmur radiating to the carotids | Aortic stenosis, best heard at the right upper sternal border [ROSH][PEER-PH] |
| Holosystolic murmur at the apex radiating to the axilla | Mitral regurgitation [ROSH] |
| Early diastolic blowing murmur at the left lower sternal border | Ascending dissection with aortic regurgitation; heard in 31.6% of patients [PEER-PH] |
| Central cyanosis in the first 2 weeks of life | Ductal-dependent congenital heart disease as the ductus closes [PEER-PH] |
| Single second heart sound with a harsh systolic ejection murmur | Tetralogy of Fallot, the most common cyanotic congenital heart disease [PEER-PH] |
| Absent femoral pulses with a well-perfused right arm, severe hypertension in an infant | Coarctation of the aorta [PEER-PH] |
| Harsh holosystolic murmur at the left lower sternal border | Ventricular septal defect, the most common congenital lesion [ROSH][PEER-PH] |

- Hyperoxia test: PaO2 above 250 mm Hg suggests a noncardiac cause, below 100 mm Hg suggests congenital heart disease, and 100 to 250 mm Hg may indicate a partial mixing lesion [PEER-PH].
- Severe aortic stenosis corresponds to a valve area below 1.0 cm2, with 0.8 cm2 used in the workup of syncope; it is the most common valvular heart disease above age 65 [ROSH].
- The most common cause of tricuspid stenosis is rheumatic heart disease; acute mitral regurgitation from papillary muscle rupture follows myocardial infarction, with pulmonary edema on chest x-ray [ROSH][PEER-PH].

## Management

### 1. Adult Cardiac Arrest

1. High-quality chest compressions: rate 100–120/min, depth 5–6 cm, full recoil, ventilation 10 breaths/min [ROSH].
2. VF and pulseless VT are shockable — defibrillate as early as possible, resuming compressions for 2 minutes after each shock [PEER-PH].
3. Epinephrine 1 mg IV every 3 to 5 minutes [PEER-PH][ROSH]. It raises the probability of return of spontaneous circulation, admission and discharge but has not been shown to improve neurologic outcome [PEER-PH].
4. Amiodarone after the second unsuccessful defibrillation and epinephrine in refractory VF or pulseless VT. Keep end-tidal carbon dioxide above 10 mm Hg as a perfusion marker [PEER-PH].
5. Refractory VF (three failed defibrillations): change the vector orientation of the pads and defibrillate again, or use dual sequential defibrillation. VT storm may respond to esmolol 0.5 mg/kg bolus [PEER-PH].
6. Asystole: confirm in at least two leads, then CPR, ventilation, IV or IO access and epinephrine every 3 to 5 minutes; without IV/IO access give epinephrine by the endotracheal route [ROSH].
7. Pulseless electrical activity: CPR, epinephrine and reversal of the cause. Narrow-complex PEA suggests mechanical obstruction, ischemia or hypovolaemia — consider tension pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism and tamponade [ROSH][PEER-PH]. Hyperkalaemic PEA: calcium chloride, which delivers three times the calcium of gluconate [PEER-PH].
8. Pseudo-pulseless electrical activity: organized electrical activity with an impalpable pulse but a measurable blood pressure. Treat with pressors and fluids rather than compressions, which can be detrimental [PEER-PH].
9. Massive pulmonary embolism causing arrest: thrombolysis with alteplase or tenecteplase. Penetrating chest trauma with loss of pulses: resuscitative thoracotomy [PEER-PH].
10. Targeted temperature management after return of spontaneous circulation in comatose patients: maintain 32°C to 37.5°C and strictly avoid fever for the first 72 hours [ROSH].

### 2. Tachyarrhythmia

Step 1 — establish stability. Instability means hypotension, altered mental status, chest pain or heart failure; unstable patients receive synchronized cardioversion, stable patients drug therapy [PEER-PH].

Step 2 — stable narrow-complex SVT

1. Modified Valsalva manoeuvre; 43% converted with the modified Valsalva alone at 1 minute in the REVERT trial [PEER-PH].
2. Adenosine 6 mg IV, then 12 mg if the rhythm does not terminate within 1 to 2 minutes [PEER-PH][ROSH].
3. If adenosine fails, diltiazem or metoprolol; a calcium channel blocker is preferred with a history of asthma [PEER-PH].

Step 3 — stable wide-complex tachycardia

1. Procainamide is the antiarrhythmic of choice; amiodarone and lidocaine are less effective [PEER-PH].
2. Monomorphic VT: procainamide 1 g IV, or an infusion of 25 to 50 mg/min to a maximum of 17 mg/kg. Avoid in acute MI, long QT or significant left ventricular dysfunction [PEER-PH].

Step 4 — Wolff-Parkinson-White

1. Use procainamide; it blocks the accessory pathway and terminates pre-excited tachycardia [PEER-PH][ROSH].
2. Avoid all drugs that slow or block AV conduction — they may accelerate conduction down the accessory pathway and precipitate ventricular fibrillation. Adenosine is contraindicated in antidromic AVRT [PEER-PH][ROSH].

Step 5 — atrial fibrillation and flutter

1. Assess for reversible causes: infection, myocardial ischemia, hyperthyroidism, alcohol and bleeding [PEER-PH].
2. Rate control with diltiazem or metoprolol; a calcium channel blocker is preferred in chronic lung disease, a beta-blocker with recent MI or systolic heart failure [PEER-PH].
3. Anticoagulate non-valvular atrial fibrillation when CHA2DS2-VASc is ≥ 2 in men or ≥ 3 in women. Factor Xa inhibitors — rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban — need no routine blood level or INR checks [PEER-PH].
4. Atrial flutter is treated in the same manner as atrial fibrillation [PEER-PH].

Step 6 — torsade de pointes: correct metabolic abnormalities, defibrillate if the arrhythmia recurs, and admit to a coronary care unit. Synchronized cardioversion may fail because the defibrillator cannot lock onto the polymorphic QRS, so unsynchronized defibrillation is used in arrest [PEER-PH].

| Energy | Setting |
|---|---|
| 0.5 to 1 J/kg | Synchronized cardioversion, paediatric SVT [PEER-PH][ROSH] |
| 2 J/kg, then 4 J/kg | Defibrillation, paediatric VF and pulseless VT [PEER-PH] |
| 25 to 50 J, typically 50 J on biphasic | Synchronized cardioversion of atrial flutter; 100 J is also described [PEER-PH] |
| 50 to 100 J | Synchronized cardioversion for SVT [PEER-PH] |
| 100 J | Synchronized cardioversion for unstable AVNRT and unstable monomorphic VT [ROSH][PEER-PH] |
| 200 J | Unsynchronized defibrillation for torsade de pointes causing arrest [ROSH] |

### 3. Bradyarrhythmia and Pacing

1. Atropine 1 mg IV, repeated every 3 to 5 minutes to a maximum of 3 mg [PEER-PH].
2. Inferior STEMI with third-degree block is more likely to respond to atropine because the block is partly due to increased vagal tone from the Bezold-Jarisch reflex. Complete heart block is otherwise often unresponsive [PEER-PH].
3. In the symptomatic patient place transcutaneous pacer pads, admit to telemetry, and proceed to transcutaneous then transvenous pacing [PEER-PH][ROSH].
4. Temporary pacing is indicated for new right bundle-branch block with left anterior fascicular block, because both share a supply from the proximal LAD; PR prolongation or new alternating bundle-branch block further raises the risk of complete heart block [PEER-PH].
5. Definitive management of third-degree block is implantation of a permanent pacemaker [PEER-PH].

### 4. Acute Coronary Syndromes

1. Aspirin — the intervention with the greatest mortality impact in acute coronary syndrome [ROSH].
2. Heparin, for example an unfractionated heparin bolus followed by infusion in NSTEMI [ROSH].
3. P2Y12 inhibition with ticagrelor in addition to aspirin and heparinization when percutaneous coronary intervention is imminent [PEER-PH].
4. Primary percutaneous coronary intervention, ideally within 90 minutes, with immediate transfer when that door-to-balloon time is achievable. Fibrinolysis with tenecteplase is used when intervention is not available [PEER-PH][ROSH].
5. Beta-blockers are contraindicated within 24 hours of STEMI in the presence of hypotension [PEER-PH].
6. Nitrates are contraindicated in patients taking sildenafil because of profound hypotension [ROSH]. Supplemental oxygen may cause coronary vasoconstriction; reserve it for hypoxaemia [ROSH].
7. LBBB with positive modified Sgarbossa criteria, or any STEMI-equivalent pattern such as de Winter T waves, warrants emergent catheterisation [ROSH][PEER-PH].

### 5. Acute Aortic Dissection

1. Give a beta-blocker first — esmolol or labetalol — to lower heart rate and reduce aortic shear stress. Never use a pure vasodilator alone because of reflex tachycardia [PEER-PH].
2. Target systolic blood pressure 100–120 mm Hg and heart rate below 60 bpm [PEER-PH].
3. Add a vasodilator such as nicardipine after the heart rate goal is met. Control must be faster than the standard reduction of mean arterial pressure by 25% over 1 to 2 hours [PEER-PH].
4. Fibrinolytic agents are contraindicated because of catastrophic consequences including cardiac tamponade and death [PEER-PH].
5. Type A dissection requires emergent surgical repair; type B may be suitable for endovascular repair. Pregnancy is an independent risk factor [PEER-PH][ROSH].

| Drug | Dose |
|---|---|
| Esmolol | 250 to 500 mcg/kg loading dose, then 25 to 50 mcg/kg/min, maximum 300 mcg/kg/min; first-line because it is short acting and easily titrated [PEER-PH] |
| Labetalol | 20 mg IV repeated every 10 minutes to a maximum of 300 mg, or an infusion of 0.5 to 2 mg/min [PEER-PH] |
| Nicardipine | Titratable infusion, added after the heart rate goal is achieved [PEER-PH] |

### 6. Hypertensive Emergencies

1. Hypertensive emergency is blood pressure above 180/120 mm Hg with target-organ damage to brain, eyes, heart, aorta or kidneys [ROSH].
2. Lower mean arterial pressure by 20% to 25% within the first hour, then to 160/100–110 mm Hg over the next 6 hours, using titratable IV infusions to prevent overcorrection [PEER-PH].
3. Preferred agents are IV nitroglycerin or nicardipine, both titratable to goal [ROSH][PEER-PH].
4. More aggressive goals are required in aortic dissection, severe preeclampsia or eclampsia, and pheochromocytoma crisis. Severe hypertension in pregnancy is treated with hydralazine [PEER-PH].
5. Hypertensive encephalopathy with posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome: nicardipine titrated to a mean arterial pressure of 126 [PEER-PH].
6. Ischemic stroke in a thrombolysis candidate: lower blood pressure only if it is ≥ 185/110 mm Hg [ROSH].
7. Asymptomatic severe hypertension: no specific systolic threshold warrants acute treatment. Resume the patient's usual medication at the same dose and arrange follow-up [ROSH].

### 7. Acute Heart Failure and Cardiomyopathy

1. Supplemental oxygen, cardiac monitoring, pulse oximetry and intravenous access. Noninvasive positive pressure ventilation — CPAP or BiPAP — decreases intubation rates and in-hospital mortality when added to standard therapy in acute decompensated systolic heart failure with pulmonary edema [PEER-PH].
2. Nitroglycerin and furosemide improve symptoms; nitrates are the preferred agents for pulmonary edema from hypertensive emergency [ROSH][PEER-PH].
3. Avoid morphine: out-of-hospital morphine for presumed pulmonary edema is associated with more intubation, longer stay, more ICU admission and higher mortality [PEER-PH].
4. Survival-improving chronic therapy in dilated cardiomyopathy: carvedilol or metoprolol, with ACE inhibitors also reducing mortality. Metoprolol succinate is started at 12.5 to 25 mg daily with a later goal of 200 mg daily. Digoxin may be added for symptoms but does not improve survival [PEER-PH].
5. Peripartum cardiomyopathy: hydralazine with isosorbide dinitrate is the vasodilator combination of choice and prolongs survival. Diuretics, beta-blockers and digoxin are considered safe in pregnancy; angiotensin II receptor blockers, ACE inhibitors and angiotensin receptor-neprilysin inhibitors are contraindicated [PEER-PH].
6. Hemodynamically unstable takotsubo cardiomyopathy: phenylephrine is first-line. In hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, avoid nitroglycerin, which can worsen the hemodynamic state [PEER-PH][ROSH].

### 8. Pulmonary Embolism and Venous Thromboembolism

1. The strongest indication for systemic thrombolysis in pulmonary embolism is hypotension; other proposed indications are a large clot burden, right ventricular dysfunction with troponin or BNP elevation, and progressive decompensation despite treatment [PEER-PH].
2. Acute proximal DVT in pregnancy: enoxaparin throughout pregnancy and the postpartum period when kidney function is normal, because low-molecular-weight heparin does not cross the placenta [ROSH].
3. DVT with end-stage kidney disease on haemodialysis: intravenous unfractionated heparin [ROSH].
4. Factor Xa inhibitors — rivaroxaban, apixaban, edoxaban — treat DVT and PE and prevent stroke in non-valvular atrial fibrillation [PEER-PH].

### 9. Pericarditis and Tamponade

1. Acute viral pericarditis: NSAIDs, with ibuprofen 400 to 800 mg three times daily for 7 to 14 days, plus colchicine, which reduces symptoms and decreases recurrence [PEER-PH][ROSH].
2. Admit for fever over 38°C, a large effusion or tamponade. Uremic pericarditis is managed initially with hemodialysis [PEER-PH][ROSH].
3. Tamponade: intravenous fluids while arranging definitive care, then emergent pericardiocentesis or pericardial window [PEER-PH][ROSH]. Constrictive pericarditis is treated with pericardiectomy [ROSH].

### 10. Infective Endocarditis

1. Draw a minimum of three blood cultures before starting antibiotics [ROSH].
2. Empiric therapy for suspected native valve endocarditis: vancomycin [PEER-PH].
3. Order a transthoracic echocardiogram; if negative in a high-risk patient such as a person who injects drugs, proceed to transesophageal echocardiography [ROSH].
4. Consult cardiothoracic surgery for persistent hypotension with regurgitation at the mitral and tricuspid valves [PEER-PH].

### 11. Devices and Mechanical Support

- Left ventricular assist device: mean arterial pressure best estimates blood pressure in continuous-flow devices; bleeding is the most common major non-mechanical complication, the driveline is the most likely source of infection, and a suction event presents with a low-flow alarm after vomiting or diarrhoea [PEER-PH].
- Pacemaker undersensing is most often caused by fibrosis at the lead tips; Twiddler syndrome causes arm twitching. A magnet over a pacemaker returns it to default settings and a fixed rate, while a magnet over a defibrillator disables its ability to sense dysrhythmias [PEER-PH][ROSH].

### 12. Paediatric and Neonatal

1. Asystole is the most common rhythm in paediatric cardiac arrest, with respiratory failure the most common antecedent. Give epinephrine 0.01 mg/kg; compress at 100–120/min using the hand-encircling technique in infants [ROSH].
2. VF or pulseless VT: defibrillate at 2 J/kg for the first shock and 4 J/kg for the second, resuming CPR for 2 minutes after each shock [PEER-PH].
3. Refractory paediatric VF: amiodarone 5 mg/kg IV over 20 to 60 minutes, repeatable up to two additional times, maximum 15 mg/kg or 300 mg total [PEER-PH].
4. Unstable SVT in a child: synchronized cardioversion at 0.5 to 1 J/kg; adenosine 0.1 mg/kg is used for the stable child [PEER-PH][ROSH].
5. Ductal-dependent lesions: start prostaglandin E1 at 0.05 mcg/kg/min IV. Expect apnea, bradycardia and hypotension; the patient requires intensive care admission and cardiology consultation [PEER-PH].
6. Tet spell: supplemental oxygen and knee-to-chest position increase systemic vascular resistance. If these fail, give a fluid bolus and morphine, recognising that morphine may further decrease systemic vascular resistance [PEER-PH].
7. Targeted temperature management after paediatric cardiac arrest: 32–34°C for 48 hours then 36–37.5°C for 3 days, or 36–37.5°C for 5 days, to prevent hyperthermia [PEER-PH].
