## Pathophysiology

### Ascending genital tract infection
- PID is an ascending infection from the vagina and cervix, progressing through endometritis and salpingitis to oophoritis, tubo-ovarian abscess, and pelvic peritonitis [ROSH].
- Perihepatitis (Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome) is PID that has ascended into the peritoneal cavity, producing focal peritonitis with inflammation of the liver capsule and adhesions between the capsule and the right upper quadrant peritoneum [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Because the liver and biliary system themselves are not affected, liver function tests and right upper quadrant ultrasound remain normal [PEER-PH].
- Chlamydia trachomatis and Neisseria gonorrhoeae are the organisms most frequently implicated [PEER-PH].
- The high incidence in adolescents — 27% of adolescents with PID versus 4% to 13.8% of adults — is hypothesised to result from cervical ectropion [PEER-PH].
- HIV coinfection promotes progression of PID to tubo-ovarian abscess [PEER-PH].
- Tubal damage destroys the ciliated epithelial cells of the fallopian tube, producing tubal-factor infertility and predisposing to ectopic implantation [ROSH].

### The vaginal ecosystem and vaginitis
- BV occurs when normal lactobacilli are replaced by higher concentrations of anaerobic bacteria, principally Gardnerella vaginalis and Mycoplasma hominis, with Mobiluncus, Bacteroides, and Prevotella species also involved [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Clue cells — epithelial cells coated with bacteria that obscure their borders — are the pathognomonic finding [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Trichomonas vaginalis is a flagellated protozoan infecting the urogenital squamous epithelium — vagina, urethra, and endocervix — triggering neutrophil recruitment to the vaginal mucosa [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Candida infection produces hyphae and pseudohyphae on wet mount, and noninfectious vaginitis follows chemical or allergic insult or retention of a foreign body such as a tampon [PEER-PH][ROSH].

### Ectopic implantation
- Ectopic pregnancy is implantation away from the normal uterine endometrium, most commonly in the fallopian tube. Ectopic pregnancies are nonviable and intervention is nearly always required [ROSH].
- Risk arises from any factor that scars or distorts the tube: PID, prior STI, previous ectopic pregnancy, tubal surgery or instrumentation, an IUD in place, smoking, and assisted reproductive technology [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Rupture produces hemoperitoneum and life-threatening hemorrhage [ROSH].

### Ovarian torsion and ovarian hyperstimulation
- Ovarian torsion is twisting of the ovary around the oviduct, which first impairs venous drainage and ultimately disrupts arterial inflow, producing ischemia [ROSH].
- Mass lesions predispose to torsion because they cause the ovary to rotate around the infundibulopelvic and utero-ovarian ligaments, which contain the vasculature supplying the ovary. The most common sonographic finding is an enlarged ovary on the affected side [ROSH].
- Ovulation induction may produce large ovarian follicular cysts and significant ovarian enlargement, placing fertility patients at particular risk [PEER-PH].
- Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome is driven by increased vascular permeability from vascular endothelial growth factor release, causing transudation of fluid and albumin out of blood vessels [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- The result is third-space fluid extravasation with hemoconcentration, pleural effusions, prerenal azotemia, hyponatremia, and hyperkalemia [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Because the ovaries are significantly enlarged and fragile, bimanual pelvic examination carries a substantial risk of ovarian rupture or torsion [ROSH].

### Hypertensive disorders: placental dysfunction and end-organ injury
- Preeclampsia and HELLP syndrome are thought to arise secondary to placental dysfunction, although the pathogenesis is not fully clear [PEER-PH].
- Preeclampsia with severe features causes necrosis and hemorrhage of organs, including intracranial hemorrhage, hepatic and splenic hemorrhage, placental abruption, pulmonary edema, stroke, maternal death, and fetal death [PEER-PH].
- HELLP syndrome consists of hemolysis with schistocytes on the peripheral smear, elevated lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and increased bilirubin, with transaminase elevation and thrombocytopenia. The coagulation system is typically spared — fibrinogen, PT, and PTT are not usually affected [PEER-PH].
- Eclamptic seizures are controlled by magnesium sulfate, which works through membrane stabilization and vasodilatation [PEER-PH].
- Magnesium toxicity produces central nervous system depression, hyporeflexia, respiratory depression, heart block, and hypotension; intravenous calcium reverses the neuromuscular and cardiac effects [ROSH][PEER-PH].

### Placental abruption and placenta previa
- Placental abruption is bleeding at the interface of the placenta and the uterine decidua, causing complete or partial detachment before delivery of the fetus. It occurs in about 1% of pregnancies and is a leading cause of third-trimester bleeding [ROSH].
- Cocaine induces vasoconstriction and significantly increases the risk of placental separation; up to 10% of women who use cocaine in the third trimester may experience an abruption [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Other mechanisms include maternal trauma, hypertension and preeclampsia, tobacco use, advanced maternal age or parity, and prior abruption [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Ultrasound sensitivity for abruption is poor because blood and placenta have similar echogenicity, so the diagnosis is usually clinical [ROSH].
- In placenta previa the placenta implants lower in the uterus and may extend near, partially over, or completely over the internal cervical os — marginal, partial, and complete previa respectively. A low-lying or partial previa normally migrates upward as pregnancy progresses [ROSH].
- The pathogenesis is abnormal implantation in the lower uterine segment, likely related to endometrial damage from prior uterine surgery or impaired decidualisation. The most significant risk factor is prior cesarean delivery, with risk increasing dramatically with each subsequent cesarean [ROSH].

### Uterine rupture
- Uterine rupture is a complete division of all three layers of the uterus, interrupting normal uterine contractions and expelling the fetus, amniotic fluid, and blood into the peritoneal cavity [PEER-PH].
- Prior cesarean section is the most common etiology; poor uterine healing or scar formation leaves the lower segment vulnerable [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Most ruptures in resource-rich countries occur during a trial of labor after cesarean delivery (TOLAC). Other risk factors include previous uterine rupture, a high vertical hysterotomy, induction, labor, trauma, and prostaglandin use [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- In a young pregnant patient with uterine rupture and no obvious risk factors, a collagen vascular disease should be strongly suspected; vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome is the most serious form [PEER-PH].

### Amniotic fluid embolism and disseminated intravascular coagulation
- Amniotic fluid embolism is a rare, life-threatening complication of pregnancy and delivery. Disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) is the most common coagulopathy associated with it [PEER-PH].
- DIC is characterised by severe, extensive clotting with microthrombi in small blood vessels. This consumes clotting factors, producing low platelets, low fibrinogen, and prolonged coagulation times, while the extensive clotting raises D-dimer levels [PEER-PH].

### Postpartum hemorrhage: failure of myometrial hemostasis
- After delivery of the placenta, hemostasis is normally achieved by contraction of the myometrium, which constricts the spiral arteries and provides mechanical hemostasis, aided by local hemostatic factors that promote clotting [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- If the uterus does not contract effectively, the exposed spiral arteries at the site of placental implantation continue to bleed. Uterine atony accounts for the vast majority of cases, responsible for 75–90% of postpartum hemorrhages [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Because pregnancy produces a 40% to 50% increase in circulating blood volume, shock may not appear until more than 1,500 mL has been lost [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Retained uterine products of conception are the most common cause of late or delayed postpartum hemorrhage, occurring between 24 hours and 6 weeks after delivery [PEER-PH].

### Physiologic changes of pregnancy
- The uterine fundus grows up out of the pelvis at approximately 12 weeks and becomes an intra-abdominal organ, reaching the umbilicus at 20 weeks, the xiphoid process at 36 weeks, and a few centimetres below the xiphoid at 40 weeks [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- The gravid uterus compresses the inferior vena cava and decreases venous return, producing supine hypotension that the left lateral decubitus position relieves [PEER-PH].
- Increased progesterone raises resting minute ventilation through a larger tidal volume, but the respiratory rate itself remains unchanged — a respiratory rate of 28 breaths/minute in pregnancy therefore indicates pathology [ROSH].
- Radiation risk to fetal neurologic development is highest between 10 and 17 weeks of gestational age. Exposure below 50 mGy does not lead to fetal death or central nervous system effects [PEER-PH].

### Sheehan syndrome (postpartum pituitary necrosis)
- The pituitary gland enlarges during pregnancy and becomes vulnerable to ischemia when massive hemorrhage causes hypovolemia and impaired blood supply, producing ischemic necrosis with panhypopituitarism [ROSH].
- Low thyroid-stimulating hormone indicates central (secondary) hypothyroidism from loss of thyrotroph cell function, which explains bradycardia and hypotension [ROSH].
- Laboratory abnormalities include hyponatremia, hypoglycemia, and anemia secondary to adrenal insufficiency [PEER-PH].

### Endometriosis, abnormal uterine bleeding, and trophoblastic disease
- Endometriosis is implantation of endometrial glands and stroma at extrauterine sites, most commonly affecting pelvic organs, bowel, and bladder. The most common extra-pelvic site is the thorax, where it may cause pneumothorax or hemothorax with chest pain, dyspnea, and reduced breath sounds coinciding with menstruation [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Abnormal uterine bleeding is bleeding of abnormal quantity, duration, or schedule. Causes include fibroids, endometrial polyps, adenomyosis, coagulopathy, ovulatory dysfunction, thyroid derangements, and hyperprolactinemia [PEER-PH].
- In adolescents with heavy uterine bleeding soon after menarche, a bleeding disorder may be the underlying cause and may be its first manifestation [PEER-PH].
- A molar pregnancy is gestational trophoblastic disease characterised by disordered proliferation of chorionic villi. A complete hydatidiform mole has no fetal tissue; a partial mole has some fetal tissue with trophoblastic hyperplasia [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- The spectrum includes benign placental site nodules and exaggerated placental sites, and malignant gestational trophoblastic neoplasia such as invasive moles and choriocarcinoma, estimated to complicate 1 in 40,000 pregnancies [ROSH].
- Risk factors include advanced maternal age, prior molar pregnancy or gestational trophoblastic disease, pregnancy before age 20, and low dietary carotene intake [ROSH][PEER-PH].

### Intraamniotic infection and labour
- Intraamniotic infection (chorioamnionitis) is caused by ascending infection of normal vaginal flora; from 16 weeks the chorioamniotic membranes adhere to the cervical os and become at risk. It may cause prolonged labour and a decreased response to oxytocin [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Once membranes rupture, the risk of ascending infection increases in proportion to the number of examinations performed [ROSH].
- In umbilical cord prolapse the cord is compressed between the fetal presenting part and the maternal pelvis, impairing fetal oxygenation and progressing rapidly to hypoxemia, acidosis, and death if not corrected [PEER-PH].
- Cord compression from a nuchal cord produces variable or prolonged decelerations [ROSH].

### Postpartum infection and mastitis
- Postpartum endometritis begins in the endometrium and can extend to the myometrium or parametrium. It is usually polymicrobial with two to three aerobes and anaerobes — typically the flora of the bowel, perineum, vagina, and cervix [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Lactational mastitis results from milk stasis from ineffective latching or incomplete breast emptying, predisposing the breast to bacterial infiltration, most commonly by Staphylococcus aureus from the newborn's pharynx [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Necrotizing soft tissue infection of the perineum (Fournier gangrene) is a fulminant, rapidly progressive soft-tissue necrosis with a high risk of systemic decompensation. Up to 75% are type 1 infections from a polymicrobial combination of gram-positive cocci, gram-negative rods, and anaerobes [ROSH].
