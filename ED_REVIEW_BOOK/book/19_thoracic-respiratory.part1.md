## High-Yield Exam Review

- Massive pulmonary embolism (PE): systolic BP < 90 mm Hg for more than 15 minutes. Fibrinolysis is indicated for cardiac arrest, hypotension, or respiratory failure; alteplase 100 mg IV over 2 hours; norepinephrine is the recommended vasopressor; excess preload worsens right ventricular (RV) dilation. [PEER-CS]
- Most specific ECG finding for PE: simultaneous T-wave inversion in the inferior (II, III, aVF) and right precordial (V1–V4) leads; reported specificity 99%. [PEER-CS]
- The most common sign of PE is tachypnea; the most common vital-sign abnormality is tachycardia, seen in about half of patients; Westermark sign is the most specific radiographic finding. [ROSH]
- D-dimer is falsely elevated by liver disease (reduced clearance), pregnancy, kidney disease, malignancy, trauma, surgery in the previous week, rheumatoid arthritis, infection, and age over 70 years; warfarin falsely lowers it. [PEER-CS]
- Absolute contraindication to systemic thrombolysis in massive PE: prior intracranial haemorrhage. [ROSH]
- Hestia criteria exclude outpatient management of PE in pregnancy and in current apixaban use for atrial fibrillation. [ROSH]
- Tension pneumothorax: air enters but cannot escape the pleural space. Tracheal deviation is rare and very late. Needle decompression comes first, then tube thoracostomy. [PEER-CS]
- Needle decompression: 14-gauge angiocatheter at the second intercostal space in the midclavicular line, or the fourth to fifth intercostal space in the anterior axillary line; longer catheters improve success. [ROSH]
- Open pneumothorax (sucking chest wound): the size of the wound determines the physiology; treat with a three-sided dressing plus a chest tube. [ROSH]
- Secondary spontaneous pneumothorax: COPD accounts for over half of cases, usually through rupture of apical blebs. [ROSH]
- Small primary spontaneous pneumothorax under 10% of the hemithorax in a stable patient: oxygen and repeat chest radiograph in 6 hours. [ROSH]
- Croup: parainfluenza virus type 1 is the most common cause; dexamethasone for every severity; racemic epinephrine for stridor at rest; observe 2–4 hours after epinephrine before discharge. [ROSH]
- Epiglottitis: keep the child calm, defer intravenous access and tongue-blade examination, secure the airway first, then obtain cultures and give ceftriaxone plus vancomycin. [ROSH]
- Bacterial tracheitis: croup that improves and then worsens; Staphylococcus aureus is classic; vancomycin with a third-generation cephalosporin or ampicillin-sulbactam. [ROSH]
- Retropharyngeal abscess: the number-one cause of death is upper airway obstruction; ampicillin-sulbactam 50 mg/kg every 6 hours is first-line. [PEER-CS]
- Ludwig angina with a still-maintainable airway: flexible fiberoptic nasal intubation with topical anesthesia is the preferred technique. [ROSH]
- Moderate-to-severe asthma exacerbation: systemic corticosteroids for all; IV magnesium is added after inhaled short-acting beta-agonist, inhaled short-acting muscarinic antagonist, and steroids in severe exacerbations. [ROSH]
- Silent chest in asthma: start a nebulized beta-agonist; intubation is not the first move. [PEER-CS]
- Ventilated asthma or COPD: prolong the expiratory phase; breath stacking (auto-PEEP) responds to deeper analgesia and sedation and to a longer expiratory time. [ROSH][PEER-CS]
- Noninvasive ventilation in COPD exacerbation is indicated for acute hypercapnic respiratory failure, such as pH 7.28 with PCO2 58 mm Hg. [ROSH]
- Long-term oxygen therapy is one of the few COPD interventions that improves mortality. [ROSH]
- Inpatient community-acquired pneumonia (CAP): a macrolide plus a third-generation cephalosporin, or a respiratory fluoroquinolone alone. [ROSH]
- Streptococcus pneumoniae is the most common cause of CAP in both immunocompetent and immunocompromised patients; rust-coloured sputum with lobar consolidation is classic. [ROSH]
- Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia: CD4 < 200/µL, batwing bilateral interstitial infiltrates, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole; add prednisone when the alveolar–arterial gradient exceeds 35 mm Hg or PaO2 falls below 70 mm Hg. [ROSH]
- Tuberculosis: apical upper-lobe or superior lower-lobe disease; airborne precautions; RIPE regimen with hepatitis as a common adverse effect. [ROSH][PEER-CS]
- Exudative pleural fluid: protein ratio > 0.5, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) ratio > 0.6, or pleural LDH above two-thirds the upper limit of normal serum LDH. [ROSH]
- Empyema: bacterial pneumonia is the most common cause; drain with a 14 French thoracostomy tube plus antibiotics. [ROSH]
- Fat embolism syndrome: long bone fracture, onset 24–72 hours, triad of respiratory distress, confusion, and petechial rash. [ROSH]
- Fat embolism syndrome complicates 1–2% of isolated long bone fractures and 5–10% when multiple fractures are present. [ROSH]
- Acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS): PaO2/FiO2 < 300 mm Hg; lung-protective mechanical ventilation gives the largest mortality benefit; prone positioning improves oxygenation. [ROSH]

## Core Concepts

### The airway decision comes first

- Airway obstruction is the leading cause of death in retropharyngeal abscess and the terminal event in epiglottitis; both are managed by securing the airway before diagnostic imaging. [PEER-CS][ROSH]
- A child who is drooling, tripoding, or refusing to lie flat has a threatened airway: keep them calm, defer pharyngeal examination and intravenous access, and call anesthesia or otolaryngology.
- Distinguish the paediatric stridor syndromes by cough and secretions: croup (barking cough, no drooling), epiglottitis (no cough, drooling, toxic appearance), bacterial tracheitis (croup that worsens, copious purulent secretions, secretions still handled), retropharyngeal abscess (neck pain, prevertebral widening).
- Adults obstruct through a different set of lesions: Ludwig angina (submandibular cellulitis displacing the tongue), peritonsillar abscess, esophageal food impaction, and post-tracheostomy tracheal erosion.

### Lower airway obstruction: shared physiology, different management

- Both asthma and COPD produce expiratory airflow limitation, air trapping, and hyperinflation. Asthma is reversible bronchospasm and inflammation; COPD is inflammatory airflow limitation that is not fully reversible and has significant extrapulmonary effects. [ROSH]
- Asthma triggers are upper respiratory infection, inhaled allergens, exercise, and dry cold air; many children with asthma also have allergic rhinitis or eczema. [ROSH]
- A COPD exacerbation is defined by increased dyspnea, cough severity or frequency, or sputum volume or purulence; most are due to pulmonary infection. [ROSH]
- Asthma management is bronchodilator-led with steroids for every moderate-to-severe attack. COPD adds antibiotics only for those most likely to benefit: increased dyspnea, sputum volume and sputum purulence, particularly older patients or those with significant comorbidity. [ROSH]
- Both share a ventilator strategy: prolonged expiratory time, recognition of auto-PEEP, and acceptance of hypercapnia rather than high tidal volumes. [ROSH][PEER-CS]

### Pulmonary vascular disease

- PE is stratified as massive (systolic BP < 90 mm Hg for over 15 minutes), intermediate or submassive (normotensive with RV strain), and low risk (stable, no RV strain). Markers of RV strain are RV dilation or dysfunction on ultrasound, elevated brain natriuretic peptide, elevated troponin, and new ECG changes such as right bundle branch block or anterolateral T-wave inversion. [PEER-CS][ROSH]
- Stratification drives treatment: thrombolysis for massive disease, anticoagulation for the remainder, and as many as 30% of patients may be suitable for discharge on a direct oral anticoagulant such as apixaban or rivaroxaban. [ROSH]
- Pulmonary hypertension is a resting mean pulmonary artery pressure of 25 mm Hg or more, classified into five groups: pulmonary arterial hypertension, left heart disease, chronic lung disease with hypoxaemia, chronic thromboembolic disease, and multifactorial causes. [ROSH]
- These patients are preload-dependent; induction of anaesthesia and positive-pressure ventilation further reduce preload and can precipitate cardiovascular collapse. Norepinephrine before intubation is preferred; phenylephrine is avoided because it causes pulmonary vasoconstriction. [PEER-CS]

### Pleural space and mediastinum

- Air and fluid are the two pleural-space emergencies. Pneumothorax is primary spontaneous (no underlying lung disease; tall thin young men; at rest or minimal exertion), secondary spontaneous (underlying lung disease, COPD in over half), or traumatic. [ROSH]
- Pleural effusion is transudative (raised hydrostatic or reduced oncotic pressure: heart failure, hypoalbuminemia, cirrhosis, nephrotic syndrome) or exudative (malignancy, pneumonia, pulmonary embolism, pancreatitis). [ROSH]
- Pneumomediastinum usually follows alveolar rupture with air dissecting along bronchovascular sheaths into the mediastinum; in young people it is typically benign and self-limited. [ROSH]
- Mediastinitis from esophageal rupture carries time-dependent mortality that nearly doubles within the first 12 hours. [PEER-CS]

### Parenchymal infection

- Anatomy predicts organism and antibiotic: aspiration in the recumbent patient hits the posterior segment of the right upper lobe; reactivation tuberculosis favours the apical upper lobe and superior lower lobe; lung abscess follows aspiration or intravenous drug use. [ROSH]
- Streptococcus pneumoniae remains the most common cause of CAP at every level of immune competence. Atypical pathogens (Mycoplasma pneumoniae, Chlamydophila pneumoniae, Legionella species) account for roughly 15% of cases and are commoner in young outpatients. [ROSH]
- Host defence predicts the opportunist: CD4 < 200/µL suggests Pneumocystis jirovecii, transplant immunosuppression suggests PCP or tuberculosis, cystic fibrosis suggests Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and intravenous drug use suggests septic emboli with tricuspid endocarditis. [ROSH]
- Hospital-acquired pneumonia develops 48 hours or more after admission, and empiric therapy must cover multidrug-resistant organisms including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and Pseudomonas aeruginosa. [ROSH]

### Respiratory failure and mechanical ventilation

- ARDS is non-cardiogenic pulmonary oedema defined by PaO2/FiO2 < 300 mm Hg with bilateral radiographic infiltrates and no cardiogenic cause; a pulmonary capillary wedge pressure above 18 mm Hg points to a cardiac cause. It develops 6–72 hours after sepsis, pneumonia, aspiration, pancreatitis, or major trauma. [ROSH]
- Lung-protective ventilation is the emergency department intervention with the largest mortality benefit in ARDS; tidal volume is set from ideal body weight, not actual weight. [ROSH]
- Tolerated hypercapnia is not the enemy. The enemy is volutrauma, auto-PEEP, and, in COPD, abolition of the chronic hypoxic drive by uncontrolled high-flow oxygen. [ROSH]
