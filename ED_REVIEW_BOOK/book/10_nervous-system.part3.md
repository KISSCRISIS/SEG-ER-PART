## Diagnosis

### Headache: Separating Secondary from Primary

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Thunderclap onset, peaks in under 1 minute, onset during exertion | SAH |
| Positional, worse standing, relieved supine, after dural puncture | Post–dural puncture headache |
| Dull, worst in the morning, with focal weakness | Intracranial mass lesion |
| Unilateral temporal pain, jaw claudication, visual loss, age over 50 | Giant cell arteritis |
| Obese woman aged 20–44, papilledema, pulsatile tinnitus | Idiopathic intracranial hypertension |
| Unilateral periorbital pain with lacrimation, rhinorrhea, ptosis, miosis | Cluster headache |

- The Ottawa SAH Rule applies only to alert patients over 15 years with a nontraumatic, new-severe headache who are not neurologically impaired, and is not applied outside that population — for example a 78-year-old with new-onset headache. Its components are age 40 or older, peak intensity in under 1 minute, onset during exertion, neck pain or stiffness, limited neck flexion, and loss of consciousness; specificity is about 15% [PEER-CS].
- Post–dural puncture headache begins within 48 hours of lumbar puncture, is worse on standing, and is usually self-limiting within 24–48 hours [PEER-CS].

### Vertigo and Dizziness

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Episodic dizziness with a specific trigger | Peripheral vertigo |
| No corrective saccade on head impulse testing | Central vertigo |
| Bidirectional or direction-changing nystagmus | Central vertigo |
| Constant dizziness with neck pain after chiropractic manipulation | Vertebral artery dissection |

- The head impulse test helps only when nystagmus is present; an abnormal result — a corrective saccade — is very specific for peripheral vertigo. The HINTS examination has sensitivity for central vertigo approaching 100% [PEER-CS].
- Dizziness is the most common symptom of vertebral artery dissection; only about 50% have trauma or known risk factors [PEER-CS].

### Cerebrovascular Imaging

- Noncontrast CT of the head is the first-line study in suspected acute stroke and the most sensitive and specific test for hemorrhage. In suspected SAH it has 98–100% sensitivity within 6 hours; after that window the next step is CT angiography of the head and neck [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Ten percent of strokes are hemorrhagic and 90% ischemic; CT angiography identifies large-vessel occlusion suitable for clot retrieval, and elevated troponin is associated with increased mortality in ischemic stroke [PEER-CS][PEER-PH].
- CT venography of the head demonstrates cerebral venous sinus thrombosis in a patient with headache, confusion, seizure, and a hypercoagulable state such as polycythemia vera [PEER-CS].

### Cerebrospinal Fluid Analysis

| Parameter | Bacterial | Viral | Normal |
|---|---|---|---|
| White cells | High, neutrophil predominant | Lymphocytic, under 2,000/mm3 | Under 5/mm3 |
| Glucose | Low | Normal or over 60% of serum | 45–85 mg/dL |
| Protein | High | Mildly elevated | 15–45 mg/dL |

- Bacterial meningitis typically shows increased opening pressure, increased protein, low glucose, and a high white cell count with neutrophilic predominance; the fluid may be cloudy, though not all patients have significant alterations [PEER-CS][ROSH].
- Neurosyphilis produces a lymphocytic pleocytosis, usually 200 to 400 cells/microL in symptomatic meningitis and above 5 cells/microL in asymptomatic patients; initial evaluation is serum treponemal testing [PEER-CS].
- Albuminocytologic dissociation — normal white cell count with elevated protein — is found in 66% of Guillain-Barré syndrome cases 1 week after onset, and its absence does not exclude the diagnosis [ROSH][PEER-CS].
- Head CT before lumbar puncture is indicated for focal neurologic deficit, altered mental status, head trauma, new-onset seizures, papilledema, immunocompromise, or a history of CNS disease. Antibiotics are never delayed for CT or lumbar puncture [PEER-PH][ROSH].

### Spine and Spinal Cord

- Back pain is the most common presenting symptom of spinal epidural abscess, present in 70–90% of cases, but the classic triad of back pain, fever, and neurologic deficit occurs in only about 15%. Risk factors are intravenous drug use, diabetes, immunosuppression, and recent spinal procedures [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- MRI of the spine with gadolinium is the diagnostic study of choice and C-reactive protein is the most sensitive laboratory test [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Red flags for cauda equina syndrome are acute bowel incontinence, urinary retention with a post-void residual greater than 200 mL, and saddle anesthesia; the pudendal nerve carries S2 through S4 [PEER-CS].

### Neuromuscular Weakness

| Pattern | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Fatigable weakness, ptosis, diplopia, worse at day's end | Myasthenia gravis |
| Weakness that improves with continued stimulation, with a perihilar mass | Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome |
| Ascending symmetric weakness with absent reflexes after diarrheal illness | Guillain-Barré syndrome |

- Myasthenia gravis is more common in women and has a bimodal onset; proximal weakness exceeds distal weakness. An ice pack test places ice on the affected eye for 2 minutes, and improvement of at least 2 mm in ptosis is positive [PEER-CS][PEER-PH].

### Movement and Toxic-Metabolic Disorders

- An acute dystonic reaction produces involuntary movements of the neck, eyes, mouth, or trunk; akathisia is an inner restlessness with an urge to move; tardive dyskinesia is a persistent hyperkinetic disorder of the face and tongue after long-term dopamine receptor-blocking drugs [ROSH][PEER-PH].

## Management

### Acute Ischemic Stroke

1. Check a fingerstick blood glucose; hypoglycemia reproduces focal deficits and is immediately reversible [ROSH][PEER-CS].
2. Establish the last known well time and perform a noncontrast CT of the head.
3. Give aspirin 324 mg once hemorrhage is excluded. If the CT is negative and large-vessel occlusion is suspected, obtain CT angiography of the head and neck [PEER-CS].
4. Assess eligibility for intravenous thrombolysis within 4.5 hours of onset. Absolute contraindications include a history of intracranial hemorrhage, ischemic stroke within 3 months, and gastrointestinal bleeding within 21 days; multilobar or extensive infarction on CT is also a contraindication, and an observed seizure at stroke onset with postictal deficits is relative [ROSH][PEER-PH].
5. Refer for mechanical thrombectomy; benefit is shown for large-vessel occlusion at 6 to 24 hours after onset, and 6 to 16 hours in selected patients [PEER-CS].

### Intracerebral and Subarachnoid Hemorrhage

1. Exclude hemorrhage before giving any antithrombotic, then control blood pressure with a titratable intravenous agent and obtain neurosurgical consultation.
2. In spontaneous ICH from hypertensive vasculopathy, give intravenous labetalol to a target systolic pressure of 140 mm Hg to limit hematoma expansion, and avoid anticoagulation or thrombolysis [ROSH].
3. In aneurysmal SAH, target a systolic blood pressure below 160 mm Hg and a mean arterial pressure below 110 mm Hg with nicardipine or labetalol. This reduces rebleeding but does not improve mortality or neurologic outcomes [ROSH][PEER-CS].
4. Give nimodipine, which improves mortality in aneurysmal SAH [ROSH].

### Status Epilepticus

1. Note the time of onset and check a bedside glucose. Status epilepticus is a seizure lasting more than 5 minutes, or recurrent seizures without return to baseline; it becomes more refractory with time and with each subsequent antiepileptic dose [PEER-CS][ROSH].
2. Give a benzodiazepine at full weight-based dose, and repeat it if the seizure continues; underdosing is common [PEER-CS].

| Agent | Dose | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Lorazepam | 0.1 mg/kg IV, maximum 4 mg | Not to exceed 2 mg/min |
| Diazepam | 0.15 mg/kg IV, up to 10 mg per dose | Alternative first-line |
| Midazolam | 10 mg IM | No IV access, weight over 40 kg |

3. Move to a second-line agent — fosphenytoin or levetiracetam. Give pyridoxine with the benzodiazepine when isoniazid ingestion is suspected, since these seizures are refractory to standard therapy, and exclude hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, hemorrhage, meningitis, metabolic derangement, and drug overdose [PEER-PH][ROSH][PEER-CS].
4. For a prolonged postictal state or persistent altered mentation, obtain electroencephalography for nonconvulsive status epilepticus; beta-lactam antibiotics are an associated trigger [PEER-CS][PEER-PH].

### Meningitis

1. Do not delay antibiotics for CT or lumbar puncture; delay increases mortality and neurologic deficits. Obtain blood cultures first if this causes no delay; the CSF culture is not significantly affected if obtained within 2 hours of antibiotics [PEER-CS][ROSH].
2. Give empiric ceftriaxone plus vancomycin, adding ampicillin for patients over 50 years, under 3 months, or immunocompromised, and vancomycin where ceftriaxone resistance exceeds 1%. Give dexamethasone just before or with the first antibiotic dose, then every 6 hours for 4 days; the effect is greatest in pneumococcal disease [PEER-PH][ROSH].
3. Perform head CT before lumbar puncture for focal deficit, immunocompromise, history of CNS disease, new-onset seizures, papilledema, altered mental status, or head trauma [PEER-PH][ROSH].
4. Give rifampin, ciprofloxacin, or ceftriaxone as chemoprophylaxis to close contacts of meningococcal meningitis [PEER-PH].

### Raised Intracranial Pressure and Shunt Failure

1. Tap the shunt reservoir and measure opening pressure. A pressure greater than 20 cm H2O indicates distal catheter obstruction, because CSF cannot drain into the peritoneum [PEER-PH].
2. Obtain a head CT. Slit ventricles indicate overshunting; ventricular enlargement with an intact shunt series suggests proximal obstruction [PEER-PH][ROSH].
3. Suspect shunt infection in the first month after placement; headache, nausea, vomiting, lethargy, or altered mental status may be the only findings and fever may be absent. Coagulase-negative staphylococci (50%) and Staphylococcus aureus (33%) predominate; give vancomycin plus an antipseudomonal beta-lactam [ROSH][PEER-PH].
4. Recognise paralysis of upgaze and the "setting sun" appearance as signs of raised intracranial pressure from shunt malfunction [PEER-PH].
5. For symptomatic hyponatremia below 120 mEq/L, give 100 mL of 3% sodium chloride over 10 minutes, repeatable twice more if seizures persist, and correct by 4 to 6 mEq/L in the first 2 to 4 hours and 10 to 12 mEq/L in the first 24 hours to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome [PEER-CS].

### Spinal and Neuromuscular Emergency

1. Obtain urgent MRI of the entire spine with gadolinium when epidural abscess or cord compression is suspected, and ask specifically about bowel incontinence, urinary retention with a post-void residual above 200 mL, and saddle anesthesia [PEER-PH][PEER-CS].
2. Refer for surgical decompression; epidural abscess causing cauda equina syndrome is a surgical emergency, compression must be relieved to prevent permanent deficit, and systemic antibiotics are given [PEER-CS].
3. For an acute demyelinating exacerbation, give methylprednisolone 500–1,000 mg IV daily for 3 to 7 days [PEER-CS][ROSH].
4. Measure the negative inspiratory force in myasthenia with concern for crisis. A value below 30 cm H2O warrants critical care admission, and −20 cm H2O with CO2 retention requires endotracheal intubation [PEER-CS][PEER-PH].
5. Give pyridostigmine for myasthenia. For Guillain-Barré syndrome give intravenous immune globulin or plasmapheresis, ideally within 7 days of onset but beneficial up to 30 days; corticosteroids have consistently failed to improve outcomes, and succinylcholine is contraindicated [PEER-PH][PEER-CS].

### Symptomatic and Antidotal Therapy

| Condition | Treatment |
|---|---|
| Cluster headache, acute | 100% oxygen at 12–15 L/min for 15 minutes; subcutaneous sumatriptan 6 mg |
| Cluster headache prevention | Verapamil |
| Idiopathic intracranial hypertension | Acetazolamide 250 mg twice daily |
| Giant cell arteritis | Glucocorticoids; intravenous methylprednisolone when vision is threatened |
| Acute dystonic reaction | Benztropine 1–2 mg IV or IM, or diphenhydramine 25–50 mg, for 3 days; diphenhydramine 50 mg IV for laryngeal dystonia |
| Wernicke encephalopathy | Thiamine 500 mg IV to optimise diffusion across the blood-brain barrier |
| Myxedema coma | Intravenous levothyroxine 200–400 mcg with hydrocortisone; fluids 20–30 mL/kg then norepinephrine for persistent hypotension |
| Febrile seizure | Antipyretic such as acetaminophen and supportive care |

- Cocaine and sympathomimetic toxicity: control agitation with a benzodiazepine such as midazolam 10 mg IM, and prepare for intubation, sedation, and paralysis when core temperature exceeds 41.1°C. For wide-complex tachycardia from sodium channel blockade give sodium bicarbonate 1 to 2 mg/kg IV as a bolus, redosing until the QRS narrows, then infuse while keeping serum pH below 7.55 [PEER-CS].
- Opioid toxicity: titrate naloxone to adequate respirations and oxygenation rather than full arousal. Lithium toxicity: dialysis is recommended if the level exceeds 4 mEq/L with impaired kidney function, or at any level with mental status change, seizure, or cardiac dysrhythmia. Carbon monoxide poisoning: remove from the source and give 100% oxygen by nonrebreather mask [PEER-CS][PEER-PH].
