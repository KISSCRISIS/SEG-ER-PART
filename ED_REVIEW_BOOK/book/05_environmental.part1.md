## High-Yield Exam Review

- Acute mountain sickness (AMS) is headache plus at least one of anorexia, nausea, vomiting, weakness or fatigue, dizziness, or lightheadedness after recent ascent to ≥ 8,000 ft (~2,440 m). [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Ataxia is the earliest and most specific finding of high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE); confusion, stupor, and coma mark the transition from AMS to HACE. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Immediate descent is the most important treatment for HACE and for high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE); dexamethasone is added in HACE, and a portable hyperbaric chamber simulates descent when weather blocks evacuation. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Acetazolamide prevents and treats AMS by causing bicarbonate diuresis and a non–anion gap metabolic acidosis, which raises ventilation and shortens acclimatization time. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- HAPE is a noncardiogenic pulmonary edema from hypoxic pulmonary artery vasoconstriction above 2,500 m (~8,200 ft) and is the leading cause of death in altitude illness. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Early HAPE is a dry cough with decreased exercise performance and dyspnea on exertion; pink frothy sputum and generalized rales are advanced disease. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Rapid ascent is the major risk factor for AMS, HACE, and HAPE; Down syndrome is an independent risk factor for HAPE in children. [ROSH]
- Middle ear barotrauma (barotitis, "ear squeeze") is the most common concern of SCUBA divers and is usually a disorder of descent; it is managed conservatively with analgesics, decongestants, and rest, with antibiotics added when the tympanic membrane ruptured. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Decompression sickness (DCS) presents within 6 hours of surfacing; type I involves musculoskeletal, skin, and lymphatic systems, type II the neurologic, cardiopulmonary, and abdominal systems. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Vertigo, ataxia, seizure, motor weakness, and "the chokes" are type II DCS and mandate emergent hyperbaric oxygen therapy. [PEER-PH]
- Arterial gas embolism is the most severe form of pulmonary barotrauma and the second-leading cause of death among sport divers; any focal deficit during or immediately after ascent is managed as arterial gas embolism with supine positioning, 100% oxygen, and hyperbaric recompression. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- A bubble study (transthoracic to transesophageal echocardiography with bubble contrast) is the gold standard for detecting the patent foramen ovale that predisposes to arterial air embolism. [PEER-PH]
- Ascent barotrauma causes pneumomediastinum and pneumothorax; pneumomediastinum with stable saturation and lung sliding on ultrasound is treated with supplemental oxygen and observation. [ROSH]
- Asystole is the most common immediately life-threatening arrhythmia after lightning strike; it is often transient, but medullary stunning leaves a prolonged respiratory arrest. [PEER-PH]
- Lightning triage is reversed from other mass-casualty incidents: pulseless, apneic victims are treated first because prolonged resuscitation is often successful. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Lichtenberg figures are pathognomonic for lightning injury, are not true thermal burns, and resolve spontaneously within 24 hours. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- A low-voltage (< 1,000 volts) shock that does not traverse the heart, with normal examination and normal ECG, can be discharged home. [PEER-PH]
- Drowning resuscitation prioritizes ventilation: rescue breaths come first, and rescue breath rate should not exceed 20 breaths/minute. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Four-sided pool fencing at least 4 feet high with a self-closing latch gate is the most effective measure to prevent childhood drowning. [PEER-PH]
- Heat stroke is a core temperature > 40°C (104°F) with central nervous system dysfunction; altered mental status separates it from heat exhaustion, and cold water immersion is the first-line cooling method. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Transaminase elevation is so common in classic heat stroke that its absence should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis. [ROSH]
- Below 32°C (89.6°F) hypothermia causes bradycardia, loss of shivering, and Osborn (J) waves; fixed and dilated pupils occur below 30°C (86°F) and do not indicate brain death. [ROSH]
- Resuscitation of primary hypothermia may be terminated when asystole persists despite raising core temperature above 32°C (89.6°F). [PEER-PH]
- Frostbite is rewarmed in gently circulating water at 37–39°C (98.6–102.2°F) for 15–30 minutes; massaging the limb is contraindicated. [ROSH]
- Crotalid (pit viper) venom causes local tissue damage, coagulopathy, and platelet dysfunction; elapid (coral snake) venom causes descending flaccid paralysis and warrants at least 24 hours of observation because neurotoxicity is delayed. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Antivenom is indicated for moderate-to-severe crotalid envenomation. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Hot water immersion treats stingray, lionfish, scorpion fish, and sea urchin envenomation; acetic acid (vinegar) is the initial therapy for box jellyfish stings. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Lymphocytes are preferentially damaged by ionizing radiation; the absolute lymphocyte count at 48 hours estimates absorbed dose and prognosis. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

## Core Concepts

### The organizing frame

- Environmental disorders arise from the physical environment — altitude, ambient pressure, heat, cold, electricity, radiation — and from animal toxins.

### Altitude illness

- AMS, HACE, and HAPE form the altitude spectrum; AMS is the most common high-altitude illness and is a clinical diagnosis.
- Onset is within a few hours of arrival, peak severity at 24–48 hours, resolution by the third or fourth day. [ROSH]
- The biggest risk factor is rapid ascent to a high absolute elevation; cardiovascular training status does not influence risk. [ROSH]
- Mild AMS is managed by halting ascent and resting until symptoms resolve; persistent symptoms forbid further ascent. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- HACE is the end-stage of AMS and is highly lethal; it is uncommon below 10,000 ft and most common above 13,000 ft (3,000 m). [ROSH]
- HAPE is the most lethal altitude illness but is easily reversible when recognized early, and it can occur regardless of risk factors. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- HAPE risk factors: rapid ascent, heavy exertion, a history of pulmonary hypertension, male sex, and use of a respiratory depressant. [ROSH]
- High-altitude DCS is separate from diving DCS: it occurs unpressurized above 18,000–25,000 ft (5,400–7,600 m), and above 30,000 ft (9,100 m) even pressurized cabins may not fully protect during rapid ascent. [ROSH]

### Dysbarism and diving disorders

- Diving injuries from dysbarism are classified as disorders of descent, of depth, and of ascent.
- Boyle law — pressure and volume of a gas are inversely related at constant temperature — explains every pressure injury here; Dalton law explains nitrogen loading during descent. [ROSH]
- At 33 feet of seawater, pressure is 2 atmospheres absolute and gas volume is 50% of surface volume. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Disorders of descent are barotrauma of gas-filled cavities, chiefly the middle ear; failure of eustachian tube equalization bends the tympanic membrane inward and causes pain and fullness. Barosinusitis is the second most common diver concern. [ROSH]
- Ascent barotrauma ranges from pneumomediastinum and pneumothorax to alveolar rupture with arterial gas embolism; the greater fractional change in gas volume occurs at shallow depths, and breath-holding is the precipitant. [ROSH]
- DCS results from inert gas coming out of solution as bubbles when ambient pressure falls; bubbles cause obstruction, ischemia, an inflammatory cascade, thrombosis, and capillary leak. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Type I DCS is musculoskeletal, cutaneous, and lymphatic; type II DCS is neurologic, cardiopulmonary, vascular, and abdominal and mandates hyperbaric oxygen. [PEER-PH]
- Air travel shortly after diving is a DCS risk factor; a patent foramen ovale predisposes to arterial air embolism. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Drowning

- Drowning is the process of respiratory impairment from submersion or immersion in liquid; it is a process, not an outcome, classified as fatal or nonfatal with and without morbidity. [PEER-PH]
- Only up to 4 mL/kg is aspirated because of glottis closure and laryngospasm; respiratory sequelae are the most common consequence of submersion. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Outcome is driven by the degree of central nervous system hypoxia, which is why duration of resuscitative effort predicts prognosis. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Cervical spine injury occurs in fewer than 0.5% of drowning victims, and routine immobilization can delay ventilation. [PEER-PH]

### Heat illness

- Heat-related illness is a spectrum: heat cramps, heat exhaustion, and heat stroke.
- Heat exhaustion is volume depletion in a hot environment, expressed as water depletion or salt depletion; water depletion predominates in older patients and salt depletion in those replacing losses with water or hypotonic fluid. [ROSH]
- It presents with headache, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, and muscle cramps without central nervous system dysfunction. [ROSH]
- Heat stroke is thermoregulatory failure; altered mental status is the defining discriminator from heat exhaustion. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Classic heat stroke affects those without the drive to compensate, typically older adults; exertional heat stroke affects healthy people generating excessive metabolic heat. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Cold illness

- Cold injury has freezing and nonfreezing syndromes, which may occur alone or with systemic hypothermia.
- Frostbite is the most common freezing injury; nonfreezing syndromes include immersion foot (trench foot) and chilblains (pernio). [ROSH]
- Chilblains follow repeated damp, nonfreezing exposure and are most common in women and children, in Raynaud phenomenon and lupus erythematosus, and in those without adequate heating or warm clothing. [ROSH]
- Hypothermia is graded by core temperature and by level of consciousness, movement, shivering, and alertness. [ROSH]
- Rewarming is passive (insulation, blankets), active external (forced air, heated blankets, radiant heat), or active core (heated humidified oxygen, warm intravenous fluids, bladder lavage). [ROSH]

### Electrical and lightning injury

- Severity depends on tissue resistance, duration of contact, current pathway, and the characteristics of the current. [ROSH]
- Bone offers the greatest resistance, so electricity travels through lower-resistance tissue and most electrical injuries are superficial. [ROSH]
- Wet skin offers little resistance, which is why flashover along wet skin dominates lightning injury. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Low-voltage exposure is under 1,000 volts (US household voltage is 120 volts); high-voltage alternating-current injuries may reach 200,000 volts and last seconds to minutes. [PEER-PH]
- Lightning is a unidirectional current of 10 million to 2 billion volts delivered over 10 μs to 3 ms, and most injuries are indirect — contact with a struck object, side flash, or ground current. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Radiation injury

- Dose is measured in gray (Gy); the rapidity of cell turnover determines organ sensitivity, and the hematopoietic and gastrointestinal systems are most sensitive. [ROSH]
- Hematopoietic injury begins at 1.5 Gy, gastrointestinal injury above 6 Gy, and cardiovascular or central nervous system injury above 20–30 Gy. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Lymphocytes are preferentially damaged and are the first hematopoietic cells to fall after whole-body penetrating irradiation. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Envenomation and bites

- Scorpion venom opens neuronal sodium channels, causing prolonged depolarization of somatic and autonomic nerve fibers; the bark scorpion (Centruroides) causes most symptomatic US envenomations. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Black widow (Latrodectus) venom acts through alpha-latrotoxin, triggering massive presynaptic release of acetylcholine, norepinephrine, and other catecholamines. [ROSH]
- Brown recluse (Loxosceles) venom contains hemolytic enzymes and vasoconstrictors; most bites resolve without consequence, but envenomation can produce a necrotic eschar and, rarely, acute hemolytic anemia. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Fire ant venom contains alkaloid toxins, chiefly solenopsin, causing immediate burning pain, inflammation, and sterile pustules. [ROSH]
- Tick paralysis is caused by a neurotoxin from the salivary glands of female ticks; symptoms appear 4–7 days after attachment. [ROSH]
- Pit vipers (rattlesnakes, copperheads, water moccasins) account for 98% of venomous snake bites in the United States; venom disrupts the coagulation cascade and increases capillary permeability. [ROSH]
- Elapid (coral snake) venom does not cause hemotoxicity; it produces descending flaccid paralysis resembling botulism, and bites may leave no visible mark. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Marine venoms are largely heat-labile proteins, which is why hot water immersion relieves stingray, scorpion fish, lionfish, and sea urchin envenomation. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
