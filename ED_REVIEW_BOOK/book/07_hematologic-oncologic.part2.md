## Pathophysiology

### Haemostasis and where it fails

- Clotting proceeds through the intrinsic pathway (factors XII, XI, IX and VIII), measured by the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), and the extrinsic pathway (factor VII with tissue factor), measured by the prothrombin time (PT) and reported as the INR. [PEER-PH]
- The liver synthesises almost all coagulation proteins, both procoagulant and anticoagulant; this is why liver disease simultaneously prolongs the PT and aPTT and creates a prothrombotic state. [PEER-PH]
- Advanced liver disease reduces protein C and antithrombin, the natural anticoagulants, so despite a bleeding tendency these patients are at increased risk of deep vein thrombosis. [PEER-PH]
- Congenital bleeding disorders map onto the pathway: factor VIII and factor IX deficiency prolong the aPTT, while von Willebrand factor deficiency prolongs the aPTT indirectly through the fall in its carrier-bound factor VIII. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Platelet function depends on von Willebrand factor bridging platelet adhesion at sites of endothelial injury; when the factor is absent or dysfunctional, primary haemostasis fails at mucosal surfaces. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Haemophilia

- Haemophilia A results from reduced quantity or function of factor VIII and haemophilia B from reduced factor IX, both X-linked recessive, so disease is expressed in males while carriers are usually asymptomatic. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Residual factor activity determines phenotype: below 1% causes spontaneous and traumatic bleeding from infancy, 1–5% allows life-threatening bleeding after minor trauma, and 20–30% may produce little or no bleeding until challenged. [ROSH]
- Repeated joint bleeds — classically elbows, knees and ankles — cause haemarthrosis, and muscle bleeding in a confined fascial compartment can cause compartment syndrome. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Intracranial haemorrhage is the leading cause of haemorrhagic death across all age groups, can occur spontaneously or after minimal trauma, and may be delayed after the initial injury. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### von Willebrand disease

- von Willebrand factor is synthesised and released by vascular endothelial cells, assists platelet adhesion, and circulates bound to factor VIII, preventing its proteolytic breakdown. [PEER-PH]
- Type 1 has a mild to moderate quantitative decrease and is the most common form, type 2 has a functionally defective factor, and type 3 has an absolute absence with potentially life-threatening bleeding. [ROSH]
- Because factor VIII survival depends on von Willebrand factor, moderate to severe disease lowers factor VIII levels and prolongs the PTT. [PEER-PH]
- Deficiency of the factor alone usually produces a mild disorder dominated by skin and mucosal bleeding; clinically significant bleeding is uncommon except in type 3. [PEER-PH]

### Disseminated intravascular coagulation

- An acquired disorder of inappropriate, diffuse activation of coagulation and fibrinolysis: tissue factor released into the circulation generates intravascular thrombin, deposits fibrin in small vessels, and consumes clotting factors and platelets. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- The result is simultaneous thrombosis and haemorrhage, with spontaneous gastrointestinal bleeding, petechiae, purpura and oozing from puncture sites. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Progressive thrombocytopenia is the most common abnormality, occurring despite platelets being an acute phase reactant that would otherwise be expected to rise. [PEER-PH]
- Fibrinolysis generates fibrin degradation products, so the D-dimer is elevated and is often the finding that separates DIC from other causes of thrombocytopenia with prolonged PT and aPTT. [PEER-PH]
- Infection is the most common trigger, with historical series attributing nearly 40% of cases to an infectious process and 20% of gram-negative septic patients developing DIC; other triggers are trauma, burns, shock, malignancy, obstetric complications such as amniotic fluid embolism and placental abruption, transfusion and surgery. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Fragmented red cells appear on the smear, and because red cells, platelets and white cells share a myeloid progenitor, the marrow's attempt to replace consumed lines can leave all three depressed. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Thrombotic microangiopathies

- TTP arises from severely reduced activity of ADAMTS13, the plasma protease that cleaves von Willebrand factor multimers; uncleaved ultra-large multimers cause widespread platelet activation and platelet-rich microthrombi. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Adults aged 30–50 are most commonly affected; associations include pregnancy, female sex, obesity, systemic lupus erythematosus, HIV, drugs, infection and inflammation. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Red cells are fragmented as they traverse partially occluded arterioles and capillaries, producing microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia with schistocytes, raised lactate dehydrogenase and reticulocytosis. [ROSH]
- HUS is the paediatric counterpart: Shiga toxin from *E. coli* activates platelets and renovascular cells 5–10 days after bloody diarrhoea, producing a predominantly renal picture. [PEER-PH]
- TTP is a medical emergency that is almost always fatal without prompt treatment and carries an untreated mortality approaching 100%. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Immune thrombocytopenia and marrow failure

- In ITP, autoantibodies against platelet membrane glycoproteins form antibody-platelet complexes that are cleared mainly by the spleen, shortening platelet survival with a normal marrow and no other cytopenia. [ROSH]
- Aplastic anaemia is pancytopenia with a hypocellular marrow and a low reticulocyte count; it may be congenital, as in Fanconi anaemia from defective DNA repair, or more commonly acquired through immune-mediated destruction of haematopoietic stem cells or direct toxic injury. [ROSH]
- Bone marrow suppression is the mechanism of drug-induced aplasia; implicated agents include phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproic acid, chloramphenicol and chemotherapy or radiation, and HIV and hepatitis are infectious causes. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Sickle cell disease

- A point mutation in the beta-globin gene substitutes a sickle beta chain; patients with sickle cell anaemia carry two sickle beta chains, while trait carriers have one normal and one sickle chain. [PEER-PH]
- Deoxygenation of haemoglobin S triggers polymerisation, red cell sickling, endothelial injury and ischaemia — the common pathway to vaso-occlusion and end-organ damage. [ROSH]
- Sickled cells have a shortened lifespan, so patients depend on high baseline haematopoiesis; anything that halts red cell production causes a rapid, profound haemoglobin fall. [ROSH]
- Bone is in a state of chronic trauma from marrow expansion and repetitive infarction, which produces avascular necrosis — most often of the femoral head — and chronic back pain in older patients. [PEER-PH]
- Repeated splenic microinfarction leaves the spleen functionally silent by about age 5, and splenic sequestration causes abrupt haemoglobin collapse in young children. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Sickled cells confer resistance to malaria, explaining the geographic distribution of the gene. [PEER-PH]

### Anaemias

- Iron deficiency results from chronic blood loss, nutritional insufficiency or malabsorption; in children, excessive cow's milk — more than 24 ounces per day — is the leading cause because of its low iron concentration and poor bioavailability. [ROSH]
- Megaloblastic anaemia reflects impaired DNA synthesis during erythropoiesis, so cells continue to grow without dividing, producing large red cells and hypersegmented neutrophils. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Folate stores last only about 3–4 months whereas hepatic vitamin B12 stores last 3–5 years, so folate deficiency develops first in a malnourished patient; isolated vitamin B12 deficiency additionally causes peripheral neuropathy, gait instability and cognitive change. [ROSH]
- Thalassaemias are hereditary haemoglobinopathies in which reduced globin chain production causes microcytic, hypochromic anaemia; beta chains are affected in beta-thalassaemia and any of the four alpha-globin genes in alpha-thalassaemia. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Beta-thalassaemia major presents around 6 months of age once fetal haemoglobin wanes and haemoglobin A production fails, and it requires regular transfusions. [PEER-PH]
- Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia arises when autoantibodies target antigens common to all human red cells rather than ABO antigens, probably through molecular mimicry after a viral infection or medication exposure. [PEER-PH]
- Intravascular haemolysis from turbulent flow through a narrowed or faulty valve — aortic stenosis, aortic or mitral regurgitation, hypertrophic subaortic stenosis — produces a normocytic anaemia with elevated red cell distribution width, reticulocytosis and low haptoglobin. [PEER-PH]

### Oxidative haemolysis and methaemoglobinaemia

- Glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD) generates the reduced glutathione that protects haemoglobin from oxidative injury; deficiency is X-linked and most prevalent in African, Asian and Mediterranean populations, and it is the most common red cell enzymopathy in humans. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Oxidative stress from drugs, foods or infection precipitates haemolysis, releasing haemoglobin that is oxidised into Heinz bodies and removed by the spleen. [ROSH]
- Methaemoglobinaemia oxidises haem iron from the ferrous (Fe2+) to the ferric (Fe3+) state, shifting the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve and impairing oxygen delivery, so cyanosis appears with a saturation around 85% that does not improve with supplemental oxygen. [ROSH]
- Infants under 4 months have an immature methaemoglobin reduction system, making them vulnerable to oxidising agents such as benzocaine. [PEER-PH]

### Marrow neoplasms and their complications

- Leukaemias are clonal proliferations of haematopoietic precursors; acute forms produce marrow failure with cytopenias and blasts, while chronic myelogenous leukaemia is a myeloproliferative neoplasm following a triphasic course. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Hyperleukocytosis causes leukostasis through intravascular sludging and microvascular occlusion with local tissue hypoxia, and the large, rigid, poorly deformable myeloblasts of acute myeloid leukaemia make it the highest-risk subtype. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Hodgkin lymphoma arises from germinal centre B cells that become Hodgkin and Reed-Sternberg cells, with Epstein-Barr virus implicated in transformation. [ROSH]
- Burkitt lymphoma is an aggressive B-cell non-Hodgkin lymphoma carrying a translocation of the MYC proto-oncogene on chromosome 8, with a rapid cell turnover that makes tumour lysis likely once treatment starts. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Multiple myeloma is a clonal plasma cell proliferation producing monoclonal immunoglobulin (M protein); accumulation in marrow stimulates osteoclast-mediated resorption, giving lytic lesions and hypercalcaemia, while light chain deposition in renal tubules causes kidney insufficiency. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Hypercalcaemia of malignancy arises through parathyroid hormone-related protein, bone metastasis, calcitriol production or ectopic parathyroid hormone. [PEER-PH]
- Hyperviscosity syndrome is elevated serum viscosity causing sludging, reduced microvascular perfusion and vascular stasis; it is most often due to elevated serum protein from Waldenström macroglobulinaemia and less often myeloma, and can also follow marked leukocytosis above 100,000 cells/mm3 or erythrocytosis. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Polycythaemia vera is a myeloproliferative disorder commonly driven by a JAK2 mutation, with overproduction of erythrocytes, granulocytes and platelets in the absence of a physiological stimulus such as hypoxia; symptoms follow from hyperviscosity and elevated histamine and uric acid. [PEER-PH]
- Tumour lysis syndrome follows massive tumour cell breakdown: potassium and phosphate spill from the cytosol, phosphate precipitates with calcium to cause hypocalcaemia, and nucleic acid metabolism generates uric acid; uric acid and calcium phosphate precipitation cause acute kidney injury. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- In neutropenic patients, bacteria and fungi translocate across damaged intestinal surfaces, and fever may be the only sign of a life-threatening infection. [ROSH]

### Inherited Thrombophilia

- Activated protein C resistance from the factor V Leiden mutation is the most commonly inherited hypercoagulable disorder: mutant factor V resists inactivation by activated protein C, so factor V activity and thrombin generation increase, predisposing to venous and, much less often, arterial thromboembolism. [ROSH]
- Macrophage activation syndrome is a secondary form of haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis and a life-threatening hyperinflammatory state with excessive macrophage and T cell activation, haemophagocytosis and multiorgan dysfunction, most often complicating systemic juvenile idiopathic arthritis. [ROSH]
