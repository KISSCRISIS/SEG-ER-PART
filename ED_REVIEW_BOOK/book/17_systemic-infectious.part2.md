## Pathophysiology

### The Sepsis Cascade

- Sepsis is a dysregulated host response to an infectious agent rather than a direct toxic effect of the organism; the resulting inflammatory response produces end-organ dysfunction and is the most common cause of death in medical and traumatic intensive care units worldwide. [ROSH]
- The clinical syndrome is identified through the systemic inflammatory response (SIRS) criteria or the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, neither of which is specific for infection. [ROSH]
- Shock becomes septic shock when hypotension persists despite adequate fluid resuscitation and requires vasopressors, in the presence of a lactate greater than 2 mmol/L. [ROSH]
- Steroids exert a permissive effect on blood pressure and upregulate alpha-1 receptors on the vasculature, increasing the effect of vasopressors — the rationale for stress-dose glucocorticoid use. [ROSH]

### Superantigen-Mediated Shock

- Staphylococcal toxic shock syndrome is driven by toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 (TSST-1) and enterotoxin type B; streptococcal toxic shock syndrome is driven by streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins produced by Streptococcus pyogenes. [PEER-PH]
- Both causative organisms are gram-positive, and both syndromes depend on toxin production rather than on bacterial invasion alone. [PEER-PH]
- These toxins act as superantigens, provoking massive cytokine production with a resulting systemic inflammatory state, increased vascular permeability, haemodynamic instability, coagulopathy, and multiorgan failure. [PEER-PH]
- Because the illness is toxin-mediated rather than simply bacterial, clindamycin — which inhibits exotoxin synthesis — is given alongside agents that kill the organism. [ROSH]
### Necrotizing Soft Tissue Infection

- Entry requires a breach in the skin, and risk is increased by diabetes, obesity, immunosuppression, and trauma. [PEER-PH]
- Anaerobic, low-oxygen tension within damaged tissue allows spore-forming organisms to convert to their vegetative form and produce exotoxin; this is the mechanism shared by clostridial myonecrosis (gas gangrene) and tetanus. [PEER-PH]
- Crepitus reflects gas production by gas-forming organisms such as Clostridium perfringens; group A Streptococcus and Enterobacteriaceae are also commonly implicated, and polymicrobial infection is usual. [PEER-PH]
- Gas gangrene is classified by microbiology: type I infections are polymicrobial and more common, while type II infections are monomicrobial and occur more often in healthy hosts with a history of trauma. [ROSH]
- Involvement of the perineum and groin is termed Fournier gangrene. [PEER-PH]
- Vibrio vulnificus is a gram-negative halophilic organism of warm brackish and saltwater; it causes disease through wound exposure to seawater or marine animals, or by ingestion of raw or undercooked seafood, particularly oysters, and can produce primary septicaemia without an obvious wound. [ROSH]
- Patients with chronic liver disease, especially cirrhosis, are disproportionately affected because impaired iron metabolism creates a high-iron environment in which the organism thrives and immune defence is reduced. [ROSH]
- Serious Vibrio vulnificus infection can produce a clinical picture equivalent to necrotizing fasciitis. [ROSH]

### Neurotoxin Syndromes

- Botulinum neurotoxin blocks acetylcholine release at the neuromuscular junction, producing a symmetric descending paralysis that begins with the cranial nerves and bulbar muscles and later involves the respiratory muscles. [PEER-PH]
- Death in botulism is usually due to hypoventilation and respiratory failure, not to the toxin itself; U.S. mortality is 5% to 8%. [PEER-PH]
- Toxin already bound at the neuromuscular junction is not reversed by antitoxin; recovery occurs gradually over months with supportive care. [PEER-PH]
- Tetanospasmin, produced by Clostridium tetani, reaches the spinal cord and brainstem by retrograde transmission in motor neurons and decreases release of the inhibitory neurotransmitters GABA and glycine at the anterior horn cells. [PEER-PH]
- Loss of this inhibition produces increased muscle tone, painful spasms, and a hypersympathetic state with hypertension, tachycardia, and diaphoresis. [PEER-PH]
- Strychnine, a rodenticide now used as an adulterant in heroin and cocaine, antagonises glycine neurotransmission in the spinal cord and therefore mimics tetanus clinically. [PEER-PH]
- Rabies virus replicates locally in muscle after inoculation, then enters peripheral nerves and travels to the central nervous system by retrograde axonal transport; the incubation period is 20–90 days, and once clinical signs begin the disease is nearly always fatal. [PEER-PH]
- Rabies is transmitted through the saliva of infected mammals; bat bites and scratches can be small and easily missed, and saliva contacting conjunctiva or oral mucosa is sufficient for transmission. [ROSH]
### Rickettsial and Tick-Borne Mechanisms

- Rickettsia rickettsii is a pleomorphic, obligate intracellular organism; its pathogenicity is mediated by endothelial cell invasion with subsequent vascular injury, which explains the rash, the centripetal spread, and the late features of renal, respiratory, and myocardial failure. [ROSH]
- Ehrlichia chaffeensis is a gram-negative, obligate intracellular, rickettsia-like coccobacillus that infects leukocytes; morulae are seen within monocytes on peripheral smear, and the haematological consequences — thrombocytopenia, leukopenia, and transaminitis — follow from this. [ROSH]
- Babesia microti is a protozoan that parasitises red blood cells, producing a malarial-like appearance on blood smear and, in severe cases, haemolytic anaemia, jaundice, thrombocytopenia, and disseminated intravascular coagulation. [PEER-PH]
- Borrelia burgdorferi, the spirochete of Lyme disease, is transmitted during tick feeding, approximately 2 days after attachment; erythema migrans is the clinical marker of early localised infection within 7–14 days, and untreated infection may later involve the heart or nervous system. [ROSH]
- Tick-borne relapsing fever is caused by spirochetes, most commonly Borrelia hermsii; the alternating febrile and afebrile periods reflect cycles of parasitaemia. [PEER-PH]
- Southern tick-associated rash illness mimics early localised Lyme disease, but no causative pathogen has been definitively identified. [ROSH]

### Malaria and Other Parasites

- Sporozoites are injected into the bloodstream by an infected mosquito, invade hepatic parenchymal cells within hours, and begin asexual reproduction; the liver is therefore involved in the human life cycle. [ROSH]
- The undulating, cyclic fever is associated with the timing of haemolysis and systemic parasite release: tertian (48-hour) cycles with P. falciparum, P. vivax, and P. ovale, quartan (72-hour) cycles with P. malariae, and a roughly 24-hour quotidian cycle with P. knowlesi. [PEER-PH]
- Necator americanus larvae penetrate intact skin — typically bare feet in contact with faeces-contaminated soil — then enter the bloodstream, travel to the lungs, ascend the trachea, are swallowed, and mature into adult worms in the small intestine, where they attach and feed on host blood; the resulting chronic blood loss produces microcytic anaemia. [ROSH]
- Strongyloides stercoralis is a nematode that infests the small intestine; in elderly and immunocompromised patients it can cause fatal hyperinfestation, with large numbers of migrating larvae producing septicemia, pneumonia, meningitis, or ileus. [PEER-PH]
- Cryptosporidium is a protozoan that causes severe, prolonged diarrhoeal disease in patients with weakened immune systems, particularly HIV infection. [ROSH]
- Toxoplasma gondii is a protozoan parasite acquired from cat faeces; in immunocompromised hosts it produces encephalitis, and it is an AIDS-defining illness. [ROSH]
- Entamoeba histolytica causes dysentery distinguished from ordinary diarrhoea by visible blood in the stool, and can seed the liver to produce an amebic hepatic abscess. [PEER-PH]

### Fungal Pathogenesis

- Cryptococcus neoformans is an encapsulated yeast that produces meningitis most often in advanced AIDS; its large capsule is the basis of the India ink stain finding. [ROSH]
- Mucormycosis is a life-threatening fungal infection seen in immunocompromised patients and in diabetes; additional risk factors include deferoxamine and corticosteroids, and the orbital and facial regions are commonly affected with pain and vision loss. [ROSH]
- Coccidioides is a dimorphic fungus acquired by inhaling airborne particles after soil disturbance; primary pulmonary infection may resemble pneumonia that fails to respond to standard antibiotics, and dissemination can involve the nervous system. [PEER-PH]
- Histoplasmosis, coccidioidomycosis, and blastomycosis can all be radiographically indistinguishable from pulmonary tuberculosis. [ROSH]
- Invasive aspergillosis occurs in profoundly neutropenic patients, such as those undergoing induction chemotherapy for acute myeloid leukemia. [ROSH]
- Sporothrix schenckii is a dimorphic fungus inhabiting soil, decaying vegetation, and plant matter such as rose thorns and sphagnum moss; after inoculation and a 1–12 week incubation it spreads along lymphatic channels, producing nodules in a linear distribution. [ROSH]
- Mycobacterium marinum is a nontuberculous mycobacterium associated with skin and soft tissue injury exposed to marine environments; it is resistant to several typical antimycobacterial drugs, so treatment should be reserved for culture-confirmed cases. [PEER-PH]

### Bacterial Invasion, Immunity, and Transmission

- Neisseria meningitidis is spread by droplets or close contact and attaches to and enters nasopharyngeal epithelial cells; complement deficiency and asplenia increase risk. [PEER-PH]
- Neisseria gonorrhoeae is a gram-negative diplococcus transmitted sexually; in women infection is frequently asymptomatic and often coexists with chlamydia, and untreated infection leads to pelvic inflammatory disease with tubal inflammation and damage. [PEER-PH]
- Chlamydial infection in women is often asymptomatic, which delays diagnosis and permits episodes of pelvic inflammatory disease or tubo-ovarian abscess; the resulting tubal inflammation and damage are associated with a higher risk of ectopic pregnancy and infertility. [PEER-PH]
- Treponema pallidum replicates at the inoculation site after an incubation of 10–90 days to produce the chancre of primary syphilis; central nervous system invasion occurs in almost one-half of cases even during early infection, although most such patients remain asymptomatic. [ROSH]
- Staphylococcus aureus commonly colonises skin and mucous membranes, and vascular catheters provide a direct pathway for inoculation into the bloodstream; repeated vascular access and skin barrier disruption make catheter-related bloodstream infection a major route of invasion in haemodialysis patients. [ROSH]
- Multidrug-resistant organisms such as ESBL-producing Escherichia coli are transmitted primarily by contact, most commonly via the hands of health care personnel. [ROSH]
- Early neonatal sepsis follows vertical transmission of bacterial infection via infected amniotic fluid; group B Streptococcus colonises the birth canal and passes to the infant during labour and delivery, and less frequently spreads haematogenously from the mother. [PEER-PH]
- Chorioamnionitis is a polymicrobial infection of the amniotic membranes and fluid, usually occurring after membrane rupture; length of labour and duration of membrane rupture are the greatest risk factors. [PEER-PH]
- Parvovirus B19 causes transient aplastic crisis in children with haematological abnormalities such as sickle cell anaemia or hereditary spherocytosis through a marked reduction of erythroid cell precursors. [ROSH]
- Epstein-Barr virus is a double-stranded DNA virus transmitted through saliva with an incubation period of up to 50 days; it infects B lymphocytes, and splenomegaly — present in up to 50% of cases — is the substrate for the complication of splenic rupture. [PEER-PH]
- Subacute sclerosing panencephalitis is a fatal complication of measles occurring 7–10 years after the initial infection and is thought to be associated with a variant of the measles virus in those who are not vaccinated. [ROSH]
- Acute disseminated encephalomyelitis is a rare post-infectious autoimmune process that can complicate measles. [PEER-PH]
- Prior dengue infection increases the risk of developing severe dengue. [PEER-PH]
- Hantavirus is transmitted by inhalation of dried rodent excrement or, less commonly, a rodent bite; after a 3–4 day prodromal flu-like phase, it rapidly produces hantavirus pulmonary syndrome with pulmonary oedema, hypoxia, and hypotension. [PEER-PH]
- Sin Nombre virus is a rodent-borne RNA virus whose most common host is the deer mouse; rodent control is therefore the primary prevention strategy. [PEER-PH]
- Influenza severity and spread follow a predictable epidemiological sequence: a localised outbreak, then an epidemic as it spreads regionally, then a pandemic once it reaches multiple countries and continents. [PEER-PH]
- Oseltamivir inhibits neuraminidase, a viral enzyme involved in releasing new virions from host cell surfaces; amantadine and rimantadine resistance in influenza reaches up to 90%. [PEER-PH]
