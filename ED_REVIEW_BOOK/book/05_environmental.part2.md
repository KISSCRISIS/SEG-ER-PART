## Pathophysiology

### Hypobaric hypoxia and altitude illness

- As barometric pressure falls with altitude, the partial pressure of oxygen falls with it; at ≥ 8,000 feet the environment becomes hypoxic and altitude syndromes can occur. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Hypoxemia is the principal driver of acute mountain sickness (AMS): fluid retention and cerebral hypoxia produce mild symptomatic cerebral edema. [PEER-PH]
- AMS and high-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) share one underlying pathophysiology and lie on a continuum of the same disease process; rate of ascent and absolute altitude are the dominant risk factors. [PEER-PH]
- In HACE, cerebral vasodilation raises cerebral blood flow and blood volume; transcapillary leakage follows from a leaky blood-brain barrier, from loss of autonomic autoregulation, increased vascular permeability, or both. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- The result is vasogenic cerebral edema with raised intracranial pressure, which untreated may progress to cerebral herniation. [ROSH]
- The cerebellum is very sensitive to hypoxia, which is why ataxia is the earliest and most specific sign of HACE; the syndrome is uncommon below 10,000 feet (3,000 m) and most common above 13,000 feet (4,000 m). [ROSH]
- High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is a noncardiogenic pulmonary edema: patchy hypoxic pulmonary artery vasoconstriction leaves some capillary beds overperfused, raising hydrostatic capillary pressure and driving protein-rich fluid across a broken alveolar barrier, sometimes with alveolar hemorrhage. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- At altitude, alveolar sodium channel function is reduced, further inhibiting fluid clearance from the lungs; the net lesion is pulmonary microvascular hypertension. [ROSH]
- HAPE is generally preceded by AMS and develops over roughly 2 to 4 days, although a rapid and intense insult can shorten that interval. [PEER-PH]

### Gas laws and pressure injury

- Boyle law governs every barotrauma: at constant temperature, the volume of a given quantity of gas varies inversely with pressure. [ROSH]
- Dalton law governs inert gas loading: the partial pressure of each component of a gas mixture increases proportionally with increasing ambient pressure, so nitrogen becomes pressurized and dissolves in tissues during descent. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- A water column of 33 feet exerts 2 atmospheres absolute, at which gas volume is 50% of its surface volume; because the fractional change in volume is greatest near the surface, expansion injury is disproportionately a hazard of the last few meters of ascent. [ROSH]
- On descent, gas volumes shrink: middle-ear gas volume decreases, the tympanic membrane bows inward, and pain and fullness follow. The medial third of the eustachian tube collapses when equalization fails. [ROSH]
- Rupture of the tympanic membrane exposes the middle ear to cold water, and caloric stimulation then produces transient nystagmus and vertigo; most perforations heal spontaneously within 4 weeks. [ROSH]

### Decompression sickness and arterial gas embolism

- DCS is the liberation of gas from tissues as small bubbles when ambient pressure falls; bubbles precipitate an inflammatory cascade and cause mechanical obstruction and ischemia. [ROSH]
- Nitrogen dissolved in tissues during descent comes out of solution during ascent and exits as bubbles. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Risk factors are increasing depth and length of dives, rapidity of ascent, obesity, male sex, a patent foramen ovale, and air travel shortly after diving. [ROSH]
- Arterial gas embolism is the most severe form of pulmonary barotrauma and, among sport divers, the second-leading cause of death after drowning. [ROSH]
- Rapid ascent without exhaling, or breath-holding, overexpands the lungs and ruptures alveoli; gas crosses the alveolar-capillary membrane into the pulmonary venous circulation, passes through the left atrium and ventricle, and embolizes systemically, most seriously to the coronary and cerebral arteries. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Bubbles may also reach the arterial side through an intracardiac shunt such as a patent foramen ovale or through pulmonary arteriovenous shunts, or may develop spontaneously on the arterial side. [PEER-PH]
- Gas can track along the bronchovascular sheath into the mediastinum, producing pneumomediastinum, or into the pleural space, producing pneumothorax; air trapping from asthma or COPD increases the risk. [ROSH]
- DCS can also follow air travel soon after diving, because reduced cabin pressure adds a further pressure drop; delayed onset is possible after flying within 24 hours. [ROSH]

### Drowning

- Submersion first produces breath-holding and apnea; retained carbon dioxide causes hypercapnia and respiratory acidosis, and hypoxemia follows. [PEER-PH]
- Panic before loss of consciousness disrupts normal breathing, causes extreme physical exertion, and leads to aspiration of water and bronchospasm. [ROSH]
- Fatal drowning results from apnea induced by loss of consciousness in the setting of hypoxemia. [ROSH]
- Tissue hypoxemia after nonfatal drowning is responsible for cerebral edema, arrhythmias, and renal failure; the cerebral edema raises intracranial pressure, and measures to lower it — mannitol, hypertonic saline, mechanical hyperventilation — have not proven beneficial. [PEER-PH]
- Aspiration triggers pulmonary edema and acute respiratory distress syndrome with bronchospasm and dyspnea; beta agonists are directed at the bronchospasm. [PEER-PH]
- Cold water immersion increases the QT interval, which can provoke dysrhythmia in patients with an underlying prolonged QT syndrome — a recognized trigger of unexplained fatal drowning. [PEER-PH]

### Heat illness

- The hypothalamus balances heat production from cellular metabolism, heat accumulation from the environment, and heat dissipation; heat illness results from impaired thermoregulation, redistributed blood volume, or unreplaced insensible water and salt losses. [ROSH]
- As heat stores rise, intracranial pressure increases and cerebral blood flow falls, which is the basis of the neurologic dysfunction of heat stroke. [ROSH]
- Heat exhaustion is volume depletion in a hot environment. Water depletion is essentially severe dehydration within a hot environment; salt depletion follows sweat losses inadequately replaced by fluids containing too little or no salt. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Hyperventilation in heat exhaustion continues even after moving to a cooler location; the resulting respiratory alkalosis causes carpopedal spasm and paresthesias — hyperventilation tetany. [PEER-PH]
- Classic heat stroke occurs when environmental heat and humidity make heat gain overwhelm native heat loss, so exertion is not required; exertional heat stroke occurs when working muscle generates excessive metabolic heat. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Cold injury

- Cold exposure triggers vasoconstriction to conserve heat; as tissue temperature falls below 10°C, cutaneous sensation is compromised. [PEER-PH]
- Microvascular vasoconstriction causes plasma to leak into the interstitial space; ice crystals begin to form as the temperature approaches 0°C. [PEER-PH]
- Once crystals form, intracellular osmolarity rises and cells collapse and die; blood flow then sludges, with stasis and cessation of capillary flow. [PEER-PH]
- Frostbite occurs when tissue temperature drops below −4°C and results from microvascular thrombosis and ice crystal formation with structural cellular damage; crystals form extracellularly and produce intracellular dehydration. [ROSH]
- Frostbite is seen when ambient temperatures fall below −12°C (10.4°F) and wind speeds exceed 10 mph. [ROSH]
- Nonfreezing immersion injuries such as immersion foot and chilblains arise from impaired circulatory regulation with direct damage to the microcirculation; the first symptom is loss of sensation, typically complete anesthesia. [PEER-PH]
- Cold water immersion syndrome is a continuum of stages of the body's response to water below 21–25°C. Short-term immersion, roughly 3–30 minutes, produces superficial neuromuscular cooling and muscle fatigue, most marked in the arms because of their high surface area-to-mass ratio; function falls as muscle temperature drops below 25°C. [PEER-PH]
- Peri-rescue collapse can follow sudden vasodilation with loss of hydrostatic support on removal from water, or "afterdrop," in which core temperature continues to fall after rescue and can precipitate cardiac arrest. [ROSH]
- One of the first cardiac effects of hypothermia is bradycardia from decreased firing of pacemaker cells; Osborn waves represent abnormal ion flux with delayed depolarization and early repolarization of the left ventricular wall. As temperature falls further, the ECG shows progressive PR, then QRS, then QTc prolongation. [ROSH]

### Electrical and lightning injury

- Which structures the current traverses depends on tissue resistance. Bone offers the greatest resistance to electrical flow; thick dry skin also resists current, so most electrical injuries remain superficial, whereas wet skin offers little resistance. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Lightning produces an extremely high-intensity burst of direct current of very brief duration — 10 million to 2 billion volts over 10 μs to 3 ms. [PEER-PH]
- Because current preferentially travels across wet skin, flashover produces cutaneous injury: Lichtenberg figures from the showering of electrons with extravasation of red blood cells, flash burns, punctate burns, and blistering. [PEER-PH]
- Lichtenberg figures are not true thermal burns and resolve spontaneously within 24 hours. Contact burns are true thermal burns produced when electricity heats metal that is against the skin, and their severity depends on the surface area of the contacting object. [PEER-PH]
- Rapid myocardial depolarization causes asystole, which is often transient as automaticity restarts; simultaneous depolarization and stunning of the medullary respiratory center causes a respiratory arrest that does not recover as quickly, and the resulting prolonged hypoxia can degenerate into ventricular fibrillation. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Radiation injury

- Ionizing radiation is energy released from unstable atoms as they decay; it breaks chemical bonds, forms ion pairs, cleaves DNA strands, and generates free radicals. [ROSH]
- Absorbed dose is expressed in gray (Gy). Organ sensitivity is largely determined by the rapidity of cell turnover, making the hematopoietic and gastrointestinal systems the most sensitive. [ROSH]
- The hematopoietic system is injured at the lowest doses, from 1.5 Gy; gastrointestinal injury predominates above 6 Gy; cardiovascular and central nervous system injury require more than 20–30 Gy. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Lymphocytes are the most radiosensitive circulating cells and the first hematopoietic line to fall, often within 8 hours; red cells are relatively resistant because they lack nuclear material. [PEER-PH]

### Venom pathophysiology

- Scorpion venom opens neuronal sodium channels, producing prolonged depolarization of somatic and autonomic fibers, which explains restlessness, hypersalivation, jerking movements, and oculomotor dysfunction. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Black widow venom's alpha-latrotoxin triggers massive presynaptic release of acetylcholine, norepinephrine, and other catecholamines, giving autonomic instability with severe muscle pain and abdominal rigidity. [ROSH]
- Brown recluse venom contains hemolytic enzymes and vasoconstrictors; the bite is painless, with burning pain and a white area of surrounding vasoconstriction 3–4 hours later, then a central bleb and necrotic eschar. Hemolysis can cause acute hemolytic anemia. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Africanized honeybees are far more aggressive than North American honeybees, and the resulting massive sting burden can cause rhabdomyolysis and acute kidney injury. [PEER-PH]
- Tick paralysis is caused by a neurotoxin released from the salivary glands of female ticks; ascending weakness begins in the lower extremities and moves upward over hours to days, and respiratory depression can be fatal. [ROSH]
- Crotalid venom causes local tissue injury, hematologic abnormalities, and neuromuscular effects; it disrupts the coagulation cascade and increases capillary permeability, so swelling spreads proximally with petechiae, ecchymosis, and hemorrhagic bullae that may progress to necrosis. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Elapid venom causes no hemotoxicity; it produces descending flaccid paralysis similar to botulism, and shorter fangs with enclosed venom channels make dry bites more frequent than with pit vipers. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Marine venoms are largely heat-labile proteins, which is the basis for hot water immersion; jellyfish nematocysts are harpoon-like structures that discharge on mechanical or osmotic stimulation to inject venom subcutaneously. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Sea urchin spines break off and introduce debris that acts as a nidus for infection, often with Vibrio species. [ROSH]
