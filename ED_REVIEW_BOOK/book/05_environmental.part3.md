## Diagnosis

### High-altitude illness

- Altitude illness is a clinical diagnosis; no laboratory test confirms it. The trigger is recent ascent in a lowland resident, usually above 2,440 m (8,000 ft). [ROSH][PEER-PH]

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Headache plus anorexia, nausea, vomiting, weakness, dizziness, or sleep disturbance | Acute mountain sickness (AMS) |
| Ataxia, confusion, stupor, or coma | High-altitude cerebral edema (HACE) |
| Dry cough and falling exercise tolerance, then dyspnea at rest, tachycardia, cyanosis, rales, pink frothy sputum | High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) |
| Patchy central edema on chest x-ray, worse on the right | HAPE |

- Ataxia is the most specific early marker of HACE and may be subtle; any new gait disturbance is a red flag even without mental status change. Vital signs are typically normal in AMS. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Diving disorders

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Ear pain, hearing loss, tinnitus, or transient vertigo during descent; hemotympanum or bloody otorrhea | Middle ear barotrauma (barotitis, "ear squeeze"), with perforation if bloody |
| Crepitus of neck and chest, odynophagia, chest pain after surfacing, preserved lung sliding on ultrasound | Pneumomediastinum from ascent barotrauma |
| Dyspnea and pleuritic chest pain partway through ascent, no neurologic symptoms | Pneumothorax from ascent barotrauma |
| Focal deficit, hemiplegia, aphasia, or seizure during or immediately after ascent | Arterial gas embolism |
| Joint and muscle pain, pruritus, mottling, extremity edema within 6 hours of surfacing | Type I decompression sickness (DCS) |
| Vertigo, ataxia, dyspnea, chest pain, or abdominal symptoms | Type II DCS, mandating hyperbaric oxygen |
| Dyspnea, chest pain, and dry cough after diving plus air travel | Pulmonary DCS ("the chokes") |

- Middle ear barotrauma is the most common disorder of divers and the most common aural barotrauma; barosinusitis is second. [ROSH]
- Neurologic onset during ascent favors arterial gas embolism; onset within 6 hours of surfacing with musculoskeletal or cutaneous symptoms favors DCS. A patent foramen ovale is shown by bubble contrast echocardiography, not by chest x-ray. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Drowning

- Classified as fatal or nonfatal, the latter with and without morbidity; the process is respiratory impairment from submersion or immersion. [PEER-PH]
- Increased respiratory rate and work of breathing with adventitious lung sounds are almost universal after nonfatal drowning; wheezing with a normal chest x-ray reflects aspiration pneumonitis. [ROSH]
- Prognosis tracks the degree of central nervous system hypoxia; more than 30 minutes of CPR and no bystander CPR indicate a worse outcome. [ROSH]
- Consider an underlying arrhythmia syndrome in unexplained drowning; cold water immersion lengthens the QT interval. [PEER-PH]

### Heat illness

| Feature | Heat exhaustion | Heat stroke |
|---|---|---|
| Mental status | Normal | Altered — confusion, ataxia, seizures, coma |
| Core temperature | Not defining | > 40°C (104°F) |
| Other | Headache, dizziness, nausea, vomiting, cramps, dehydration, hyperventilation tetany | Multiorgan dysfunction; liver injury is common |

- Hyperventilation tetany — carpopedal spasm and paresthesias from respiratory alkalosis — is consistent with heat exhaustion. [PEER-PH]
- Transaminase elevation is so common in classic heat stroke that its absence should prompt reconsideration of the diagnosis. [ROSH]

### Cold injury and hypothermia

| Core temperature | Findings |
|---|---|
| Mild, 32–35°C | Awake and alert, shivering, impaired motor function, tachycardia |
| Moderate, 28–32°C | Loss of shivering, progressive confusion and lethargy, bradycardia |
| Severe, < 28°C | Obtundation, life-threatening dysrhythmias |

- Osborn (J) waves are upward deflections at the QRS–ST junction, appear below 32°C, and vary inversely in height with temperature; atrial fibrillation, atrioventricular blocks, and progressive PR, then QRS, then QTc prolongation follow. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Fixed and dilated pupils occur at 30°C (86°F) and below and must not be mistaken for brain death; shivering ceases below 32°C (89.6°F). [ROSH]
- Nonfreezing cold immersion injury begins with loss of sensation, typically complete anesthesia; chilblains are edematous, red or purple, painful or pruritic lesions of the fingers, toes, ears, and lower legs. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Tick paralysis presents as acute ascending weakness with paresthesias and is found on a thorough skin examination including the scalp. [ROSH]

### Electrical and lightning injury

| Lesion | Mechanism |
|---|---|
| Lichtenberg figure (ferning) | Electron showering with red cell extravasation; not a true burn, resolves within 24 hours |
| Flash burn | Arc of electricity without direct contact; mild erythema or corneal injury on fluorescein staining |
| Contact burn | Electricity heats metal against skin; a true thermal burn |
| Punctate burns and blistering | Flashover across wet skin |

- Lichtenberg figures are pathognomonic for lightning injury; paralysis of blue, mottled lower extremities is common and typically transient, and external trauma may be absent despite cardiac or respiratory arrest. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Wet skin conducts readily, whereas thick dry skin resists current, so electricity follows lower-resistance tissue and most electrical injuries remain superficial. [ROSH]

### Radiation injury

- Lymphopenia is the earliest laboratory indicator of biological damage from whole-body penetrating irradiation; the absolute lymphocyte count at 48 hours estimates absorbed dose and prognosis. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Hematopoietic injury begins at 1.5 Gy and gastrointestinal injury above 6 Gy. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Envenomation

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Pinprick then redness and swelling, with dull cramping spreading to the whole body; abdominal rigidity, diaphoresis, hypertension, vomiting | Black widow (Latrodectus) |
| Motor restlessness, hypersalivation, jerking limb movements, opsoclonus, nystagmus, tongue fasciculations; local numbness and weakness | Bark scorpion (Centruroides) |
| Painless bite, then burning pain with a white vasoconstricted center, central bleb, and later a necrotic eschar; painless dark urine if hemolysis follows | Brown recluse (Loxosceles) |
| Fang marks, progressive edema, severe pain, bulla at the site, elevated PT, hypofibrinogenemia, thrombocytopenia | Crotalid (pit viper) envenomation |
| Minimal pain and skin findings, sometimes no bite marks, then diplopia, ptosis, dysphagia, drooling, slurred speech, drowsiness | Elapid (coral snake) |
| Triangle head, elliptical pupils, nasal pit, long folding fangs, single row of subcaudal plates | Pit viper |
| Linear or multiple puncture wounds in shallow tropical water with severe pain and discoloration persisting beyond 24 hours | Stingray, scorpion fish, or sea urchin with retained spines |

- Pit vipers account for 98% of US venomous snake bites; hematologic workup includes a serum fibrinogen level. Elapid bites cause no hemotoxicity and may leave no mark. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- X-rays detect more than 90% of radiopaque foreign bodies larger than 1 mm but are poor at detecting wood or thorns. [ROSH]

### Mammalian bites

| Feature | Implication |
|---|---|
| Cat, human, or monkey bite | Higher infection risk than dog bite; cat bites infect in up to 50% |
| Puncture wound, hand or foot, poor blood supply, crush injury, presentation after 12 hours, immunosuppression | Increased infection risk |
| Capnocytophaga canimorsus, a gram-negative rod | Fulminant bacteremic illness 1–14 days after a dog bite with fatal multiorgan failure, especially in splenectomized or immunosuppressed patients |

- Typical bite wound flora includes Pasteurella multocida, Bartonella henselae, Staphylococcus, Streptococcus, Fusobacterium, and anaerobes. [ROSH]
- An insect bite to a child's eyelid may cause preseptal cellulitis; painful limitation of extraocular movements, ophthalmoplegia, and proptosis indicate orbital cellulitis instead. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

## Management

### Time-critical pathways

1. **Arterial gas embolism.** Supine position, 100% oxygen, circulatory support, then hyperbaric recompression. For suspected air embolism during hemodialysis, clamp the venous blood line first. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
2. **Type II decompression sickness.** Inner ear or neurologic findings (vertigo, ataxia, seizure, motor weakness, sensory change, bowel or bladder change) and cardiopulmonary, vascular, or abdominal symptoms mandate emergent hyperbaric oxygen. [PEER-PH]
3. **High-altitude illness.** Descend at least 1,000 m (~3,300 ft) immediately for HACE and HAPE; add dexamethasone and oxygen in HACE. Mild AMS is treated by limiting ascent and resting. Acetazolamide prophylaxis suits rapid ascent above 2,500 m (~8,200 ft) and acts through a mild metabolic acidosis that speeds acclimatization. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
4. **Lightning strike.** Reverse triage: treat pulseless and apneic victims first and begin compressions immediately, because prolonged resuscitation is often successful. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
5. **Heat stroke.** Remove from the hot environment, resuscitate, and cool immediately; cold water immersion is preferred for exertional heat stroke, effective even below 17°C (62.6°F). Prehospital cooling uses cool mist with airflow, wet towels, or ice, with normal saline for hypotension. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
6. **Hypothermia.** Mild hypothermia needs passive or external warming only; moderate and severe hypothermia need active and passive rewarming — forced air blankets, warm intravenous fluids, warm saline bladder irrigation, heated humidified oxygen. Stop resuscitation only when asystole persists above a core temperature of 32°C (89.6°F). [ROSH][PEER-PH]
7. **Drowning.** Ventilation first: two rescue breaths once the scene is safe, then CPR, at no more than 20 breaths/minute. Routine cervical immobilization delays ventilation. Titrate oxygen to 92% to 96% saturation, give inhaled beta agonists for bronchospasm, and observe mild cases 4–6 hours. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
8. **Frostbite.** In the field, remove wet clothing, cover with bulky dry padding, and splint; do not rewarm if refreezing is possible. Rewarm rapidly in gently circulating water at 37–39°C (98.6–102.2°F) for 15–30 minutes until pliable and erythematous, with analgesia; massage and direct ice are contraindicated. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Antivenom and envenomation therapy

| Agent | Therapy |
|---|---|
| Pit viper (crotalid) | Antivenom for moderate to severe toxicity — 4–6 vials of Crotalidae polyvalent immune Fab or 10 vials of Crotalidae immune equine F(ab)2 — to halt swelling and reverse thrombocytopenia |
| Coral snake (elapid) | Admit at least 24 hours, reassess pulmonary function frequently, give antivenin for neurotoxicity |
| Bark scorpion | Centruroides immune antigen-binding fragment for severe envenomation; opsoclonus guides treatment in infants |
| Black widow | Parenteral opioids, benzodiazepines for spasm, antiemetics, tetanus prophylaxis |
| Brown recluse | Wound care and return precautions; no treatment prevents necrosis, so allow edges to demarcate before grafting. Hemolytic anemia: acetaminophen 15 mg/kg and a 20 mL/kg intravenous fluid bolus |
| Fire ant, tick | Local wound care, ice, oral analgesia, antihistamines; remove the tick |

- Marine injuries: hot — not scalding — water immersion for stingray, scorpion fish, lionfish, and sea urchin, watching closely for skin burns; acetic acid (vinegar) for box jellyfish. Seawater puncture wounds need irrigation, exploration for retained spines, antibiotics covering marine organisms, and tetanus prophylaxis. [PEER-PH][ROSH]

### Mammalian bite management

1. Control hemorrhage, inspect for deeper structure injury, and irrigate copiously with high-pressure saline. [ROSH]
2. X-ray when the bite is over a joint, is a puncture wound, is grossly contaminated, or when fracture or retained foreign body is suspected. [ROSH]
3. Close primarily only if the wound is under 12 hours old (24 hours on the face), on the face or scalp, a simple laceration, in a healthy host; hand and distal extremity bites heal by secondary intention. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
4. Amoxicillin-clavulanate is the standard antibiotic, with clindamycin plus ciprofloxacin as an alternative; a healthy adult with a hand puncture wound can be treated with oral amoxicillin-clavulanate for 3 days. [ROSH]
5. Reserve prophylaxis for high-risk wounds, such as a diabetic patient with a closed 3-cm lower-leg bite from an unknown dog. [ROSH]

### Prevention and disposition

- Four-sided pool fencing at least 4 feet high with a gate and self-closing latch is the most effective measure to prevent childhood drowning. [PEER-PH]
- After lightning strike, discharge a well-appearing patient with normal laboratory results and no ECG changes; admit for persistent muscle pain, neurologic deficits, dysrhythmias, or signs of arterial spasm such as nonpalpable pulses or elevated troponin. [PEER-PH]
- Low-voltage electrical injury with a normal examination and ECG that did not traverse the heart is discharged with return precautions. [PEER-PH]
- Uncomplicated middle ear barotrauma is discharged with analgesics and decongestants, adding antibiotics if the tympanic membrane ruptured. [ROSH]
- Asymptomatic brown recluse bites are discharged with return precautions for delayed necrosis; a hypothermic patient with stroke-like deficits needs active rewarming, not discharge. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
