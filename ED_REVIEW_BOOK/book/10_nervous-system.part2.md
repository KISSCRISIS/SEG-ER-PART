## Pathophysiology

### Cerebrovascular Mechanisms

- Ischemic stroke results from thrombosis, embolism, or hypoperfusion; thrombosis is the most common mechanism. Risk factors are smoking, increasing age, male sex, diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia [ROSH].
- The MCA is the artery most commonly affected; ACA infarction is comparatively rare because collateral flow is supplied through the anterior communicating artery [ROSH].
- Hypertensive vasculopathy is the leading cause of spontaneous ICH. Long-standing hypertension degenerates and eventually ruptures the small penetrating arteries that branch from the major intracerebral arteries, which is why hypertensive hemorrhage occurs in deep locations such as the putamen [ROSH].
- Atraumatic SAH is aneurysmal in 80% of cases; saccular aneurysms form at the junctions of the major cerebral arteries, particularly within the circle of Willis. Arteriovenous malformations, mycotic aneurysms, and CNS vasculitis are less common causes [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis obstructs venous outflow, raising intracranial pressure and causing venous infarction and sometimes hemorrhage. Hypercoagulable states — polycythemia vera, inherited thrombophilia such as factor V Leiden, the postpartum period, and oral contraceptive use — are the principal risk factors [ROSH][PEER-PH][PEER-CS].
- In SLE, antiphospholipid antibodies are present in 30–40% of patients and cause ischemic stroke through in situ vascular thrombosis; they also predispose to nonbacterial endocarditis, which can embolize [PEER-CS].
- In PRES, severe hypertension or immunosuppression compromises the blood–brain barrier; capillary leakage produces vasogenic edema of the posterior cerebral white matter, which is why the occipital lobe is most susceptible to hydrostatic injury [PEER-CS][ROSH].

### Intracranial Pressure and CSF Dynamics

- Normal pressure hydrocephalus results from CSF accumulation in the ventricles, producing raised intracranial pressure with edema of the periventricular white matter and corona radiata. The sacral motor fibers carried there govern gait, so compression produces gait instability, with incontinence following. The defect is decreased CSF absorption [ROSH].
- Obstructive hydrocephalus arises from mechanical obstruction, and CSF accumulates proximal to it. The interventricular foramina, cerebral aqueduct, caudal fourth ventricle, and fourth ventricular foramina are the common sites. Aqueductal obstruction dilates the lateral and third ventricles while the fourth ventricle stays normal [ROSH].
- Raised intracranial pressure from shunt failure exerts pressure on the midbrain and quadrigeminal plate, giving paralysis of upgaze and the “setting sun” appearance [PEER-PH].
- Shunt failure is most often mechanical. Ventricular catheter obstruction is the most common cause, from contact of the catheter tip with brain or choroid plexus, or from debris, blood, or proteinaceous fluid within the catheter [PEER-PH].
- Distal catheter obstruction occurs when the distal tip contacts omentum, adhesions, or other intraperitoneal structures; because CSF cannot drain into the peritoneum, a shunt tap shows an opening pressure greater than 20 cm H2O [PEER-PH].
- Shunt infection most often occurs during the first month after placement, reflecting skin contamination at surgery. Coagulase-negative staphylococci account for 50% and Staphylococcus aureus for 33% of shunt infections [ROSH].
- Overshunting drains too much CSF and lowers intracranial pressure. The ventricles collapse into a slit configuration, the catheter presses against the ependyma and choroid plexus, and those tissues block the catheter holes, creating a cycle of functional shunt failure. Rapid CSF pressure reduction can also tear bridging veins and produce subdural hematoma [PEER-PH][ROSH].
- Severe hyponatremia, with a serum sodium below 120 mEq/L, raises intracranial pressure and can present with nausea, malaise, altered mentation, and seizures [PEER-CS].

### Seizure Generation

- A seizure is an episode of abnormal neurologic function caused by an inappropriate electrical discharge of brain neurons. In known epilepsy, status epilepticus is most often caused by medication discontinuation [ROSH].
- Isoniazid is chemically related to pyridoxine (vitamin B6). A large overdose causes a functional pyridoxine and gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) deficiency; because GABA is inhibitory, its loss produces seizures. Seizures typically begin 30–120 minutes after a severe ingestion, and ingestion of more than 2 g rapidly depletes pyridoxine and GABA [ROSH][PEER-CS].
- Phenytoin prolongs the refractory period of voltage-gated sodium channels in the cerebrum, making it a sodium channel antagonist [ROSH].
- Beta-lactam antibiotics are associated with nonconvulsive status epilepticus, and phenytoin is likely to be ineffective for seizures caused by tricyclic antidepressant overdose [PEER-PH].
- Alcohol withdrawal seizures occur within 6 to 48 hours of the last intake and are usually a single generalized tonic-clonic event or a cluster of short seizures. Status epilepticus is not consistent with alcohol withdrawal alone and should prompt a search for hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, intracranial hemorrhage, meningitis, severe metabolic derangement, or drug overdose [PEER-CS].
- Hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy is the most common cause of neonatal seizures [PEER-PH].
- Febrile seizures occur in children aged 6 months to 5 years, and human herpesvirus 6 (HHV-6) infection is the most common precipitating factor [PEER-PH].
- Infantile spasms are defined by clustered myoclonic seizures on awakening, a hypsarrhythmia pattern on electroencephalography (EEG), and developmental delay, and begin at 2–8 months of age [ROSH].
- Neurocysticercosis results from ingesting eggs of the tapeworm Taenia solium, usually through fecally contaminated food. The larva forms an expanding brain cyst that provokes an intense immunologic reaction; symptoms begin when neural tissue can no longer accommodate the enlarging cyst [ROSH].

### CNS Infection and Inflammation

- Encapsulated bacteria such as Neisseria meningitidis enter through the upper airway and spread hematogenously to the subarachnoid space; capsule elements trigger an inflammatory cascade that makes the brain and meninges edematous and reduces CSF drainage [ROSH].
- Streptococcus pneumoniae causes approximately 50% of bacterial meningitis in adults; neonates are most often infected by group B Streptococcus, Escherichia coli, and Listeria monocytogenes [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Fungal meningitis is increasing with the growing immunosuppressed population. Cryptococcus neoformans is the most common agent, with Candida and Aspergillus also implicated; HIV/AIDS, hematologic malignancy, and iatrogenic immunosuppression are the main risk factors [ROSH].
- Viral encephalitis is inflammation and infection of the brain parenchyma. It resembles meningitis clinically but is distinguished by neurologic dysfunction — focal weakness, seizures, and personality change [PEER-PH].
- Neurosyphilis is a small and medium vessel vasculitis that can also cause ischemic stroke. Untreated disease may progress to general paresis, a frontotemporal dementia with grandiose delusions and personality change that develops 10–25 years after infection and does not improve with treatment. Tabes dorsalis affects the posterior columns, causing sensory ataxia with sharp limb and back pain [PEER-CS].
- Lyme disease is transmitted by the Ixodes scapularis tick; in early disseminated infection, erythema migrans may be followed by facial nerve palsy, and bilateral facial nerve palsy is most often caused by Borrelia burgdorferi [ROSH].
- Transverse myelitis is an inflammatory disorder of the spinal cord that produces a functional transection with sensory deficits. Roughly 30% of cases follow an acute viral infection — postinfectious myelitis — and the thoracic cord is affected in 60–70% [ROSH].
- Multiple sclerosis is an autoimmune demyelinating disease of brain and spinal cord with axonal damage; the signature is neurologic deficits separated in time and in anatomic space [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Guillain-Barré syndrome follows a preceding infection through molecular mimicry, destroying the myelin sheath or the axon itself. Roughly 75% of cases are triggered by infection; Campylobacter jejuni accounts for about one-third of those, with cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus, and HIV as other triggers [PEER-PH][PEER-CS].
- Myasthenia gravis is caused by autoantibodies against postsynaptic nicotinic acetylcholine receptors of the motor endplate, producing receptor degradation, dysfunction, or blockade. Thymus dysfunction or previous infection is believed to underlie antibody formation [PEER-PH].
- Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome produces weakness that improves with continued stimulation, and is associated with an underlying perihilar mass [ROSH].
- Tick paralysis is caused by a tick neurotoxin that inhibits acetylcholine release, producing ascending flaccid paralysis with ataxia that can progress to involve the respiratory muscles [ROSH].
- Acute flaccid myelitis is an acquired disorder of motor neurons of the brainstem and spinal cord caused by viral infection, most often enterovirus D68 and A71; outbreaks peak in even-numbered years and most commonly affect children aged 7–11 years [ROSH].

### Neurodegeneration and Movement Disorders

- Huntington disease is autosomal dominant. Misfolding of the huntingtin protein forms intraneuronal inclusion bodies, producing progressive neurodegeneration with chorea, psychiatric symptoms, and cognitive decline [ROSH].
- Parkinson disease is characterized by rigidity, resting tremor, and difficulty stopping while walking; the cellular pathology is Lewy bodies. Haloperidol is a common pharmacologic cause of parkinsonism [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Dementia with Lewy bodies combines progressive cognitive decline with visual hallucinations [ROSH].
- Creutzfeldt-Jakob disease is a prion-mediated disorder; sporadic disease arises from spontaneous prion misfolding and causes rapidly progressive dementia, myoclonus, gait instability, and increased tone with hyperreflexia. MRI shows diffusion restriction in the basal ganglia and EEG shows periodic sharp wave complexes. Most patients die within 6–24 months of onset [ROSH].
- Tardive dyskinesia is caused by dysregulated dopaminergic signaling within the basal ganglia after chronic use of dopamine receptor-blocking drugs; duration of antipsychotic use is an established risk factor [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Acute dystonic reactions reflect dopamine receptor blockade with a consequent rise in cholinergic activity. First-generation antipsychotics, especially haloperidol, are the most common culprits, followed by atypical antipsychotics and antidopaminergic antiemetics such as metoclopramide and promethazine [ROSH][PEER-PH].
- Wernicke encephalopathy is caused by thiamine deficiency. Only about 10% of patients present with the full triad of mental status change, ocular motor abnormality, and gait ataxia; nystagmus is the most common ocular finding. Roughly 80% of these patients go on to develop Korsakoff syndrome, persistent memory impairment with confabulation, and the memory deficit rarely improves [PEER-CS].
- Nitrous oxide abuse causes neurotoxicity through a functional vitamin B12 deficiency, producing lower extremity weakness, numbness, gait imbalance, and macrocytic anemia [PEER-PH].

### Toxic and Metabolic Encephalopathy

- Hepatic encephalopathy is a toxic-metabolic encephalopathy; asterixis — intermittent loss of motor tone seen as flapping of dorsiflexed hands — supports the diagnosis in a patient with stigmata of liver disease. Intercurrent illness can precipitate it, and up to 12% of patients with spontaneous bacterial peritonitis have no abdominal tenderness or fever [PEER-CS].
- Lithium has a narrow therapeutic range of 0.8–1.2 mEq/L and is renally excreted. Any state that reduces renal perfusion — diarrheal illness, dehydration, or heat exposure — reduces excretion and causes toxicity, presenting with confusion, lethargy, obtundation, hyperreflexia, tremor, myoclonus, and seizures [PEER-CS].
- Opioid toxicity produces pinpoint pupils and respiratory depression; the therapeutic goal of naloxone is to maintain adequate respirations and oxygenation, not full arousal [PEER-CS].
- Sympathomimetic toxicity produces hyperthermia, tachycardia, hypertension, mydriasis, and diaphoresis with agitation and delirium. A core temperature above 41.1°C is a toxicologic emergency, with risk of rhabdomyolysis, metabolic acidosis, ventricular dysrhythmias, and seizures. Cocaine also blocks myocardial fast sodium channels [PEER-CS].
- Carbon monoxide poisoning produces headache, dizziness, chest pain, nausea, altered mental status, seizures, and coma, and should be suspected when several household members are affected in winter months [PEER-PH].
- Myxedema coma is severe hypothyroidism causing a decompensated metabolic state with mental status change and 30–50% mortality. Hyponatremia is present in about half of patients, from impaired free-water excretion due to excess vasopressin, and bradycardia, decreased contractility, and hypotension accompany it [PEER-CS].
