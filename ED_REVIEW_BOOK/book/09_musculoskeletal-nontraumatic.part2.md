## Pathophysiology

### Vascular supply and the origins of avascular necrosis
- AVN begins with occlusion of the microcirculation, raising intraosseous pressure and killing cells. The femoral head has a precarious blood supply, so it is at risk whenever that supply is disrupted — femoral neck fracture, hip dislocation, or a systemic process obstructing small vessels. [PEER-PH]
- In sickle cell disease the femoral head is affected in up to 50% of patients over a lifetime and the humeral head in up to 20%. Glucocorticoids cause osteonecrosis independently of duration — even short courses have been implicated. [PEER-PH]
- In the scaphoid the arterial supply enters distally, so a proximal pole fracture isolates the proximal fragment from all entering vessels; the more distal the fracture, the lower the risk of osteonecrosis. [PEER-PH]
- Legg-Calvé-Perthes disease is idiopathic AVN of the paediatric femoral head driven by repeated episodes of ischaemia; late radiographs show fragmentation, sclerosis, and collapse of the capital femoral epiphysis.
- In SCFE the proximal femoral physis is relatively weak and cannot support the child's weight, so the epiphysis displaces posteriorly and inferiorly. Injury to the lateral epiphyseal artery is the mechanism by which SCFE leads to femoral head AVN. [ROSH]

### Routes of bone infection
- Osteomyelitis occurs by haematogenous seeding or by direct extension from a contiguous infected site; contiguous spread accounts for about 80% of adult cases. Normal bone resists infection unless there is trauma, a foreign body, or disrupted blood flow. [ROSH]
- Children acquire osteomyelitis almost exclusively by the haematogenous route. A thin periosteum, low oxygen tension around the growth plate, and transphyseal vessels crossing it all facilitate metaphyseal seeding. In sickle cell disease infection involves the diaphysis rather than the metaphysis, distinguishing it from bone infarction. [PEER-PH]
- Pseudomonas aeruginosa suits plantar puncture wounds through the sole of a shoe because the damp interior of the footwear supports it. The human mouth is polymicrobial — aerobes, Eikenella, and anaerobes such as Fusobacterium — so fight bites readily seed the metacarpophalangeal joints and metacarpals. [PEER-PH]
- Septic arthritis is facilitated by the absence of a protective basement membrane in the synovial membrane, allowing bacteria direct entry into the joint. [PEER-PH]

### Spinal infection and abscess formation
- Haematogenous spread is the commonest route to vertebral osteomyelitis and discitis. The corkscrew geometry of the spinal vessels is thought to predispose them to bacterial seeding, and endocarditis is a common source — one-third of patients with spinal infection also have endocarditis. [PEER-PH]
- Disc infection commonly coexists with vertebral osteomyelitis and can extend into the epidural space or paravertebral tissues as an abscess. Mass effect compresses the spinal cord, nerve roots, and lumbar plexus. [PEER-CS]
- Spinal epidural abscess usually sits in the thoracic or lumbar region, where the epidural space holds a larger volume of infection-prone fatty tissue. Neurological deficits appear in only half of patients and are typically late, so fever may be the only early clue. A psoas abscess is similarly nonspecific: about 91% of patients have pain localised to the back, flank, or lower abdomen and about 75% are febrile. [ROSH][PEER-CS]

### Disc degeneration and nerve root compression
- Lumbar radiculopathy — sciatica — results from compression, inflammation, or injury to the lumbar nerve roots, producing dermatomal pain with possible paraesthesias, weakness, or loss of deep tendon reflexes. [PEER-CS]
- The commonest cause is disc degeneration compressing nerve roots as they exit; disc herniation is the leading cause of sciatica, particularly in younger and nongeriatric adults. Herniation is most common at L5-S1 and the L5 root is most commonly affected. Paracentral herniations affect the root at the level of the disc, lateral herniations the root below the disc. [PEER-PH]

### Compression of the cauda equina and conus medullaris
- The spinal cord ends at the T12-L1 vertebral level; below that the nerve roots continue as the cauda equina. CES therefore results from compression of lumbar, sacral, and coccygeal roots distal to the termination of the cord, classically below L1. [ROSH]
- Disc herniation is the most common cause of CES, implicated in up to 45% to 57% of cases, most often at L4/L5. Other causes include degenerative disc change, spinal fracture, tumour, discitis, spinal stenosis, epidural haematoma, and epidural abscess. [PEER-PH]
- Bladder dysfunction in CES begins as a sensory change with incomplete emptying and progresses to urinary retention and overflow incontinence — which is why urinary retention is both the most common symptom and the most sensitive finding. [PEER-PH]
- Conus medullaris syndrome is compression of the terminal cord, which lies at about L1-L2 in adults; compression from T12 to L2 produces it. It shares the causes of CES and occurs roughly one-third to one-half as often. The distinguishing feature is the absence of upper motor neuron signs in CES, because the lesion lies below the cord rather than within it. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Neurogenic versus vascular claudication
- Spinal stenosis is narrowing of any part of the spinal canal, the intervertebral foramina, or the nerve root canal. It develops through degenerative change — osteoarthritis, degenerative disc disease, facet joint arthritis, and spondylolisthesis — and becomes clinically apparent between ages 50 and 60. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Lumbar stenosis causes midline back pain with nerve root symptoms in the legs. Pain worsens with activity and lumbar extension and eases with rest or flexion, because flexion opens the spinal canal. Pseudoclaudication is distinguished from vascular claudication by peripheral pulses and ankle-brachial indices, which are normal in spinal stenosis. Cervical stenosis arises from the same process, termed cervical spondylosis. [PEER-PH]

### Crystal deposition and crystal-induced synovitis
- Gout is precipitation of uric acid from extracellular fluid. Hyperuricaemia results from underexcretion of purine end products by the kidney or, less commonly, overproduction. A flare occurs when polymorphonuclear cells ingest the crystals and release cytokines, generating inflammation within the synovium. [ROSH]
- Risk factors for urate precipitation include male sex, age over 50, alcohol, diuretics, dehydration, hypertension, obesity, diabetes, a purine-rich diet, ciclosporin, and aspirin. Pseudogout instead arises from calcium pyrophosphate crystals in the synovial space, and the resulting immunological response produces the same pain, swelling, erythema, and warmth. [ROSH][PEER-PH]

### Autoimmune and enthesial inflammation
- Ankylosing spondylitis targets the sites where ligaments attach to bone, causing new bone formation, lumbar stiffness, and the radiographic appearance of bamboo spine. It affects men more than women and begins before age 45. Sacroiliitis is inflammation of the sacroiliac joint and is a feature of the spondyloarthropathies — ankylosing spondylitis, psoriatic arthritis, and the arthritis of inflammatory bowel disease. [PEER-PH][ROSH]
- Reactive arthritis follows urethritis or enteritis by a few weeks, often after the original infection has abated; Chlamydia trachomatis is the classic trigger in the sexually acquired form. [PEER-PH]
- Juvenile idiopathic arthritis is a chronic inflammatory condition of children under 16 years; oligoarticular disease involving four or fewer large joints is the most common pattern, and systemic JIA is defined by a quotidian fever with rash and arthritis. Its most serious complication is macrophage activation syndrome — excessive immune activation with high fever, hepatosplenomegaly, cytopenias, coagulopathy, and markedly elevated ferritin. [ROSH]
- Dermatomyositis is a systemic autoimmune perimysial inflammatory myopathy with cutaneous manifestations; polymyositis is an idiopathic inflammatory myopathy causing gradual proximal weakness. Both spare sensation and reflexes. [ROSH]

### Muscle breakdown: from myocyte to myoglobin
- Rhabdomyolysis begins with myonecrosis releasing intracellular contents into the circulation, producing hyperkalaemia, myoglobinuria, and acute kidney injury. Myoglobin is nephrotoxic and precipitates in the renal tubules, especially in acidic urine. [ROSH]
- Muscle damage also third-spaces fluid into the affected muscle; the resulting intravascular volume contraction further reduces renal perfusion, so volume expansion is the central intervention. [ROSH]
- Causes include alcohol and drug use (most commonly cited), muscular disease, trauma, seizures, neuroleptic malignant syndrome, immobility, infection, strenuous activity, and heat-related illness. Viral myositis is more common than bacterial, fungal, or parasitic; influenza A and B and enteroviruses are the most frequent pathogens. [ROSH][PEER-CS]

### Compartment syndrome: the pressure-volume problem
- Acute compartment syndrome develops when pressure within a fascial compartment rises enough to compromise perfusion. The clinically used index is the delta compartment pressure — diastolic blood pressure minus compartment pressure; a delta below 30 mmHg is concerning and warrants orthopaedic consultation regardless of the absolute pressure. [PEER-CS]

### Bone remodelling, osteoporosis, and metastatic destruction
- Osteoporosis is low bone mass with microarchitectural disruption. Fragility reflects not only low bone mineral density but increased bone cell turnover, disturbed microarchitecture, and abnormal geometry. Vertebral compression fractures are the most common manifestation and are frequently asymptomatic. [PEER-PH]
- Bone metastasis dysregulates normal bone modelling so that bone is dissolved by increased osteoclastic activity, releasing calcium and producing hypercalcaemia. Lesions may be osteolytic (myeloma), osteoblastic (prostate), or mixed (breast), and favour highly vascular bone in the axial skeleton and proximal limbs. [PEER-PH]
- Pathological fracture follows destruction of normal architecture; because healing is poor these fractures usually require surgical stabilisation. [PEER-PH]

### Sarcoma biology in growing bone
- Osteosarcoma peaks in adolescents aged 16 to 18 during rapid skeletal growth, with a second peak over 60, and arises in the metaphysis of long bones — most often the distal femur. Ewing sarcoma arises in the diaphysis of long bones in children aged 5 to 25; fever may be cytokine-induced. [ROSH]

### Growth plate vulnerability and congenital disease
- Developmental dysplasia of the hip is diagnosed clinically before 6 months of age; the palpable clunk of the Ortolani manoeuvre is the femoral head relocating anteriorly into the acetabulum. Osteogenesis imperfecta is a genetic bone disorder of variable severity; some forms carry hearing loss and dentinogenesis imperfecta, and bone fragility produces frequent fractures after minimal trauma. [ROSH][PEER-PH]
- Craniosynostosis occurs when skull sutures close prematurely; the fontanelles normally do not close completely until about age 2, and premature closure produces cranial deformity and can raise intracranial pressure. [PEER-PH]

### Tendon overload and nerve entrapment
- Repetitive athletic movement, especially eccentric contraction, predisposes tendon to injury; continued stress converts this to tendinosis, with infiltration by disorganised tissue and vasculature. [ROSH]
- An apophysis is the attachment point between tendon and bone and the weakest part of the bone-muscle-tendon unit; apophysitis is a traction injury that is self-limited and resolves at skeletal maturity. [PEER-CS]
- Carpal tunnel syndrome results from compression of the median nerve beneath the transverse carpal ligament, from mass lesions, oedema, inflammation, or tendon fibrosis. Ulnar entrapment at the cubital tunnel is the second most common upper extremity neuropathy after carpal tunnel syndrome; the common peroneal nerve, compressed at the fibular head, is the most common lower extremity compressive neuropathy. [ROSH][PEER-CS]
- Guillain-Barré syndrome is an immune-mediated polyneuropathy that usually follows infection, most often Campylobacter jejuni. It is a flaccid paralysis, so contracture is uncommon, and the cerebrospinal fluid shows elevated protein with a normal cell count. [PEER-CS]

### Necrotising soft-tissue infection
- Necrotising fasciitis is a deep soft-tissue infection causing rapid destruction of the muscle fascia and overlying subcutaneous fat. It spreads along fascial planes because of their poor blood supply, while muscle is relatively spared. The hallmark is pain out of proportion to the physical findings; necrotic patches with bullae appear in only about 38% of cases at presentation. [ROSH]
